# Lens L1: isomorphs

> Raw lens output, kept as provenance for the [discipline map](../map.md). Its claims are unverified unless marked otherwise.

**Method note.** Run on 22 October 2026, working alone. About 70 web searches and 7 page fetches (two fetches returned nothing usable), no repository reading. For each candidate field I looked for a canonical text, at least one concrete production or study, and at least one critique. Search results were summaries, so verification is uneven: sources I could see a bibliographic record, abstract or substantive secondary description for are cited with a URL or full reference; sources marked **†** are standard references I know but did not retrieve a record for this session, and should be checked before anyone quotes them. Claims taken from practitioner blogs and vendor pages are labelled as such. Where a number comes from a secondary source (for example the share of Sleep No More audience members who get a one-on-one) it is flagged as an estimate. Nothing here is a design recommendation for the engine; each entry says what the field knows and how to test whether it transfers.

**The problem, restated for mapping.** A system that stages story at every scale in a simulated city, observes the player but never controls them, and must avoid the "radiant quest" feel. The properties wanted: caused, relevant to the player's history, legible, consequential, noticeably varied, true to setting, well paced; plus the structural needs of level-of-detail simulation, human-authored content fitting into the living city, and staging anything from seconds to hours.

## 1. Tabletop RPG game mastering

**Definition.** The craft of a human referee who stages a shared fiction live for a small group, from prepared world state plus improvisation, without taking decisions away from the players.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Caused events.* "Fronts" and countdown clocks give off-screen agents a visible progression (portents leading to a stated doom), so incidents arrive as the world's own moves rather than as a quest dispenser's output. This is the tabletop form of facts at city scale feeding events at street scale.
- *Legibility without railroading.* The Three Clue Rule and node-based scenario design are tested redundancy laws for "the player must be able to infer X" while leaving the route open.
- *Hours-long operations.* Blades in the Dark compresses a heist into a chosen plan type and one detail, rolls an engagement for the starting position from accumulated advantages and disadvantages, and lets players pay a resource to play flashbacks that show earlier preparation as it becomes relevant. Planning is cheap and legible; the live part is reactive.
- *Relevance to history.* Apocalypse World's agenda (make the world seem real, keep the characters' lives from being boring, play to find out what happens) and its instruction to be a fan of the characters (that is, press on what they care about) are explicit procedures for turning what a player has shown into pressure.

**Key sources.**
- Vincent Baker, *Apocalypse World* (Lumpley Games, 2010)†; summary and critique at The Alexandrian, https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/9584/roleplaying-games/apocalypse-world
- Sage LaTorra and Adam Koebel, *Dungeon World* (Sage Kobold Productions, 2012)†, "Fronts"; community compendium https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Fronts ; practitioner discussion https://slyflourish.com/fronts_in_dnd.html
- John Harper, *Blades in the Dark* (One Seven Design, 2017)†; rules summary of planning and engagement https://roll20.net/compendium/BITD/Planning%20and%20Engagement
- Justin Alexander, "Three Clue Rule" and the "Advanced Node-Based Design" series, The Alexandrian, e.g. https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/8202/roleplaying-games/advanced-node-based-design-part-5-the-two-prongs-of-mystery-design

**Instance.** Blades in the Dark engagement roll: roll one die, add a die for each major advantage the crew gained in preparation, subtract one per major disadvantage, take the highest; 1-3 starts desperate, 4-5 risky, 6 controlled. The roll covers only the opening actions of the score, not the whole job.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Events that follow a visible, pre-announced progression of an off-screen agent are attributed by players to the world rather than the game, and are recalled as "consequences" more often than identical events delivered by proximity trigger.
2. For any inference a player must make, three independent evidentiary routes cut the stall rate without raising reported railroading; two routes do not.
3. Folding earlier preparation into one legible state variable that sets the opening position of a live scene makes preparation feel causal even when the variable is hidden.

**Failure modes.** Craft lore with almost no controlled evidence. Fronts decay into stock villain templates (the radiant-quest problem in analog form). A human GM reads faces and tone live, a signal a system lacks. The community's illusionism critique (prepared content that ignores choices, the "quantum ogre") is a standing warning against hidden steering.

**Relevance.** Core; confidence 0.85. **Surprise:** no, though the specific procedures (portents, clue redundancy, engagement roll) are less well known than the genre.

## 2. Improvisational theatre

**Definition.** Unscripted theatre in which performers build scenes live from one another's contributions ("offers"), governed by a small set of reflexes: accept, build, raise or lower status.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Offers and blocks.* The system will make offers and the player will respond, sometimes by ignoring. Improv's central discipline is to treat whatever the partner does as established fact. An actor in Third Rail's immersive work says ignoring an audience member's choice makes the world feel fake (see field 3), which is the same claim from the performer's side.
- *Seconds-long encounters.* Johnstone's status model (every utterance is a small move up or down in a pecking order) gives a short NPC exchange legible drama without plot.
- *Relevance to history by reincorporation.* The Harold and related forms return to earlier material in later scenes. Late-binding a detail the player originated into a new scene is the cheapest known route to "this is about me".

**Key sources.**
- Keith Johnstone, *Impro: Improvisation and the Theatre* (Faber, 1979) and *Impro for Storytellers* (Faber, 1999)†; overview of offer, accept, block at https://improwiki.com/en/wiki/accept
- Charna Halpern, Del Close and Kim "Howard" Johnson, *Truth in Comedy: The Manual of Improvisation* (Meriwether, 1994); record at https://catalog.wake.gov/Record/439337
- Viola Spolin, *Improvisation for the Theater* (Northwestern UP, 1963)†

**Instance.** The Harold: a multi-scene form built around patterns and games where earlier themes return and recombine; the publisher's description stresses pattern recognition and the subversion of expectation.

**Principles (testable).**
1. A reincorporated detail the player originated produces a stronger sense of "caused" than a newly generated detail of equal craft.
2. Status movement across an exchange (someone rises, someone falls) makes a short encounter readable: observers asked "who won?" agree more often than for status-flat exchanges.
3. Unacknowledged player moves are the largest single source of "stage set" reports; acknowledging a move without granting its goal repairs most of the damage.

**Failure modes.** Literal "yes, and" cannot hold in a simulation that must be able to refuse; improv's own refusal is craft ("yes, but"). The form assumes two bodies reading each other in real time. Johnstone's status theory is a Western, mid-century observation and has not been tested as a predictive model. Improv comedy culture biases toward gags.

**Relevance.** Core; confidence 0.75. **Surprise:** no.

## 3. Immersive theatre

**Definition.** Performance in which a roaming audience moves through a built environment where performers run scenes concurrently, with some scenes delivered to one audience member at a time.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Scarce authored attention.* Punchdrunk-style shows have far more audience than performer capacity for private scenes. One commentator estimates only 10 to 15 per cent of attendees get a one-on-one on a given night. The audience signals availability by behaviour (drifting from the crowd, lingering), not by a menu. This is the allocation problem for high-detail content under level-of-detail simulation.
- *Design for the many who miss it.* The unselected majority still must see something complete.
- *Refusal handling.* Third Rail actors describe a protocol for audience members who refuse, test boundaries or do something unexpected: honour the choice, stay safe, keep committing, and do not ignore it.

**Key sources.**
- Josephine Machon, *Immersive Theatres: Intimacy and Immediacy in Contemporary Performance* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2013); record https://pure.gsmd.ac.uk/en/publications/immersive-theatres-intimacy-and-immediacy-in-contemporary-perform/
- Thomas Cartelli, "Punchdrunk's Sleep No More: Masks, Unmaskings, One-on-Ones", *Borrowers and Lenders* (2013), https://borrowers-ojs-azsu.tdl.org/borrowers/article/view/107
- Voices of VR #730, interview with Third Rail Projects actor Alberto Denis on acting for responsive interactive narrative, https://voicesofvr.com/730-immersive-theater-acting-tips-for-responsive-interactive-narratives/
- Adam Alston, *Beyond Immersive Theatre: Aesthetics, Politics and Productive Participation* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2016), the main critique; https://research.gold.ac.uk/28079

**Instance.** *Then She Fell* (Third Rail Projects): 15 audience members per performance, rooms explored alone or in small groups, one-on-one encounters with performers, 7 years in Brooklyn (production page https://www.thirdrailprojects.com/thenshefell ; run length from a press summary).

**Principles (testable).**
1. Signals of voluntary approach (lingering, following, returning) are better triggers for high-intensity content than proximity alone, judged by acceptance and later recall.
2. A performer who acknowledges a refusal in character preserves belief better than one who proceeds as scripted, measured by willingness to re-engage.
3. Content consumed by the non-selected majority needs to be complete in itself; "you missed the real scene" reports predict churn.

**Failure modes.** Audience scale is 15 to 500, not one. Critics (Alston) argue "empowerment" is labour the audience performs for the producer, and there is documented fear-of-missing-out. Much of what is written is promotional.

**Relevance.** Core; confidence 0.65. **Surprise:** no.

## 4. Larp (live action role-play) and its plot teams

**Definition.** Participants embody characters in a physical setting over hours to days, with organizers ("plot teams", referees, game masters) steering the fiction from behind the scenes.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Hooks from histories.* Campaign larps' plot teams routinely read player-submitted histories, goal questions and post-event letters to find threads to build on. This is hand-run "relevant to the player's own history".
- *Steering.* Nordic larp theory distinguishes steering (deliberately directing your play toward a dramatic goal for reasons outside the character's own motives) from ordinary in-character play. A system observing a player can detect when they are steering for story, and offer to match it.
- *Seeding.* Organizers seed information through sheets and rumours, and send NPCs to provoke action, then let narrative emerge retroactively from what players do.

**Key sources.**
- Jaakko Stenros and Markus Montola (eds.), *Nordic Larp* (2010), https://trepo.tuni.fi/bitstream/handle/10024/95123/nordic_larp_2010.pdf
- Markus Montola, Jaakko Stenros and Eleanor Saitta, "The Art of Steering: Bringing the Player and Character Back Together", *The Knudepunkt 2015 Companion Book* (record via https://www.juhanapettersson.com/?p=2146)
- Interactive Literature GameWrap essays on larp design, https://gamewrap.interactiveliterature.org/
- LARP Portal guidance on how plot teams use character histories, https://larportal.com/how-to-update-your-character-to-help-plot.php (practitioner source)

**Instance.** A campaign larp plot team reads submitted character histories and post-event letters between events, picks a handful of threads and writes personal plots for the next event.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Offers that cite a specific, self-originated detail from the player's history are accepted more often than offers matched on a category only.
2. Detecting "steering" (the player choosing the dramatically interesting option over the in-character one) and answering with matching drama raises later engagement.
3. Letting narrative emerge from what players do, with set-pieces for them to react to, yields fewer abandoned threads than a pre-planned storyline.

**Failure modes.** Human-driven, small-scale, labour-heavy. Strong subcultural norms (workshops, safety calibration, bleed) do not transfer to a solo player. The design literature is largely practitioner essays.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.5. **Surprise:** yes.

## 5. Alternate reality games and pervasive games

**Definition.** Games staged in the everyday world and media, run live by authors ("puppetmasters") who reveal content in response to what players have found, without announcing that it is a game.

**Why it matters here.**
- *No UI seam.* ARGs deliver story through phone calls, sites, fake adverts and found objects, never as a quest marker. The player's sense that the content "exists in the world" is the design.
- *Live adaptation by release pacing.* Authors control difficulty and pace by when content appears, not by changing puzzles.
- *Scale mismatch is the lesson.* The Beast was planned for a couple of hundred thousand people and reached about three million, forcing live adaptation, the opposite of a one-player system but the same author-reacts-to-observation loop.
- Blast Theory's street-and-online games show how city space, a few live performers and remote players can be composed into one experience.

**Key sources.**
- Jane McGonigal, "This Is Not a Game: Immersive Aesthetics and Collective Play" (MelbourneDAC proceedings, 2003) and "Why I Love Bees: A Case Study in Collective Intelligence Gaming" (in *The Ecology of Games*, MIT Press, 2008); listing at https://electricarchaeology.ca/2008/09/04/so-youre-interested-in-alternate-reality-games-some-readings/
- History of alternate reality games, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_alternate_reality_games
- Jim Munroe, interview with Sean Stewart and Elan Lee, https://culturalgutter.com/2005/07/23/jim_munroe_interviews_sean_stewart_and_elan_lee/
- Markus Montola, Jaakko Stenros and Annika Waern, *Pervasive Games: Theory and Design* (Morgan Kaufmann, 2009)†
- Blast Theory, *Can You See Me Now?* (2001) and *Uncle Roy All Around You* (2003); background https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blast_Theory

**Instance.** *The Beast* (2001): a murder-mystery spread across hundreds of sites, emails, faxes and voicemails to promote a film, run live by a small team of puppetmasters; the sponsor's creative lead said they hoped for a couple of hundred thousand participants and got three million.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Content delivered through diegetic channels with no interface marker is rated more "caused" than the same content with a quest marker.
2. Tuning pace by release timing, not content difficulty, keeps solve rates in a target band at lower authoring cost.
3. A visible trace of the author reacting to what the player did (a message that mentions their discovery) raises perceived responsiveness more than a better scripted message.

**Failure modes.** Needs live puppetmaster labour; depends on a collective hive mind; spoiler and discovery asymmetries; mostly one-off campaigns. The scholarly record is thin and promotional.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.6. **Surprise:** no.

## 6. Escape-room game mastering

**Definition.** A game master watches a team in a timed puzzle environment (often by camera) and gives graduated hints to keep them progressing and enjoying it.

**Why it matters here.**
- It is the nearest commercial analog to "the system can observe and make offers". Operators have refined when to nudge, how much, and in what form.
- Recommended practice (practitioner sources): hints on a ladder where the first points attention at an object rather than supplying the answer; triggers written as observable conditions plus a time limit, not "seems stuck"; a deliberate extra wait because teams often solve the stall just before the hint arrives.

**Key sources.**
- Scott Nicholson, "Peeking Behind the Locked Door: A Survey of Escape Room Facilities" (white paper, 2015; http://scottnicholson.com/pubs/erfacwhite.pdf) and "The State of Escape: Escape Room Design and Facilities" (Meaningful Play 2016), https://meaningfulplay.msu.edu/proceedings2016/abstract.php?paperid=14
- Practitioner article on hint systems, https://roomescapeartist.com/2019/10/24/escape-room-hint-systems/
- "Escape-Master", a TU Delft thesis automating the game-master role (progress tracking, hint interaction by elapsed time), https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_d4e77450-0414-49e5-825c-1f2d0d7ce15c

**Instance.** A graduated ladder: a nudge toward the right area, then toward the right object, then the mechanism, with the operator holding back an extra minute because teams so often resolve the stall in that window.

**Principles (testable).**
1. A hint that redirects attention is rated more satisfying, and the solve more self-attributed, than one that supplies the answer.
2. Triggers defined on observable state plus a timer misfire less often than inferred "stuck" judgments.
3. Adding a short grace delay before an offered nudge reduces "you stole my solve" reports without raising abandonment.

**Failure modes.** Evidence is practitioner opinion; a hint is a rescue in a closed, linear, time-boxed space, while in an open world apparent stalling is often a choice. Over-helping is easy.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.6. **Surprise:** yes.

## 7. Stage management and show control

**Definition.** The discipline that runs a live performance from a single master document, coordinating lighting, sound, scenery and performers by timed cues.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Graded pre-commitment.* Cues run as warning (about a minute ahead), standby (seconds ahead), then go. The same grammar fits promoting simulation detail ahead of a staged beat: warn to start resources spinning up, standby to hold them ready, go to commit.
- *Partitioned views.* Each department receives only its own cue portion; the stage manager alone holds the whole book. That matches level-of-detail systems where each subsystem sees only its slice.
- *Single source of truth plus variance log.* The prompt book holds the script, cues, show reports and emergency procedures; reports record what actually happened and what went wrong.

**Key sources.**
- Lawrence Stern and Jill Gold, *Stern's Stage Management* (Routledge, multiple editions)†; Thomas A. Kelly, *The Back Stage Guide to Stage Management* (Back Stage Books, 3rd ed. 2009)†
- Prompt book explainer, https://theatrecrafts.com/pages/home/topics/stage-management/the-prompt-book/
- Cues and standby/go grammar, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cue_(theatrical)

**Instance.** A calling script marked with lighting and sound cues; the caller says the department and cue number after "warning" and "standby", and "go" only at execution, so crews know the word "go" means act now.

**Principles (testable).**
1. A three-step warn/standby/go commitment for staged beats produces fewer missed or half-ready beats than a single trigger.
2. Giving each subsystem only its cue slice, with one owner of the full sequence, reduces cross-subsystem desync.
3. A post-run variance log (planned versus actual) finds more pacing defects than aggregate telemetry.

**Failure modes.** Theatre is deterministic in time: the show is known in advance. The caller is a human who decides when to say "go" by watching the stage. Heavy documentation overhead.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.5. **Surprise:** yes.

## 8. Stage magic and the science of deception

**Definition.** Close-up and stage conjuring: engineering what an audience attends to, infers and believes about how an outcome came about.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Agency without control.* The equivoque ("magician's choice") force lets a person feel they freely chose while every option leads to the planned outcome. Experiments show the feeling of control survives two repetitions and open procedure. This is both a tool and a hazard: it is the "illusionism" that players resent when found.
- *Pattern exploitation.* Teller names exploiting pattern recognition as a principle: the audience infers a rule from a few instances, and the magician breaks it. Players infer a generator's rule within a few instances; the template feel is this process. The remedy is to control what pattern they can infer.
- *Cause reconstruction.* Magic is built so the audience's after-the-fact reconstruction of the cause fails. For story staging the aim is the inverse: the player's reconstruction should succeed and feel earned.

**Key sources.**
- Gustav Kuhn, Alym A. Amlani and Ronald A. Rensink, "Towards a science of magic", *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 12(9), 2008, 349-354, https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~rensink/publications/abs.08.1.html
- Stephen L. Macknik et al., "Attention and awareness in stage magic: turning tricks into research", *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 9, 2008, 871-879†
- Alice Pailhès, Shringi Kumari and Gustav Kuhn, "The Magician's Choice: Providing illusory choice and sense of agency with the Equivoque forcing technique", *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 150(7), 2021, 1358-1372, https://research.gold.ac.uk/29234
- Teller, "Teller Reveals His Secrets", *Smithsonian Magazine*, March 2012, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/teller-reveals-his-secrets-100744801/
- Darwin Ortiz, *Strong Magic* (Ortiz Publications, 1994)†

**Instance.** Equivoque with a key and a wallet: whichever the spectator touches, the performer keeps or discards so that the key ends up chosen; participants in the lab study did not notice the inconsistencies and were surprised by the matching prediction.

**Principles (testable).**
1. When every option on offer leads to the same low-stakes payload but options differ in framing, players report control of the outcome and do not report railroading; the effect fades when the payload is high-stakes or repeated across sessions.
2. After three exposures to the same generator structure, players predict the next beat above chance; breaking the pattern deliberately once raises recalled "surprise" more than varying surface details.
3. A reveal of the real causal chain after a surprising event (a news item, an NPC's explanation) converts surprise into "it was caused".

**Failure modes.** The goal of magic is deception about method; ours should be truthful causation. Illusionism discovered mid-campaign sours trust (a hazard from tabletop practice). Lab findings use small tasks and student samples.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.6. **Surprise:** yes.

## 9. Confidence games, cooling out and cold reading

**Definition.** The staged manipulation of a single person's beliefs and decisions over time by a coordinated team, and the sociology of what happens after the play ends.

**Why it matters here.**
- *A multi-stage experience built around one mark's psychology.* Maurer's anatomy of the big con (locate and investigate the mark, build confidence, a first small win, the big commitment, the blow-off) is the oldest recorded multi-hour interactive narrative shaped around one person, with roles (roper, inside man, shills) and a purpose-built venue (the "big store"). Heist offers and long arcs can borrow the escalation shape.
- *Consequence handled humanely.* Goffman's "cooling the mark out" studies how a person who has lost is helped to accept it; the cooler stays with the mark and reframes. It addresses what the system does after an unwelcome consequence so the player keeps the world's trust.
- *Relevance can be forged cheaply.* Forer's classroom study had students rate an identical, generic personality sketch as highly accurate (mean 4.26 out of 5). Self-reported relevance is a poor target; measure behaviour.
- *Backstopping.* A cover story must survive being checked. Any fact an NPC states should be corroborated by the simulation if investigated.

**Key sources.**
- David W. Maurer, *The Big Con: The Story of the Confidence Man and the Confidence Game* (Bobbs-Merrill, 1940); stage list summarized at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scam
- Erving Goffman, "On Cooling the Mark Out: Some Aspects of Adaptation to Failure", *Psychiatry* 15(4), 1952, 451-463
- Bertram R. Forer, "The fallacy of personal validation: a classroom demonstration of gullibility", *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* 44(1), 1949, 118-123, doi:10.1037/h0059240
- Maria Konnikova, *The Confidence Game* (Viking, 2016)†

**Instance.** The "big store": a fake betting parlour assembled, staffed and run for the benefit of one victim, in Maurer's account of the wire swindle.

**Principles (testable).**
1. A small early win with a real payoff raises acceptance of a larger later offer from the same source.
2. A loss followed by an explicit acknowledging beat (someone frames what happened) lowers abandonment compared with an immediate new offer.
3. Perceived relevance measured by self-report rises with generic statements; behavioural measures (acceptance, return) separate them. Use specifics.

**Failure modes.** The material is deception for harm; Maurer's data are anecdotal and informant-based and from a particular era; Forer-type effects mean surveys will flatter any personalization. The engine should adopt structure and care, not exploit weakness.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.6. **Surprise:** yes.

## 10. Hospitality and service design

**Definition.** The design and management of experiences delivered by staff to individual guests: restaurants, hotels, airlines, high-end counters.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Personalization by noticing, demonstrated through action.* Jiro Ono's sushi counter is reported to place guests by handedness, alter plate layout, and watch how each piece is received. The guest is never told. This models "uses the player's history without announcing it".
- *Persistent guest memory feeding front-line staff.* The Ritz-Carlton's preference database (Mystique) records preferences and past issues, with staff empowered to spend discretionary money per guest incident without approval. That is local agent autonomy within a budget: an NPC at street level allowed to change the offer on the spot.
- *Line of visibility.* Shostack's service blueprint draws a line between what the customer sees and what happens backstage, with fail points marked. It is a ready diagram for what the player can perceive of the simulation.
- *Moments of truth.* Carlzon's point that the airline exists in the short contacts between staff and customers: the staging of seconds-long encounters decides the brand.
- *Peak and end.* Kahneman's work shows retrospective evaluation of an episode is dominated by its peak and its end, with small duration effect, so spend authored budget on peak and ending.

**Key sources.**
- G. Lynn Shostack, "Designing Services That Deliver", *Harvard Business Review* (Jan-Feb 1984)
- Jan Carlzon, *Moments of Truth* (Ballinger, 1987)
- Danny Meyer, *Setting the Table: The Transforming Power of Hospitality in Business* (HarperCollins, 2006)
- B. Joseph Pine II and James H. Gilmore, "Welcome to the Experience Economy", *Harvard Business Review* (July-Aug 1998)†
- Daniel Kahneman, Barbara L. Fredrickson, Charles A. Schreiber and Donald A. Redelmeier, "When more pain is preferred to less: adding a better end", *Psychological Science* 4(6), 1993, 401-405
- IMD case, "The Ritz-Carlton: Managing the mystique", https://imd.org/research-knowledge/operations/case-studies/the-ritz-carlton-managing-the-mystique ; Gallup summary https://news.gallup.com/businessjournal/112906/how-ritzcarlton-manages-mystique.aspx

**Instance.** *Jiro Dreams of Sushi* (David Gelb, 2011): reviewers describe seating by handedness, altered presentation for left-handed guests, and the chef watching each reaction.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Personalization shown by changing what is offered is trusted more, and read as less surveillant, than personalization declared in dialogue.
2. Ending quality dominates remembered quality for encounter-scale content; moving authored detail from the middle to the peak and the close raises recalled satisfaction at constant cost.
3. Bounded discretion (a local agent may adjust an offer within a budget) outperforms scripted exact offers on perceived responsiveness.

**Failure modes.** Hospitality is labour and empathy; personalization can feel invasive; peak-end holds best for short episodes and is contested for long ones; much of the Ritz-Carlton material is marketing, with figures varying by source.

**Relevance.** Core; confidence 0.75. **Surprise:** yes.

## 11. Museum and heritage interpretation

**Definition.** The craft of communicating the meaning of a place or object to visitors who arrive unprepared and free to walk away.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Relevance as a first principle.* Tilden's first principle holds that interpretation which does not relate what is shown to something in the visitor's personality or experience is sterile. He separates information from interpretation (revelation based on information).
- *Who actually looks.* Serrell's tracking of 8,507 visitors in 110 exhibitions found people stop at roughly a third of elements, and a single-exhibition study found most visitors ready to move on after about 20 minutes. Authored content is mostly unseen; each piece must stand alone.
- *One idea per scene.* Thematic interpretation (Ham) requires every element to serve one central idea.

**Key sources.**
- Freeman Tilden, *Interpreting Our Heritage* (University of North Carolina Press, 1957); summary of principles at https://www.i-pal.net/en/post/los-principios-de-freeman-tilden-el-arte-de-provocar-experiencias-significativas-1
- Beverly Serrell, *Paying Attention: Visitors and Museum Exhibitions* (American Association of Museums, 1998)†; 51% Solution project https://informalscience.org/research/51-solution-research-project-meta-analysis-visitor-timeuse-museum-exhibitions/ ; tracking-and-timing example https://informalscience.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Seeing_tracking_and_timing.pdf
- Sam H. Ham, *Environmental Interpretation* (North American Press, 1992)†
- John H. Falk and Lynn D. Dierking, *The Museum Experience* (Whalesback, 1992)†

**Instance.** Serrell's timing-and-tracking studies across 110 exhibitions, showing a typical visitor stops at about a third of the exhibit elements.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Content explicitly linked to something the player did or owns holds attention longer than equivalent unlinked content.
2. Each scene should be complete for a player who meets only a third of the surrounding material; scenes that need neighbours to make sense show more drop-off.
3. A scene built on a single sentence-length idea is recalled better than one with two competing ideas.

**Failure modes.** Tilden's principles are craft lore, not experimentally derived; the "provoke" principle in particular is weakly evidenced; museum visitors are self-selected; the approach assumes you can know the visitor.

**Relevance.** Core; confidence 0.65. **Surprise:** yes.

## 12. Readers' advisory (librarianship)

**Definition.** Recommending books to a particular reader from what they have enjoyed, using a vocabulary of "appeal" rather than genre.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Match on appeal, not category.* Saricks's appeal factors (pacing, characterization, story line, frame/tone, style) and Nancy Pearl's four "doorways" (story, character, setting, language) let an advisor recommend across genres. For the engine: model the style of content the player seems to value (slow-burn, interpersonal, noir tone, fast action) rather than "quest type".
- *Ask about the last thing loved.* The interview starts from a recent, specific enjoyed item and asks what about it, not from stated preferences.
- *Non-judgemental stance* toward taste, so the model of the reader is not distorted by the reader's self-image.

**Key sources.**
- Joyce G. Saricks, *Readers' Advisory Service in the Public Library* (3rd ed., ALA, 2005); appeal terms article https://journals.ala.org/index.php/rusq/article/view/2892/2959 ; anniversary column https://journals.ala.org/index.php/rusq/article/view/2766
- Nancy Pearl, "doorways" model, as described in the RUSQ column above
- Catherine Sheldrick Ross, "Finding without seeking: the information encounter in the context of reading for pleasure", *Information Processing and Management* 35(6), 1999, 783-799†
- Critique from a student paper, https://ojs.library.ubc.ca/index.php/seealso/article/view/186334/185508

**Instance.** A reader's advisory interview: "tell me about a book you liked recently and what you liked about it", then recommending on pacing and tone.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Predicting a player's acceptance of an offer from appeal factors (pace, tone, interpersonal versus action) beats predicting from content category on held-out sessions.
2. Inferring taste from the most recent specific enjoyed item beats inferring from the long-run average.
3. Naming the appeal in the offer itself (not the topic) raises acceptance.

**Failure modes.** The appeal vocabulary is subjective and largely practice-based; critics note the literature models materials more than readers' experiences; advisor skill varies.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.5. **Surprise:** yes.

## 13. Music sequencing: radio programming, DJing and shuffle design

**Definition.** Choosing the order of recorded items for an audience in real time or on a schedule, balancing familiarity, variety and energy.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Perceived randomness is not randomness.* Spotify's engineers describe a gap between probability and perception: a mathematically random shuffle felt repetitive. Their "Fewer Repeats" mode generates several random orderings and scores each for how recently its tracks were played, keeping the best.
- *Rotation rules.* Radio scheduling software enforces category rotation and separation (artist and title separation of roughly an hour or two are common conventions) on a "hot clock" that assigns categories to time slots. Rules relax when no candidate fits.
- *Sequencing by transition compatibility.* DJs improvise with fixed material, choosing the next item for fit with the last (tempo, key, energy) and for the floor's response. Automated research (HP Labs' hpDJ) used crowd response to pick what to play next.

**Key sources.**
- Ludvig Borgne and Chidem Sahiner, "Shuffle: Making Random Feel More Human", Spotify Engineering, 13 November 2025, https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/shuffle-making-random-feel-more-human
- Rotation and separation: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MusicMaster_(software) ; https://www1.udel.edu/nero/Radio/readings/elements.html ; https://radioinsight.com/blogs/243230/about-artist-separation-again/ ; vendor guidance https://streaming.nobexpartners.com/blog/rule-based-rotation
- Mark J. Butler, *Playing with Something That Runs: Technology, Improvisation, and Composition in DJ and Laptop Performance* (Oxford University Press, 2014)
- Bill Brewster and Frank Broughton, *Last Night a DJ Saved My Life* (Headline, 1999)†
- Dave Cliff, "hpDJ: An automated DJ with floorshow feedback", HP Labs technical report HPL-2005-88, https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/262144/

**Instance.** Spotify's Fewer Repeats: generate candidate orderings, score by recency within and across playlists, play the highest-scoring.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Sampling several candidate content sequences and choosing the one best scored against what the player recently perceived gives higher rated variety than one random draw.
2. Separation rules defined on attributes players notice (NPC archetype, district, emotional beat), not internal identifiers, reduce perceived repetition more.
3. Every rule needs a documented relaxation when no candidate satisfies it, or the scheduler stalls or flattens.

**Failure modes.** Music has no agency or consequence; rules can over-manage into flatness; DJ crowd-reading lore is mostly practitioner talk with thin research; the evidence for Spotify's change is internal.

**Relevance.** Core; confidence 0.7. **Surprise:** yes.

## 14. Sports analytics and live sports broadcasting

**Definition.** Quantifying how much a moment matters in a contest, and live production choices about what viewers see and hear.

**Why it matters here.**
- *A numeric stakes signal for pacing.* Tom Tango's Leverage Index measures how much the win probability could move on the next event, normalized to 1.0 as average. It is prospective, depending on the state before the play. A leverage-style measure (how much could the next beat change the player's situation) could drive when to escalate and when to rest.
- *Choosing what to surface from a chaotic stream.* Esports observers act as in-game camera operators building narrative from fast action. One described choosing the player with the best chance of a game-changing play, not merely the likeliest kill. A second "delay" observer on a short lag catches missed moments for replay.
- *Missed-moment replays* map to recaps of off-screen events delivered later.

**Key sources.**
- Tom Tango, Mitchel Lichtman and Andrew Dolphin, *The Book: Playing the Percentages in Baseball* (Potomac, 2007)†; definition https://www.mlb.com/glossary/advanced-stats/leverage-index
- Esports Production Summit coverage on observers, https://www.sportsvideo.org/2019/12/06/esports-production-summit-how-observers-and-remi-define-the-breakout-era-of-live-esports-production/ ; https://kotaku.com/how-esports-broadcasters-decide-what-you-see-1822529569
- Daniel Dayan and Elihu Katz, *Media Events: The Live Broadcasting of History* (Harvard University Press, 1992)†

**Instance.** The esports "delay observer": a second operator on a lagged feed who bundles moments the live observer missed into replays.

**Principles (testable).**
1. A prospective stakes metric (expected swing in a chosen outcome probability) predicts which beats players rate as dramatic better than a count of events.
2. Placing rest at low-leverage states and escalation at high ones yields better rated pacing than a fixed rhythm.
3. A lagged "replay desk" that surfaces missed events as recaps raises the sense that the world went on without the player.

**Failure modes.** Leverage is defined for sports with a closed state space; open-world stakes are not enumerable. Broadcasters know outcomes; narrative bias distorts what they pick.

**Relevance.** Core; confidence 0.6. **Surprise:** yes.

## 15. Professional wrestling booking

**Definition.** Planned, serialized live performance in which outcomes are decided in advance but details, timing and some turns are adjusted live to crowd response.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Selling.* Performers make damage visible and lasting; a no-sell breaks the story. This is consequence legibility: a world that carries visible after-effects.
- *Heat and long arcs.* Feuds are built across weeks on a stated grievance. John Cena has described a match built on "you stole something from me for three weeks".
- *Predetermined finish with live adjustment.* Performers may change direction in reaction to cheers, boos or chants. Planned outcome plus audience-driven improvisation is the system's staging problem.
- *Kayfabe.* Sharon Mazer defines it as an agreement among wrestlers, promoters, referees and most of the audience to treat the performance as real.

**Key sources.**
- Roland Barthes, "The World of Wrestling", in *Mythologies* (1957)†
- Sharon Mazer, *Professional Wrestling: Sport and Spectacle* (University Press of Mississippi, 1998; 2nd ed. 2020); review https://ussporthistory.com/2022/04/02/review-of-professional-wrestling-sport-and-spectacle/
- Heather Levi, *The World of Lucha Libre* (Duke University Press, 2008)†
- Glossary and practitioner accounts: https://africa.espn.com/wwe/story/_/id/46307018/wwe-dictionary-essential-elements-professional-wrestling ; https://www.sescoops.com/article/john-cena-explains-how-wwe-can-change-a-match-finish-live

**Instance.** A planned finish with a mid-match change in response to the crowd, as described by Cena.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Visible, lasting after-effects of an earlier event on a person or place raise perceived consequence more than numeric penalties or text.
2. A grievance stated early and referenced across sessions produces higher return rates than the same stakes introduced fresh.
3. Letting audience signals (here, player behaviour) change the details of a scene while keeping the planned beat improves rated authenticity compared with fixed delivery.

**Failure modes.** Predetermined outcomes contradict the player-sovereignty rule; borrow selling and heat, not the finish. Studies are cultural criticism, not experiments.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.55. **Surprise:** yes.

## 16. Constraints-led coaching (ecological dynamics)

**Definition.** A school of motor-learning coaching that shapes behaviour by designing task, environment and performer constraints rather than instructing technique.

**Why it matters here.**
- It is the sporting version of "the player is never controlled". The coach manipulates what is available, how much space and time, and what pressure exists; the athlete self-organizes a solution. For the engine: the system's levers are constraints (who is where, what is scarce, what is on offer) and affordances.
- *Representative design.* Practice tasks should preserve the information the athlete will use in the real setting. Offers that preserve the real city's cues transfer; abstract quest boards do not.
- Teaching Games for Understanding builds modified games that pose a tactical problem in simplified form.

**Key sources.**
- Karl M. Newell, "Constraints on the development of coordination", in *Motor Development in Children* (1986)
- Keith Davids, Chris Button and Simon Bennett, *Dynamics of Skill Acquisition: A Constraints-Led Approach* (Human Kinetics, 2008)
- Ross A. Pinder, Keith Davids, Ian Renshaw and Duarte Araujo, "Representative Learning Design and Functionality of Research and Practice in Sport", *Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology* 33(1), 2011, 146-155, https://shura.shu.ac.uk/7369/
- David Bunker and Rod Thorpe, "A model for the teaching of games in secondary schools", *Bulletin of Physical Education* (1982; volume and pages not retrieved); background https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/education/teaching-games-understanding-tgfu
- James J. Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979)†

**Instance.** Small-sided football games: shrink the pitch or reduce numbers so players meet more one-on-one decisions, instead of instructing them.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Changing a scarcity or time constraint alters player strategy more reliably than a message telling them what to do.
2. An offer present as an affordance in the environment is taken more often than the same offer in a list.
3. Training or tutorial content that preserves the real city's cues transfers to live play better than abstract equivalents.

**Failure modes.** "Constraints" is a broad, under-specified umbrella; head-to-head trials against traditional coaching are small; coaches are present, adaptive humans.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.5. **Surprise:** yes.

## 17. Found-story editing: unscripted TV production and film editing

**Definition.** Shaping real, unscripted material into narrative by selecting, ordering and compressing it, with story producers steering in the field and editors in post.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Finding story in a stream of simulated events.* The city simulation will produce raw events; deciding what is a story and who is cast in what role is the story producer's job.
- *Priority rule for conflicts.* Walter Murch's "rule of six" ranks what a cut should satisfy: emotion about half the weight, then story, rhythm, eye-trace, plane, space. When forced to sacrifice, give up the lowest first. A defensible tie-breaker for staging conflicts.
- *The cautionary tale.* Frankenbiting re-orders fragments of speech to make a quote. It compresses time and also makes people say what they did not, which is the line the engine must not cross.

**Key sources.**
- Manfred W. Becker, *Creating Reality in Factual Television: The Frankenbite and Other Fakes* (Routledge, 2020), https://www.routledge.com/Creating-Reality-in-Factual-Television-The-Frankenbite-and-Other-Fakes/Becker/p/book/9780367492717
- Walter Murch, *In the Blink of an Eye* (Silman-James, 1995; 2nd ed. 2001)†
- Susan Murray and Laurie Ouellette (eds.), *Reality TV: Remaking Television Culture* (NYU Press, 2004)†
- Kuleshov effect replication: Barratt, Redei, Innes-Ker and van de Weijer, "Does the Kuleshov Effect Really Exist? ...", *Perception* 45(8), 2016, 847-874, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27056181/

**Instance.** The Kuleshov experiment: the same neutral face cut against soup, a coffin and a woman was read as hunger, grief and desire; the 2016 replication found an effect of some kind, with weaker eye-tracking evidence than expected.

**Principles (testable).**
1. When two staging goals conflict, preserving emotional continuity first, story second and rhythm third yields better rated scenes than the reverse.
2. Juxtaposition of two simulated events in time is read as causal when no cause is given; label events or the player will invent the wrong cause.
3. Compression that stays within what the player could have witnessed is accepted; compression that invents what a person said breaks trust.

**Failure modes.** Editing is offline; Murch's weights are an informal, self-reported heuristic; the Kuleshov effect's strength is contested; reality-TV practice is often ethically poor.

**Relevance.** Peripheral; confidence 0.4. **Surprise:** yes.

## 18. Reflecting a person's own story back: Playback Theatre, narrative therapy, utilization

**Definition.** Methods in which a practitioner listens to a person's own account and returns it in a transformed, meaningful form, immediately (Playback Theatre) or through a structured conversation (narrative therapy), using whatever the person brings (Ericksonian utilization).

**Why it matters here.**
- *Playback Theatre* performers enact a teller's real story on the spot, without a huddle, after a conductor draws it out. It returns the feeling of someone's history, not a summary of events.
- *Narrative therapy* distinguishes the dominant story from "unique outcomes", moments outside the problem story, and builds on them. Applied to a player: a long history of one style hides exceptions (the one time they spared someone). Thickening the exception makes relevance that surprises.
- *Utilization.* Erickson used what the client brought, including resistance. A refused offer or an unexpected tangent is material, not failure.

**Key sources.**
- Jo Salas, *Improvising Real Life: Personal Story in Playback Theatre* (Tusitala, 1993)
- Jonathan Fox, *Acts of Service: Spontaneity, Commitment, Tradition in the Nonscripted Theatre* (Tusitala, 1994); history of the form https://playbackcentre.org/resources/how-it-began/
- Michael White and David Epston, *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends* (Norton, 1990); overview https://www.dulwichcentre.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/Re-authoring-Some-Answers-to-Commonly-Asked-Questions.pdf
- Jeffrey Zeig on utilization, Erickson Foundation catalog, https://catalog.erickson-foundation.org/item/bt14-clinical-demonstration-01-utilization-foundation-solutions

**Instance.** A Playback session: a member of the audience tells a story, the conductor asks about its feeling, and actors improvise a stylized version immediately.

**Principles (testable).**
1. A thematic echo of a player's earlier act (the meaning, not the facts) raises recall of the origin more than a literal recap.
2. Surfacing an exception to a player's dominant pattern and building on it produces higher engagement than reinforcing the dominant pattern.
3. Using a refusal as a story fact (the NPC remembers being turned down) retains engagement better than re-offering the same hook.

**Failure modes.** Aimed at wellbeing, not entertainment; psychological profiling raises privacy and ethical issues; narrative therapy has a modest controlled-trial base; Playback evidence is mostly qualitative.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.55. **Surprise:** yes.

## 19. Montessori and Reggio Emilia practice

**Definition.** Early-education approaches that prepare an environment, observe the child and offer short presentations, rather than direct instruction.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Observe, offer, withdraw.* The Montessori teacher watches, detects readiness ("sensitive periods"), gives a short one-to-one presentation, and steps back. This matches the constraint that the system may only observe and offer.
- *Control of error.* Materials are built so a mistake is visible without an adult saying so. The city's feedback should tell the player they got something wrong.
- *Three-period lesson.* Name it, recognise it, recall it, which checks understanding without a quiz.
- *Documentation* (Reggio): make learning visible and re-listen to it later; a log feeding the next offer.

**Key sources.**
- Maria Montessori, *The Montessori Method* (1912) and *The Absorbent Mind* (1949)†
- Angeline Lillard, *Montessori: The Science Behind the Genius* (Oxford University Press, 2005)†; Angeline Lillard and Nicole Else-Quest, "The early years: Evaluating Montessori education", *Science* 313, 2006, 1893-1894†
- Carolyn Edwards, Lella Gandini and George Forman (eds.), *The Hundred Languages of Children* (Ablex, 1993; 3rd ed. 2012)†
- Descriptions of the prepared environment and three-period lesson, https://montessori-ami.org/node/7453

**Instance.** The three-period lesson: introduce vocabulary tied to a sensory experience, let the child explore and practice, then check independent recall.

**Principles (testable).**
1. An offer made once, briefly, and then withdrawn is taken up more than the same offer repeated; repeated prompts cut later uptake.
2. Waiting for a stretch of unprompted engagement before offering raises acceptance.
3. World feedback that makes a mistake self-evident reduces hint requests without raising frustration.

**Failure modes.** The evidence base is mixed and small; the setting is early childhood; fidelity to the method varies widely; adults differ.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.5. **Surprise:** yes.

## 20. Ritual and rites of passage

**Definition.** Structured, socially witnessed sequences that mark and enact a change in status.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Consequence must be witnessed.* Van Gennep's three phases (separation, margin or liminal, incorporation) and Turner's work on liminality say a status change is real when the community acknowledges it. After a milestone the world should respond with changed address, access and treatment, which is "consequential" in the strong sense.
- *Threshold marking.* Distinct entry, ordeal and return beats make a heist or initiation feel like a passage.

**Key sources.**
- Arnold van Gennep, *Les rites de passage* (1909)
- Victor Turner, *The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure* (Aldine, 1969)
- Roy A. Rappaport, *Ritual and Religion in the Making of Humanity* (Cambridge University Press, 1999)†
- Catherine Bell, *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice* (Oxford University Press, 1992)†; overviews at https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/victor-w-turner

**Instance.** Turner's account of Ndembu initiation: separation from the village, a liminal period of shared hardship and levelled status, and reincorporation with a new status.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Marking a major change with three distinct beats (leave, ordeal, return and recognition) raises later recall and felt significance over a single reward screen.
2. Recognition by other characters adopting the new status raises the sense of change more than a notification.
3. A liminal stretch where ordinary rules are loosened for the player raises bonding with companions.

**Failure modes.** Abstract, descriptive, not operational; Bell and others criticize universal models; Turner's concepts are widely over-applied.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.45. **Surprise:** yes.

## 21. Ignatian spiritual direction

**Definition.** A 16th-century protocol in which a director guides a retreatant through personalized exercises, adjusting each from the retreatant's reported inner responses, while explicitly limiting the director's influence.

**Why it matters here.** It is the oldest explicit statement of "observe, offer, never control", with rules a designer could use.
- The director stays neutral, like the balance of a scale, and does not push toward a choice (Annotation 15).
- The director is told the person's responses, not their private content, so the next exercise fits (17).
- Exercises are adapted to age, education and ability (18).
- If there are no responses, first check whether the exercises are being done at all and how (6); in distress, be gentle and help the person prepare (7).

**Key sources.**
- Ignatius of Loyola, *The Spiritual Exercises* (c. 1548), Elder Mullan translation, https://ccel.org/ccel/ignatius/exercises.ix.html
- David L. Fleming, *Draw Me into Your Friendship* (Institute of Jesuit Sources, 1996)†

**Instance.** The 30-day retreat, in which the director meets the retreatant daily, listens to what moved them, and chooses the next material.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Choosing the next offer from the player's response to the last (a stated or behavioural reaction), not from their performance, improves retention across a long arc.
2. A system with an authored preferred outcome, if players can detect it, loses trust; equalising alternatives restores it.
3. When there is no response, test whether the offer was perceived and performed before changing it.

**Failure modes.** Devotional context; no experimental evidence; reliance on a skilled human; director abuse is a known hazard; neutrality is aspirational.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.5. **Surprise:** yes.

## 22. Healthcare simulation and standardized patients

**Definition.** Staged clinical scenarios, with actors and mannequins, run by operators who control events in real time to meet learning goals.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Functional correspondence over physical fidelity.* Hamstra and colleagues argue fidelity should be judged by whether the simulation demands the same decisions as the real setting, and several studies find fidelity independent of educational effect. For "true to the setting": prioritise decisions and consequences over visual polish.
- *Realism as social practice.* Dieckmann, Gaba and Rall frame simulation as an interaction in which participants decide what counts as real.
- *Fiction contract.* A pre-brief agrees with participants how to treat the fiction; the commentary on Rudolph, Raemer and Simon (2014) groups it with clarifying expectations and psychological safety. Kayfabe in clinical dress.
- *Debriefing with good judgment.* Instructors state observations and ask real questions, exposing the learner's frame.

**Key sources.**
- Stanley J. Hamstra et al., "Reconsidering fidelity in simulation-based training", *Academic Medicine* 89(3), 2014, 387-392, https://mayoclinic.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/reconsidering-fidelity-in-simulation-based-training
- Peter Dieckmann, David Gaba and Marcus Rall, "Deepening the theoretical foundations of patient simulation as social practice", *Simulation in Healthcare* 2(3), 2007, 183-193
- Jenny W. Rudolph, Robert Simon, Ronald L. Dufresne and Daniel B. Raemer, "There's no such thing as 'nonjudgmental' debriefing", *Simulation in Healthcare* 1(1), 2006, 49-55
- Jenny W. Rudolph, Daniel B. Raemer and Robert Simon, "Establishing a safe container for learning in simulation", *Simulation in Healthcare* 9(6), 2014, 339-349, https://ubccpd.ca/sites/default/files/documents/establishing-a-safe-container-for-learning-in-simulation.pdf

**Instance.** A cardiac-arrest scenario in which an operator escalates the patient's state if the trainee stalls; the pre-brief explains what the manikin can and cannot do.

**Principles (testable).**
1. A scenario judged by whether it forces the same decisions as the real setting transfers as well as a visually richer one that does not.
2. An up-front statement of what the fiction does and does not simulate raises belief and willingness to take risks.
3. A debrief structure of observation followed by a real question surfaces more of the player's reasoning than a recap.

**Failure modes.** Training goals, not entertainment; the fidelity literature is contested (a 2026 paper still calls for nuance); effects of debriefing methods are mixed.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.5. **Surprise:** yes.

## 23. Wargaming adjudication and emergency-exercise control

**Definition.** Disciplines for running a simulated conflict or emergency for human participants, with referees ("umpires", "controllers") deciding what happens and a master list of timed events ("injects") structuring the scenario.

**Why it matters here.** This is the closest structural isomorph of the whole system.
- *Who decides what happens.* Wargaming splits adjudication into rigid (the rules are final), free (umpires use judgement and expertise) and in-between. Matrix games resolve actions by argument and plausibility. A level-of-detail city needs both: rules for what is simulated, judgement for what is only plausible.
- *Master Scenario Events List.* A chronological timeline of scripted events inserted by controllers to prompt participant action, tied to exercise objectives, with the actual delivery time and response recorded. Every inject has a stated purpose; a log allows audit.
- *Simulation Cell.* When part of the world is not present (the emergency operations centre, other agencies), a SimCell stands in and speaks in its voices. That is a low-detail simulation voicing off-stage actors to the player.
- *Free play.* Controllers can vary or drop injects as participants act.

**Key sources.**
- Peter P. Perla, *The Art of Wargaming* (Naval Institute Press, 1990); record https://researchspace.bathspa.ac.uk/8358/
- "Adjudication in Wargaming for Discovery", CSIAC, https://csiac.dtic.mil/articles/adjudication-in-wargaming-for-discovery/3
- Chris Engle's matrix games, see the History of Wargaming Project listing https://researchspace.bathspa.ac.uk/8333
- US Department of Homeland Security and FEMA, *Homeland Security Exercise and Evaluation Program (HSEEP)* (revised 2020)†; MSEL discussion at https://wmpllc.org/ojs/index.php/jem/article/view/1307 ; exercise guides https://www.mass.gov/doc/full-scale-exercise-master-scenario-events-list-inject-form/download

**Instance.** A functional exercise: controllers deliver MSEL injects by phone and message, a SimCell plays the absent agencies, and delivery times and responses are logged against objectives.

**Principles (testable).**
1. If every scripted event is tied to a stated dramatic objective and logged with its actual delivery, reviewers can say why each event existed, and defects found per hour rise.
2. Hybrid adjudication (rules for what is simulated, plausibility judgement for the rest) holds player trust better than either alone, measured by disputes and consistency.
3. A stand-in layer that speaks for off-stage actors, fed by the master list, keeps cause and effect traceable across levels of detail.

**Failure modes.** Aimed at evaluation and training, not delight; scripts can train participants to wait for cues; wargames are not predictive; guidance on writing good MSELs is thin.

**Relevance.** Core; confidence 0.8. **Surprise:** yes.

## 24. Long-running serial drama (soaps, procedurals, complex TV)

**Definition.** Television produced at volume over years, interleaving self-contained episodes with continuing arcs across an ensemble.

**Why it matters here.**
- *Interleaving at different tempos.* Daily serials run several storylines at once at different speeds, so each episode carries a bit of several.
- *Episodic plus serial.* Mittell describes recent television blending episodic and serial structures, with the X-Files balancing the pleasures of each. A human-authored set piece (the episode) must still advance something in the continuing arc; otherwise it reads as filler.
- *Continuity at scale.* Show bibles and story projections hold continuity across years and writers.
- *The viewer as the one who sees everything.* Modleski argues soaps position the viewer as the person who knows what no character knows.

**Key sources.**
- Jason Mittell, *Complex TV: The Poetics of Contemporary Television Storytelling* (NYU Press, 2015), https://muse.jhu.edu/book/76248
- Robert C. Allen, *Speaking of Soap Operas* (University of North Carolina Press, 1985)†
- Tania Modleski, *Loving with a Vengeance* (Archon, 1982)†

**Instance.** *The X-Files*: stand-alone "monster of the week" episodes alternating with mythology episodes, which Mittell treats as an episodic-serial balancing act.

**Principles (testable).**
1. Interleaving threads at three tempos (fast, medium, slow) sustains engagement across gaps in play better than a single thread at a time.
2. A self-contained piece that moves a continuing arc by one visible increment is rated as filler less often than one that does not.
3. Exposing the player to information no single character holds (the audience position) raises investment in the ensemble.

**Failure modes.** Linear and authored; recycling and bloat in long runs; the scholarship is descriptive.

**Relevance.** Supporting; confidence 0.6. **Surprise:** no.

## 25. Oral-formulaic composition and commedia dell'arte

**Definition.** Two historical performance traditions that built every performance from reusable parts and a skeleton plot, yet were experienced as fresh.

**Why it matters here.** They are the best-documented template systems that did not feel templated.
- *Oral-formulaic.* Parry and Lord's study of Balkan epic singers shows the singer does not memorise a text; each performance is composed live from formulas, themes (typical scenes) and story patterns. The song is a recognisable pattern, not a fixed text.
- *Commedia.* A canovaccio lists the acts and scenes; the rest is improvised from stock characters, comic business (lazzi) and set speeches, by actors who played one type for decades.
- *The lesson for grain.* The reusable unit was small (a phrase, a bit of business), selection was driven by live context, and the performer's persistent identity carried continuity.

**Key sources.**
- Albert B. Lord, *The Singer of Tales* (Harvard University Press, 1960), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Singer_of_Tales ; Milman Parry, *The Making of Homeric Verse* (ed. Adam Parry, Oxford, 1971)†
- Walter J. Ong, *Orality and Literacy* (1982)†
- Kenneth Richards and Laura Richards, *The Commedia dell'Arte: A Documentary History* (Blackwell, 1990); canovaccio overview https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canovaccio
- Pierre Louis Duchartre, *The Italian Comedy* (1929)†; Robert Henke, *Performance and Literature in the Commedia dell'Arte* (Cambridge University Press, 2002)†

**Instance.** Avdo Medjedovic dictated *The Wedding of Smailagic Meho* to Parry and Lord in July 1935 over five days, at over 12,000 lines (sources give 12,311 or 12,323), and said he knew longer songs (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wedding_of_Smailagi%C4%87_Meho).

**Principles (testable).**
1. Reusable units at phrase or gesture grain, recombined by live context, yield less perceived repetition than reusable units at scene grain.
2. A persistent character who performs recognisable business (a "lazzo") across different contexts builds anticipation and reduces "same NPC, new coat" complaints.
3. Varying length and detail of a fixed theme to the player's current attention produces varied performance without changing story shape.

**Failure modes.** The strict Parry-Lord thesis has been challenged (the formulaic is not the same as unoriginal; oral and literate cultures overlap); commedia improvisation may be less free than romanticised, with much memorised; both traditions relied on performers with a lifetime of training.

**Relevance.** Core; confidence 0.6. **Surprise:** yes.

## Cross-field convergences

1. **Observe, offer once, withdraw, then use the answer.** Montessori, Ignatian direction, escape-room hint ladders and immersive-theatre refusal handling agree: the guide presents, steps back and treats the response (including a refusal) as information.
2. **Whatever the participant does becomes established fact.** Improv, utilization, Playback and good GMing all treat ignoring a move as the worst fault.
3. **Consequence is legible only if it is visible and witnessed.** Wrestling's selling, ritual incorporation, Goffman's cooling out and service recovery all put the cost on show and have others acknowledge it.
4. **Scarce authored attention is allocated by the participant's behaviour.** Sleep No More one-on-ones, museum visitors stopping at a third of elements, and DJ floor signals.
5. **Perceived variety differs from statistical variety.** Spotify, magic's pattern principle, formulas and lazzi, and separation rules all manage what the audience can infer.
6. **Graded pre-commitment and traceability.** Warn/standby/go, the engagement roll and an event list tied to objectives each give a staged beat a reason and a lead time.
7. **Fidelity is functional.** Healthcare simulation, constraints-led coaching and wargaming all test whether the same decisions are demanded.
8. **Shared hazards.** Personalization read as surveillance (hospitality, therapy), illusionism discovered (magic, GMing), self-report flattered by generic content (Forer).

## Considered and rejected

- **Casino and slot design (variable-ratio reward):** aimed at compulsion and extraction, the opposite of the legible, consequential goal.
- **Theme park design (Imagineering, "weenies"):** already absorbed by game environmental storytelling, and the guest is on rails.
- **Adventure and outdoor education (Outward Bound, challenge by choice):** overlaps constraints-led coaching and ritual; no separate mechanism found.
- **Negotiation and hostage-crisis work:** informs dialogue with NPCs but not the staging of story.
- **Orchestral conducting and jazz ensemble:** useful for coordination but lack a participant whose choices shape the piece; improv covers the live-response part.
- **Standup comedy crowd work:** merged into improv.
- **Tango and contact improvisation (lead and follow):** apt metaphor for offers, but no documented craft body strong enough to cite.
- **Sports officiating (the advantage rule):** a nice analogy for not interrupting flow, but too narrow.
- **Recommender systems and intelligent tutoring:** the same engineering domain, not an isomorph.
- **Divination and tarot as narrative generators (including Calvino's *Castle of Crossed Destinies*):** folded into cold reading and Forer in field 9; combinatorial reading is covered by field 25.
- **Reality competition casting:** folded into field 17 because the part that transfers is editing and story producing.

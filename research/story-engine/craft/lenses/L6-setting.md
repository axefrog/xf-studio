# Lens L6: the setting

> Raw lens output, kept as provenance for the [discipline map](../map.md). Its claims are unverified unless marked otherwise.

**Method note.** Run on 22 October 2026 by a single lens working alone. I ran about 48 web searches and about 25 page fetches covering: Pondsmith's tabletop line (Cyberpunk 2013/2020/RED, lifepath, screamsheets, the 1991 Night City sourcebook); Shadowrun, The Sprawl, Technoir and Blades in the Dark; the genre canon and its critics (Sterling, Gibson, Jameson, Shiner, Alphin); noir criticism (Chandler, Cawelti, Dimendberg); CD Projekt Red developer interviews and critical reception of Cyberpunk 2077 and Edgerunners; and the social science the genre draws on (political economy, precarity, informal economies, organised crime, debt, surveillance, urbanism, subculture, the body, rumour and moral panic). Some sites were blocked (403/402) or truncated; I did not read any book in full. Tags: **[sec]** marks a claim resting only on a search-result summary or an unread secondary page. **[bk]** marks my background knowledge of a work, not re-read this session (bibliographic details of most of these were still confirmed against catalogue or publisher results). Untagged claims come from a fetched page, but the fetch tool returns a model summary, not raw text, so check any detail before quoting it. Quotations are kept to a minimum. Game-content details (factions, districts) come from my own knowledge of Cyberpunk 2077 unless a source is named. No story spoilers are used.

The unifying finding: "reads as Night City" is mostly produced by five things generic generators omit. These are a business model behind every faction, a felt local cost behind every corporate act, an unresolved obligation behind every named person, a media/rumour layer between events and what people believe, and a specific unexplained detail in every scene. The fields below say where each comes from.

---

## 1. Genre definition: Sterling's *Mirrorshades* preface and Gibson's Sprawl

- **Definition.** The founding statements of what cyberpunk is: technology that has escaped institutions and is reworked by the street, body-close and personal rather than monumental, global in corporate scope, and invading body and mind.
- **Why it matters here.** The system needs a positive definition of a cyberpunk *situation*, not just a look. Sterling's preface locates the genre in the merging of the technical world with street-level counterculture. It treats tech as intimate (implants, prosthetics, gadgets) and sets stories in worlds of multinationals and media networks.
- **Key sources.**
  - Bruce Sterling, preface to *Mirrorshades: The Cyberpunk Anthology* (Arbor House, 1986); text online at rudyrucker.com/mirrorshades/HTML/ (fetched).
  - William Gibson, "Burning Chrome" (*Omni*, July 1982), the story the phrase "the street finds its own uses for things" is usually credited to (attribution via Sterling's preface; story year [bk]).
  - Gibson, *Neuromancer* (Ace, 1984) [bk].
  - Larry McCaffery (ed.), *Storming the Reality Studio* (Duke UP, 1991).
- **Instance.** A street stall sells corporate-grade optic firmware reflashed to stream the wearer's view to a buyer. The encounter is about a product bent to an unintended use, and the customer has not noticed it is broadcasting.
- **Principles.**
  1. Every corporate technology shown in a scene has a street use its maker did not intend. Test: blind readers can name the "unintended use" in at least most scenes; scenes where they cannot read as generic sci-fi.
  2. Escalation is body- and block-scale before it is city-scale. Test: players rate personal-scale hooks as more "Night City" than infrastructure-scale ones.
- **Failure modes.** The mirrorshades become a costume (technology as the point); Shiner and others record this by 1987 (see 2). Reflexive mimicry of 1980s hardware (decks, dataterms) can read as retro pastiche rather than a living present.
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** No.

## 2. Critiques of cyberpunk's commodification, including of Cyberpunk 2077

- **Definition.** Critics' accounts of how cyberpunk lost its politics and became a style or power fantasy, plus political-economy readings of the genre as neoliberal subject-formation.
- **Why it matters here.** The named failure ("feels generic") is the aestheticised dystopia critics keep describing. Their objections double as tests the generator can run: is the oppressor concrete, is scarcity real, does the power fantasy undercut the dystopia?
- **Key sources.**
  - Lewis Shiner, "Confessions of an Ex-Literary Cyberpunk" (*New York Times*, early January 1991 [sec]; reprinted at fictionliberationfront.net/cyberpunk.html, fetched). By 1987 the style had become a formula of stock elements.
  - Fredric Jameson, *Postmodernism, or, The Cultural Logic of Late Capitalism* (Duke UP, 1991), the footnote regretting there is no chapter on cyberpunk, called the supreme expression of late capitalism (via Schweighauser's essay [sec]).
  - Caroline Alphin, *Neoliberalism and Cyberpunk Science Fiction: Living on the Edge of Burnout* (Routledge, 2021; PhilPapers record found) [sec].
  - Justin Charity, "Cyberpunk 2077's dystopia is a costume," The Ringer, 31 Dec 2020 (fetched; headline slug confirmed). Also the PC Gamer essay "Yes, Cyberpunk 2077 has something to say" (headline only; article not read) [sec], and the Slant Magazine review [sec].
- **Instance.** Charity's observation that Arasaka mostly harms its own staff and rivals, so ordinary citizens' grievance is unclear, becomes a design rule: each corporation gets one specific, locally felt harm (rent, repair lock-in, insurance denial) that street NPCs can name.
- **Principles.**
  1. Oppression is felt as a recurring cost to ordinary people, not only as a villain's plot. Test: ask players "what does this corporation do to people here?" and score the concreteness of answers.
  2. Scarcity cues exist: plentiful gear reads as a power fantasy. Test: count the ways the player is rationed, locked out or billed within a session.
- **Failure modes.** The defenders (PC Gamer, Slant) argue the shallowness is the point or that a megacorporation cannot sell anticapitalism cleanly [sec]; over-correcting yields lecturing. The critique is itself contested, so it is a source of tests, not a verdict.
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** No.

## 3. Pondsmith's tabletop line: attitude, street-level heroes, GM advice

- **Definition.** The source game's stated intent: players live by four tenets (*style over substance*, *attitude is everything*, *always take it to the edge*, *break the rules*) [sec, via a Wikipedia summary of Meeple Mountain, 2018]. Pondsmith frames the politics as fairness (people able to make a living without being exploited by those with more power) and the heroes as people fighting for family, friends and neighbourhood.
- **Why it matters here.** The setting's own author specifies the scale and purpose of its stories (neighbourhood-sized, personal, about being exploited) and demands *style* as a requirement. A generator that defaults to city-saving stakes contradicts the source.
- **Key sources.**
  - Mike Pondsmith interviews: NME ("Cyberpunk is a warning, not an aspiration"; fetched), GameBanshee (fetched), PC Gamer ("everything is political"; headline only) [sec], Dicebreaker 2020 [sec].
  - *Cyberpunk RED* core rulebook (R. Talsorian, Nov 2020): the GM chapter has a step-by-step Beat Chart (hook, conflict, resolution) and guidance on paranoia, pacing and mood, per The Tabletop Almanac's review (fetched).
  - *Cyberpunk* (1988, retitled *2013*), *Cyberpunk 2020* (1990) [Wikipedia article, fetched].
- **Instance.** A RED-style gig built on the Beat Chart: hook (a neighbour's debt), conflict (the lender is a gang's cousin), resolution (the player's presentation changes how the standoff goes). The stakes are one block.
- **Principles.**
  1. Default stakes are a person, crew or block; city-scale stakes are rare and earned. Test: compare retention and perceived meaning for block-scale and city-scale generated hooks.
  2. Each scene has a *style beat* in which how the character presents themself changes the outcome. Test: delete the beat and check whether blind raters call the scene flatter.
- **Failure modes.** *Style over substance* misread as "no substance"; *always take it to the edge* misread as "every scene ends in a firefight" (the same complaint is made of 2077's NCPD scanner hustles, see 8). Pondsmith's own account of the genre as a warning conflicts with the game's power-fantasy loop, which Charity notes.
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** No.

## 4. Lifepath: complications as a flowchart of plot hooks

- **Definition.** The character-background procedure in Cyberpunk 2020/RED that rolls or picks origins, family, friends, enemies, tragic love affairs and life goals as plot complications. Enemies come with who, cause, capability and intended revenge.
- **Why it matters here.** The system wants *caused* encounters. Lifepath is a compact, play-tested schema for giving an NPC unresolved relationships that make a scene arise from history rather than from a spawn table.
- **Key sources.**
  - R. Talsorian, "Cyberpunk Red Alert: Lifepath," 16 Oct 2020 (rtalsoriangames.com; fetched).
  - *Cyberpunk 2020* core (R. Talsorian, 1990): friends and enemies counted by d10 minus 7 and rolled on relationship tables (fan transcriptions [sec]).
  - Rob Wieland, Forbes, 2020, on Lifepath easing player buy-in (cited by Wikipedia's article [sec]).
- **Instance.** An NPC at a noodle stall: enemy is a former partner, cause is a sold-out job, capability is fixer contacts, revenge is a tip to the Claws. The scene is the moment he sees the player wearing his old partner's jacket.
- **Principles.**
  1. Every named NPC holds at least one open obligation (enemy, debt, mentor, lover) pointing at another NPC or faction. Test: for any encounter, a reviewer can trace "what earlier thing caused this?" through the data.
  2. Loss and tragic attachment are first-class edges, not only hostility. Test: relationship types in sampled NPCs are not all hostile or transactional.
- **Failure modes.** Pure rolling makes incoherent people; RED lets players change results that do not fit, which is a warning against uncurated randomness. Counting friends and enemies by d10 minus 7 makes most people have none, which is realistic but sparse.
- **Relevance.** Supporting (core for NPC backstory); medium-high confidence.
- **Surprise.** No.

## 5. Screamsheets: news as the hook layer

- **Definition.** In-world news pages used as adventure seeds. The 1990 *Cyberpunk 2020* box carried a separate Screamsheets booklet (credited to Pondsmith, Colin Fisk, Will Moss, Scott Ruggels) [sec]. RED's Jumpstart pairs a one-page in-world article with a short adventure [sec]. A 2024 RED DLC adds a Screamsheet Generator that assembles a page from dice or choices (R. Talsorian blog, fetched).
- **Why it matters here.** News stories solve three of the brief's requirements at once. They make events *legible* (the player learns first from the media), *caused* (a headline names a suspect and a beneficiary), and *consequential* (the story continues). They also bridge scales: one item can seed a street scene or a heist.
- **Key sources.**
  - R. Talsorian, "Cyberpunk RED Alert: November 2024 DLC Screamsheet Generator" (fetched).
  - Black Gate, "The Dark Corners of Cyberpunk 2020's Night City," on the 1991 sourcebook's headline generator (fetched).
  - Waynes Books on the 1990 box set [sec]; the GM-run "Screamsheets" Discord channel described at thecompleatdm.substack.com (fetched), a fan practice, not an official product.
- **Instance.** The fan GM's item: an explosion at a Kiroshi-owned chemical plant outside the city, the manager suspects Maelstrom, "more to follow." In the generator, one item yields a scanner event near the plant, a fixer's data-retrieval gig, and a vendor selling cheap goods nobody trusts.
- **Principles.**
  1. Every generated event above street scale leaves media residue before and after (headline, rumour, scanner chatter). Test: a player can reconstruct the event from residue alone.
  2. Headlines state the media's version, which differs from ground truth in a systematic way (wrong suspect, hidden beneficiary). Test: divergence rate correlates with investigation scenes started.
- **Failure modes.** News as unread wallpaper; tidy one-page adventures that read as templates; text-only delivery. A fixed headline grammar will repeat quickly.
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** No.

## 6. The Night City sourcebook (1991): a district as a bundle of generative tables

- **Definition.** *Night City* (R. Talsorian, 1991) presents 18 controlled urban zones in the voice of the city's information kiosks. Each zone has an overview, a map, personalities with stats and contacts, plot seeds, a d10 encounter table, gang turf and a security rating. A Combat Zone chapter adds blank maps and random-generation tables, and there is a headline generator (Black Gate review, fetched).
- **Why it matters here.** It is the best-evidenced precedent for the system's own need: per-district parameters from which both a generator and a level-of-detail sim can draw. The diegetic framing also shows how to deliver lore in-voice.
- **Key sources.**
  - Black Gate, "The Dark Corners of Cyberpunk 2020's Night City" (fetched).
  - Wikipedia, "Night City (Cyberpunk 2020)" (fetched): Don Collette's *White Wolf* #30 (Feb 1992) review calls it most useful for background and atmosphere, not a ready-to-run setting.
  - *Tales of the RED: Street Stories* (R. Talsorian, 2022): nine full missions across Night City zones [sec].
- **Instance.** A consulate entry notes that claims of a yakuza link have never been substantiated (Black Gate review), a plot seed in one line. The sim can keep an "unsubstantiated claims" slot per district and later substantiate, refute or let one lapse.
- **Principles.**
  1. A district's encounter distribution depends on its fixed identity and on live city state. Test: blind players can tell districts and times of day apart from sampled encounters alone.
  2. Each district carries standing rumours that resolve at some rate. Test: players report the city "remembering" things.
- **Failure modes.** Wandering-monster tables yield disconnected scenes; Collette's warning that background is not a playable setting; scale gags (the 5 million population is called laughable by a forum poster [sec]).
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes: a 1991 tabletop gazetteer is not where a quest designer would look first.

## 7. Cyberpunk tabletop mission cycles: Shadowrun, The Sprawl, Technoir, Blades in the Dark

- **Definition.** Formal job structures from games close to this genre. Shadowrun runs meet, legwork, the run, payoff, with the Johnson's double-cross as a complication tool. The Sprawl runs Get the Job, Legwork, Action, Get Paid, Retaliation, with clocks and a corporation clock that rises when crews take jobs against it [sec]. Technoir maps contacts on a plot map. Blades in the Dark follows each score with entanglements and downtime.
- **Why it matters here.** The system needs one job shape that works at 10 seconds and at hours. These games supply what video quests usually drop: an *aftermath and retaliation* phase that makes the job consequential.
- **Key sources.**
  - *Shadowrun, Fourth Edition* core rulebook (FanPro/Catalyst, 2005): the complications section on double-crosses (found via a third-party-hosted excerpt, not verified at source) [sec]; Catalyst forum thread proposing run phases [sec].
  - Hamish Cameron, *The Sprawl* (c. 2016) [sec].
  - Jeremy Keller, *Technoir* (2011): contacts and plot map, per The Alexandrian's description [sec].
  - John Harper, *Blades in the Dark* (Evil Hat, 2017): score, entanglement, downtime [sec].
- **Instance.** The Sprawl's Retaliation phase maps to a city-level pressure variable per corporation. It rises when the player works against that corporation and discharges as street-level consequences (a service refused, a tail, a raid).
- **Principles.**
  1. Every job ends with a payoff scene whose content varies with how loud or clean the job was. Test: payoff text correlates with a stored noise value.
  2. Complications reveal a hidden fact about the patron rather than only adding enemies. Test: players can state afterward what the patron concealed.
- **Failure modes.** Legwork swallows the session (one Reddit GM reports 90%) [sec]; 4E itself warns that too many complications make players feel they lost control; "every Johnson betrays you" is a worn cliché.
- **Relevance.** Supporting (the mission-structure lens will cover this more fully); medium-high confidence.
- **Surprise.** No.

## 8. CD Projekt Red's own practice: Street Stories, gigs and pacing

- **Definition.** Developers' statements on how Cyberpunk 2077's content was made: an Open World Team built small encounters called Street Stories; fixer gigs were compared to Witcher monster contracts; there was no quest typology (no "fetch" or "escort" boxes); quests cannot be failed, only consequenced; heavy and light quests are alternated.
- **Why it matters here.** The brief's failure (template quests that feel generic) is the one CDPR said it designed against, so its claims and its measured shortfalls both set the benchmark.
- **Key sources.**
  - Philipp Weber (Quest Design Coordinator), developer spotlight, reported by Game World Observer, 13 Sep 2019 (fetched).
  - Paweł Sasko (quest director) at E3 2019, on giving all quest types equal quality (PlayStation interview) [sec].
  - Jakub Knapik and Kacper Niepokólczycki, 80.lv interview on Night City's construction (fetched).
  - Critics of the result: ScreenRant on NCPD Scanner Hustles (via IMDb syndication) [sec]; Steam forum complaints that gigs fall into about six formulae [sec].
- **Instance.** Weber's note that quest designers mold systems around quests, not the reverse. For the generator: start from a situation with its own story logic, then pick mechanics, rather than choosing a typed template and decorating it.
- **Principles.**
  1. Do not type content as fetch/escort/kill; derive its objective from the situation. Test: players sorting generated content by "same kind of thing" produce more clusters than the generator's tag count, rather than fewer.
  2. Failure is a consequence, not a state. Test: no generated job has a "failed" terminus without a downstream effect.
- **Failure modes.** The benchmark has measured flaws: community and critic complaints that gigs repeat and that scanner hustles are short combat-only fillers out of character for V [sec]. Developers' statements are also promotion.
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** No.

## 9. Night City as typed setting data: factions, districts, corporations, fixers, media

- **Definition.** The canonical inventory: Arasaka and other megacorporations; gangs (Tyger Claws, Valentinos, Maelstrom, Voodoo Boys, 6th Street, Animals, Scavs and others); the NCPD and Max-Tac; Trauma Team; ripperdocs; fixers; netrunners; braindance; broadcast and street media; six districts plus the Badlands (the district count is from GameSpot's 2019 overview [sec]). Each faction in the game and in Pondsmith's 2020 material has its own trade and way of exerting power.
- **Why it matters here.** "True to the setting" is first of all the right entity doing the right thing in the right place. The generator needs typed, relational data: territory, economy, aesthetic, allies, enemies, methods.
- **Key sources.**
  - Marcin Batylda et al., *The World of Cyberpunk 2077* (Dark Horse, 2020) [sec].
  - Mike Pondsmith, *Cyberpunk 2020* corporation and gang material [bk].
  - CDPR art directors on district identity and time layers (80.lv, fetched). The Fandom wiki is community-written and inconsistent between 2020 and 2077 canon, so use it as a pointer only.
- **Instance.** Derive conflict from business model: a protection racket tolls, a data enclave sells access, a cyberware faction recruits, a vice gang runs entertainment venues. Their encounters differ because what they want from a passer-by differs.
- **Principles.**
  1. A faction's behaviour follows from what it sells, to whom, and how it enforces payment. Test: lore-literate players predict what a given faction's NPC wants in a scene.
  2. A faction out of its turf, or out of its era's style, is itself a story. Test: players rate "wrong place" encounters as more notable than baseline.
- **Failure modes.** Faction-as-palette-swap; lore-dumps; ethnic coding that hardens into stereotype (see 22); drift between editions.
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** No.

## 10. Noir and hard-boiled conventions

- **Definition.** The grammar of the hard-boiled tradition: a compromised protagonist working a case that is bigger than the brief, a client who lies, corruption that reaches up, and the city as clue.
- **Why it matters here.** Noir supplies the investigative and moral structure for the setting: the first story told is never the real one, and every option costs someone. It also explains why Night City works as a noir city (a space organised by money, power and movement).
- **Key sources.**
  - Raymond Chandler, "The Simple Art of Murder," *The Atlantic*, December 1944 (collected 1950); the essay argues for the detective as an honourable common man in a corrupt world.
  - John G. Cawelti, *Adventure, Mystery, and Romance* (University of Chicago Press, 1976), chapters 6 and 7 on the hard-boiled formula (flawed detectives, urban settings) [sec].
  - Edward Dimendberg, *Film Noir and the Spaces of Modernity* (Harvard UP, 2004): noir as a continuation of the metropolis, including the shift from walkable dense centres to dispersed ones [sec].
  - *Blade Runner* (Ridley Scott, 1982) as the cyberpunk-noir template [bk].
- **Instance.** A "simple retrieval" whose location contains physical evidence contradicting the client's account (a body older than the job). The player can learn the job is a pretext before any dialogue says so.
- **Principles.**
  1. The first brief is incomplete in a way discoverable from the environment alone. Test: discovery rate of the contradicting clue without dialogue.
  2. Choices are between compromises with named costs to someone the player has met. Test: no generated choice has a cost-free best option.
- **Failure modes.** Noir pastiche (voiceover, rain, femme fatale) as costume; Chandler's honourable-man ideal sits awkwardly with cyberpunk cynicism; the femme-fatale trope carries sexist baggage [bk].
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** No.

## 11. Corporate sovereignty and enclave zones

- **Definition.** Political-economy work on how private power acquires quasi-state authority through zones with their own rules (free-trade and special economic zones), infrastructure and contracts.
- **Why it matters here.** It shows how corporate power appears at street level: boundaries, checkpoints, permits, liability waivers, private security. That turns "corporations are evil" into places and procedures the player can cross.
- **Key sources.**
  - Keller Easterling, *Extrastatecraft: The Power of Infrastructure Space* (Verso, 2014): zones as spaces under authorities independent of the host state's law (publisher and reviews, found via search).
  - Aihwa Ong, *Neoliberalism as Exception* (Duke UP, 2006) [bk].
  - Colin Crouch, *Post-Democracy* (Polity, 2004) [bk].
  - Mike Davis, "Fortress L.A." in *City of Quartz* (Verso, 1990), on private police and security architecture (obituary and review results) [sec].
- **Instance.** Walking from a corporate plaza to the street: the checkpoint is the story. Inside, a different rule-set applies (a recording policy, a waiver you accepted at the door); a job requires crossing with contractor status.
- **Principles.**
  1. Zone borders are narrative objects with a rule-set that changes what the player can do. Test: players can state the rule that differs across a given border.
  2. Corporate harm is administrative (fees, permits, waivers) at least as often as violent. Test: ratio of administrative to violent corporate interactions in sampled content.
- **Failure modes.** The monolithic evil corporation that Charity criticises; bureaucracy that reads as dull friction if not tied to stakes.
- **Relevance.** Supporting; medium confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 12. Precarity and platform labour

- **Definition.** The sociology of insecure work and of platforms that dispatch it: ratings, algorithmic pay, risk transferred to the contractor, deactivation.
- **Why it matters here.** Gigs *are* a gig economy. The generator can produce recognisable gig terms (non-negotiable fees, penalty clauses, consumables the contractor pays for) and model reputation as a currency that gates access.
- **Key sources.**
  - Guy Standing, *The Precariat: The New Dangerous Class* (Bloomsbury, 2011) [bk].
  - Nick Srnicek, *Platform Capitalism* (Polity, 2016) [bk].
  - Alex Rosenblat, *Uberland: How Algorithms Are Rewriting the Rules of Work* (University of California Press, 2018) [bk].
  - Informal essays reading fixers as gig platforms (Medium; not scholarship) [sec].
- **Instance.** A fixer's posting reprices itself with the local supply of mercenaries; a reputation score gates the better listings; the payoff arrives net of a handling fee. In a street scene a courier's bike is locked by a lapsed subscription.
- **Principles.**
  1. Gig terms transfer risk to the contractor in specific, recognisable ways. Test: players can say what the gig took from them beyond the headline pay.
  2. Reputation decays and is asymmetric (a single bad result outweighs several good ones). Test: access to offers reflects history rather than level.
- **Failure modes.** Overly literal ride-share satire; punishing the player with mechanics rather than stories; the game's own gigs let the player choose freely, which weakens the precarity.
- **Relevance.** Core for gigs; medium-high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 13. Informal economies and "people as infrastructure"

- **Definition.** Ethnographies of unregistered work and of social networks doing the work of infrastructure.
- **Why it matters here.** It supplies the texture of how a street survives, which is mostly service, favour and mediation, not violence. That widens a seconds-long street encounter beyond combat and crime.
- **Key sources.**
  - Sudhir Venkatesh, *Off the Books: The Underground Economy of the Urban Poor* (Harvard UP, 2006): a Chicago neighbourhood where household services, small repair trades, storefront clergy mediating disputes and gangs all take part in the economy (reviews, found via search).
  - AbdouMaliq Simone, "People as Infrastructure: Intersecting Fragments in Johannesburg," *Public Culture* 16(3), 2004, pp. 407-429 (abstract confirmed).
  - Mike Davis, *Planet of Slums* (Verso, 2006) [bk].
- **Instance.** A ripperdoc's back room lends on credit; a street mediator stands between a Maelstrom crew and a vendor; a lookout, a cook and a mechanic form a mutually dependent trio.
- **Principles.**
  1. Most street NPC activity is informal service or mediation, not violence. Test: sample the activity distribution of generated street NPCs.
  2. Services interlock, so removing one changes others. Test: kill a vendor in a perturbation run and check that prices or availability shift nearby.
- **Failure modes.** Poverty tourism and the noble-poor trope; Venkatesh's methods and ethics have drawn criticism [bk].
- **Relevance.** Core for street scale; medium-high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 14. Organised-crime sociology: protection, trust and costly signals

- **Definition.** Studies of how groups without courts create order: protection as a product, credentials, costly signals, and trust among people who have incentives to lie.
- **Why it matters here.** It gives gang and fixer behaviour rules (what a gang taxes, tolerates or punishes) and explains how strangers establish trust for a job, which is the basis of the double-cross.
- **Key sources.**
  - Diego Gambetta, *The Sicilian Mafia: The Business of Private Protection* (Harvard UP, 1993) [bk].
  - Gambetta, *Codes of the Underworld: How Criminals Communicate* (Princeton UP, 2009): costly signals, with tattoos and scars working as résumé lines (publisher blurb and reviews).
  - Charles Tilly, "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime," in *Bringing the State Back In* (Cambridge UP, 1985), pp. 169-191: state-making as protection racket (confirmed).
  - Federico Varese, *The Russian Mafia* (Oxford UP, 2001) [bk].
- **Instance.** A new contact asks the player to carry a sealed package across a block first. This is a credential test, and the contact also gives collateral information about himself in return.
- **Principles.**
  1. Pressure in controlled turf is regular and calculable (tolls, "fees"), with violence as the exception. Test: in-turf encounters show negotiation or payment more often than assault.
  2. Trust between strangers requires a costly signal (reference, collateral, shared risk). Test: every job offer from an unknown includes a visible signal exchange.
- **Failure modes.** Mafia-film clichés; shakedown as the only gang plot; treating protection as pure villainy when residents rely on it, which Tilly's framing both explains and complicates.
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 15. Obligation and fairness: debt, gift and moral economy

- **Definition.** Anthropology and history of obligation: debt as a moral relationship, gift exchange, and communities' norms about what exploitation is legitimate and what provokes collective response.
- **Why it matters here.** Pondsmith frames the politics as fairness, which is the theory of moral economy. Obligations tracked as edges are what make later content *caused*: the debt gets called, the favour gets returned, the insult gets repaid.
- **Key sources.**
  - David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (Melville House, 2011) [bk].
  - Marcel Mauss, *The Gift* (1925; English translations 1954 and 1990) [bk].
  - E. P. Thompson, "The Moral Economy of the English Crowd in the Eighteenth Century," *Past & Present* 50 (1971) [bk].
  - James C. Scott, *Weapons of the Weak* (Yale UP, 1985), on everyday resistance [bk]; Pondsmith's fairness framing (NME, fetched).
- **Instance.** A ripperdoc patches the player on credit ("pay me when you can"). Three in-game days later a stranger invokes the debt on behalf of the ripperdoc's creditor. Elsewhere, a block answers an algorithmic rent rise with small acts: scrawls, jammed drones, cut lights.
- **Principles.**
  1. Unrepaid obligations accumulate as visible, queued hooks that resolve within an interval. Test: players can list their open obligations and when they come due.
  2. Collective reactions trigger from a norm violation, start small and deniable, and escalate only on repetition. Test: reaction intensity tracks violation count rather than random draw.
- **Failure modes.** Debt as "owe money, do mission"; forced indebtedness that annoys players; romanticising resistance; Graeber's historical claims are contested [bk].
- **Relevance.** Core for consequence tracking; medium-high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 16. Tragic register and tonal pacing

- **Definition.** The genre's tonal range: fatalism and loss (noir, Edgerunners), black humour and excess (style), and quiet street life, with the need to alternate them.
- **Why it matters here.** "Well paced" is partly tonal: a generator that only emits grim violence or only emits wry colour reads as generic. The setting wants tragedy that arises from the character's own choices inside a hostile system.
- **Key sources.**
  - Philipp Weber on mixing sad, low-action quests with upbeat dynamic ones, and on the story coming first (Game World Observer, 2019, fetched).
  - Bartosz Sztybor interviews on *Cyberpunk: Edgerunners*: there was never a happy ending, and darker alternatives were pitched (Anime Corner; PC Gamer headline) [sec].
  - Pondsmith on the setting as a warning (NME, fetched).
- **Instance.** After a bleak loss, the next generated beat is low-stakes and warm (a vendor who remembers the player's order). A foreshadowed tragedy is delivered so the player can still act inside it.
- **Principles.**
  1. Tone alternates on a tracked curve rather than at random. Test: session tone traces show heavy beats followed by lighter ones within a short span.
  2. Tragedy needs foreshadowed inevitability plus player agency inside it. Test: players name the earlier point when they could have chosen otherwise.
- **Failure modes.** Misery porn and grimdark; the Edgerunners team's own rejected pitches show how far bleakness can go [sec]; tone reported from interviews is second-hand.
- **Relevance.** Supporting; medium confidence.
- **Surprise.** No.

## 17. Brokerage and the fixer

- **Definition.** Network-theory and field studies of people who bridge gaps between groups and profit from information and access, including real-world journalistic "fixers"; in the game line, the Fixer role and Shadowrun's Johnson and fixer.
- **Why it matters here.** The fixer is the main quest-giver. A believable fixer sells access, deniability and curation. Their offer set depends on their network position and interests, not on a quest pool.
- **Key sources.**
  - Ronald Burt, *Structural Holes: The Social Structure of Competition* (Harvard UP, 1992) [bk].
  - Lindsay Palmer, *The Fixers* (Oxford UP, 2019; listings give differing subtitles): interviews with 75 fixers, who act as local collaborators rather than assistants (search results).
  - *Cyberpunk RED* core: Fixers as one of ten Roles, information brokers and "Faces" [sec]; Shadowrun 4E on Johnsons and fixers [sec].
- **Instance.** A fixer shows two of five available gigs: the ones that help another client of hers. She leaves out one that competes with her own interests and tells the player what is deniable and what is not.
- **Principles.**
  1. A fixer's offers are a function of network position and interests. Test: two fixers given the same job pool present different sets.
  2. Fixers sell information gaps by design. Test: each offer states what is withheld, discoverable on inspection.
- **Failure modes.** Fixer as a menu; fixer as secret betrayer by default (a Shadowrun cliché); the real-world fixer literature concerns journalism and may not transfer cleanly.
- **Relevance.** Core; medium-high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 18. The body: cyborg theory, commodified flesh and the disability critique

- **Definition.** Cultural theory of cyborg identity, sociology of the body as a market good, and disability studies' critique of narratives that equate augmentation with lost humanity.
- **Why it matters here.** Cyberware is the genre-specific source of consequences: maintenance, repossession, rejection, pain, access, class. The generator needs a grammar of body stories that goes beyond cyberpsychosis-of-the-week and avoids ableist tropes.
- **Key sources.**
  - Donna Haraway, "A Manifesto for Cyborgs," *Socialist Review* (1985; usually reprinted as "A Cyborg Manifesto").
  - N. Katherine Hayles, *How We Became Posthuman* (University of Chicago Press, 1999) [sec on press].
  - Nancy Scheper-Hughes and Loïc Wacquant (eds.), *Commodifying Bodies* (Sage, 2002): organ trafficking and the "ethic of parts."
  - A *Journal of Ethics and Emerging Technologies* article arguing the "ugly is evil" trope links disability and moral failing, opening with Cyberpunk 2077 promotional art [sec]; Pondsmith's remarks that augmentation will become normal (GameBanshee, fetched).
- **Instance.** A repossession gig: collect a leased optic from a debtor. The horror lies in legal ownership of a body part, not a monster.
- **Principles.**
  1. Body stories centre ownership, maintenance and access more than madness. Test: classify the cause category of sampled cyberware content.
  2. Prosthetic or augmented NPCs are not default villains or victims. Test: disposition distribution by augmentation level has no strong correlation.
- **Failure modes.** The cyberpsychosis framing is read by some as equating disability with danger [sec; the critique is informal in the sources I found]; body horror used as shock; organ-trade plots that become exploitation spectacle.
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes for the commodification and disability literature; no for Haraway.

## 19. Surveillance, control and the sorting of people

- **Definition.** Theories of how power watches and sorts: discipline, control through modulation, behavioural data as a product, and surveillance's racialised history.
- **Why it matters here.** It puts surveillance in the logistics (who sees, who sells, what changes) instead of in vibes of cameras. Graded responses (price, access, service tier) drive consequence better than a binary alarm.
- **Key sources.**
  - Michel Foucault, *Discipline and Punish* (1975; English 1977) [bk].
  - Gilles Deleuze, "Postscript on the Societies of Control," *October* 59 (1992) [bk].
  - Shoshana Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (PublicAffairs, 2019) [bk].
  - Simone Browne, *Dark Matters: On the Surveillance of Blackness* (Duke UP, 2015): how monitoring tools grow from earlier methods of policing Black people (publisher record).
- **Instance.** After a street fight, a data broker buys the player's face. Days later a vendor refuses service and a medical coverage tier shifts. The control is modulation, not arrest.
- **Principles.**
  1. Surveillance outcomes are graded (prices, access, tiers), not binary wanted states. Test: log response types and check that most are non-violent and non-binary.
  2. Evidence has owners and prices; a witness trail is tradable. Test: a scene's evidence can be bought, sold or suppressed.
- **Failure modes.** Omniscient surveillance removes player agency; wanted-level binaries; treating real-world surveillance of marginalised groups as aesthetic.
- **Relevance.** Supporting; medium-high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 20. Urban form: fortress architecture, gentrification and time layers

- **Definition.** Critical urbanism on how cities stratify space and change: security-driven architecture, capital-led displacement, the commodification of local "authenticity", and visible layers of history.
- **Why it matters here.** Places say who they are for. District state (decay or gentrification) can be a slow-moving variable the sim evolves, with visible markers. CDPR's art team used the same idea explicitly.
- **Key sources.**
  - Mike Davis, *City of Quartz* (Verso, 1990), including "Fortress L.A." (confirmed via obituary and review results).
  - Neil Smith, *The New Urban Frontier: Gentrification and the Revanchist City* (Routledge, 1996) [bk].
  - Sharon Zukin, *Naked City: The Death and Life of Authentic Urban Places* (Oxford UP, 2010) [bk].
  - Jakub Knapik and Kacper Niepokólczycki (80.lv, fetched): Night City's history encoded as time layers, and a "rule of contrast" (an ordinary house with an aircraft through its roof).
- **Instance.** A street encounter built on contrast: something ordinary to residents and jarring to outsiders (the crashed vehicle nobody remarks on). A neighbourhood's markers (older tenants' shrines, new corporate signage) let a player date its state.
- **Principles.**
  1. District state drifts along a decay-to-gentrification axis driven by capital events, with visible markers. Test: players can order two snapshots of a district in time.
  2. Each location answers "who is this space for, and who is it against?" Test: reviewers can answer for any generated location.
- **Failure modes.** Ruin-porn; gentrification as a simple villain; slum tourism; American-city theory transposed to a fictional Californian city.
- **Relevance.** Core for the LOD sim; medium-high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 21. Urban legibility and street life

- **Definition.** The image of the city (paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks), "eyes on the street," and walking as everyday tactics.
- **Why it matters here.** The brief demands *legible* content and a seconds-long encounter that feels placed. These sources give a spatial grammar: where an encounter happens (node, edge, path) and who is watching.
- **Key sources.**
  - Kevin Lynch, *The Image of the City* (MIT Press, 1960) [bk].
  - Jane Jacobs, *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (Random House, 1961) [bk].
  - Michel de Certeau, *The Practice of Everyday Life* (University of California Press, 1984) [bk].
  - Dimendberg (see 10), on noir's treatment of walkable versus dispersed space [sec].
- **Instance.** An encounter at a district edge, witnessed by a named shopkeeper who later remembers it and mentions it to someone.
- **Principles.**
  1. Encounters sit at nodes and edges with named local witnesses. Test: witnesses can be recalled and referenced by later content.
  2. Each district has recognisable landmark and edge types. Test: players can sketch a district map from memory with correct edges.
- **Failure modes.** Mid-century American urbanism does not map cleanly onto a vertical, security-stratified city; Jacobs's "eyes on the street" and Lynch's small sample have both been criticised [bk].
- **Relevance.** Supporting; medium confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 22. Subculture, style and bricolage

- **Definition.** Cultural studies of style as meaning: groups appropriate commodities to signal difference, and are then recuperated as commodity style.
- **Why it matters here.** *Style over substance* is a design brief. Gang and subculture identity is carried by visible signs, so appearance can be generated from coherent sign systems and can double as information for the player. Recuperation (the corporation sells the look back) is a built-in story engine.
- **Key sources.**
  - Dick Hebdige, *Subculture: The Meaning of Style* (Methuen, 1979): bricolage, homology, signifying practice (summaries and catalogue).
  - Gibson's "street finds its own uses" (as above), the genre's own statement of bricolage.
  - Gambetta, *Codes of the Underworld* (see 14), on tattoos and scars as signals.
- **Instance.** A corporate campaign sells a "Maelstrom look" to the safe suburbs. The gang answers by changing its signs, so a player who knows the old code misreads the new one.
- **Principles.**
  1. Appearance is an information channel. Test: informed players can predict affiliation for most generated NPCs from appearance alone.
  2. Styles get co-opted and then change. Test: sign sets drift over simulated time.
- **Failure modes.** Appearance reduced to costume swaps; "punk" without politics (Shiner's point); overlapping looks that blur factions.
- **Relevance.** Supporting; medium confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 23. Techno-orientalism and the representation of ethnicity

- **Definition.** Scholarship on the genre's habit of imagining Asia as hyper- or hypo-technological, and critiques of how ethnic communities are coded as gangs.
- **Why it matters here.** "True to the setting" includes its multicultural makeup, and the commonest lazy signifier of cyberpunk is neon East Asian lettering. Stereotyped ethnic gang coding is a known liability. The generator needs guardrails and specificity.
- **Key sources.**
  - David S. Roh, Betsy Huang and Greta A. Niu (eds.), *Techno-Orientalism: Imagining Asia in Speculative Fiction, History, and Media* (Rutgers UP, 2015).
  - Mike Pondsmith's replies to E3 2019 criticism of the Animals and Voodoo Boys (Video Games Chronicle, fetched; TheGamer [sec]): he says the Voodoo Boys originated as a commentary on cultural appropriation and that the Animals' self-image is the point.
  - A thesis analysing 2077 antagonists with postcolonial frameworks (found via search, not read) [sec].
- **Instance.** Replace a stock sign in unreadable lettering with one whose wording fits a specific community and business; give a Japanese-coded NPC a job that is not yakuza or corporate security.
- **Principles.**
  1. Every cultural marker carries a particular community and function. Test: cultural readers review a sample and can say what the marker refers to.
  2. Role distributions per coded group are not dominated by crime or threat. Test: tabulate roles by coded group in sampled content.
- **Failure modes.** Overcorrection that erases cultural detail; "positive stereotypes"; designers outside the culture deciding what is authentic; Pondsmith's defence is contested by the critics he was answering.
- **Relevance.** Supporting, with high risk; medium confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 24. Rumour, moral panic and the media ecology

- **Definition.** Sociology of how informal news and media amplification shape what a population believes: rumour as collective problem-solving when official channels fail, and moral panics in which a group is defined as a threat and the response worsens the problem.
- **Why it matters here.** Causation in the city runs through belief. Rumour and panic let one event produce different local consequences, and give a pipeline: event, media version, local rumour, institutional response. A cyberpsycho scare that ends in dragnets and shakedowns of anyone visibly augmented is a native example.
- **Key sources.**
  - Tamotsu Shibutani, *Improvised News: A Sociological Study of Rumor* (Bobbs-Merrill, 1966) (catalogue and summaries).
  - Stanley Cohen, *Folk Devils and Moral Panics* (MacGibbon & Kee, 1972): media amplification of the Mods and Rockers scuffles (summaries).
  - Stuart Hall et al., *Policing the Crisis* (Macmillan, 1978) [sec].
  - Guy Debord, *The Society of the Spectacle* (1967) [bk]; Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky, *Manufacturing Consent* (1988) [bk].
- **Instance.** An incident is amplified into a scare. Patrols sweep the district, vendors with visible chrome are shaken down, a fixer advertises private security, and local rumour blames a corporate test.
- **Principles.**
  1. Event, media version, rumour and institutional response are separate stages with different content. Test: the stages can disagree in the data.
  2. Rumour spreads along social ties with distortion, and unverifiable claims create investigative content. Test: rumour variants differ by teller and district.
- **Failure modes.** Media as the villain; rumour as pure noise; panic theory has been criticised for assuming irrational publics [bk].
- **Relevance.** Core for causal and consequence layer; medium-high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## 25. Worldbuilding by incidental detail: reading protocols and slang

- **Definition.** Science fiction is read by protocols in which a throwaway phrase is decoded as a world fact. Gibson-style prose establishes a world through brands, jargon and undefined detail rather than exposition; Cyberpunk adds its own slang and currency.
- **Why it matters here.** "Reads as Night City versus generic" is largely carried by small unexplained specifics. A generator can systematically produce them (named products, prices in eddies, local idiom) and can be measured on their density.
- **Key sources.**
  - Samuel R. Delany, "About 5,750 Words," in *The Jewel-Hinged Jaw* (Dragon Press, 1977) (essay confirmed; argument [bk]).
  - Gibson, *Neuromancer* (Ace, 1984) [bk].
  - Pondsmith's street slang and the sourcebook's diegetic Dataterm voice (Black Gate review, fetched).
- **Instance.** "A gang member" becomes "a Maelstrom ripperdoc's apprentice whose refurbished optic flickers when he lies, priced at three weeks of rent." The detail explains the world without a line of exposition.
- **Principles.**
  1. Each scene carries at least one named, priced or branded specific that it does not explain. Test: A/B preference for scenes with and without such details.
  2. Characters never explain what they would not need explained. Test: measure exposition per scene.
- **Failure modes.** Noun salad and jargon overload; "brand soup" with no consequence; slang used by the wrong faction or era.
- **Relevance.** Core; high confidence.
- **Surprise.** Yes.

## Cross-cutting tests for "reads as Night City"

1. Can a lore-literate player predict what a given faction's NPC wants (9, 14)?
2. Can a player say what a corporation does to ordinary people here (2, 11)?
3. Can a reviewer trace any encounter to an earlier cause (4, 15)?
4. Does the player first meet big events as headlines and rumour, and do the two versions differ (5, 24)?
5. Does each scene carry one unexplained specific and one unintended use of corporate tech (1, 25)?
6. Do street NPCs mostly do informal service, not violence (13)?
7. Is the first brief incomplete in a way the environment reveals (10)?
8. Is the stake the size of a block or a person (3)?

## Sources considered and rejected

- Wikipedia "Cyberpunk" overview: too general to add beyond Sterling and Gibson.
- Fandom wiki lore pages: community-authored and inconsistent across editions; used only as pointers.
- Medium essays on 2077 and neoliberalism or Marxism: unreviewed; used only to locate scholarship.
- Reddit and Lemmy threads on Shadowrun legwork and cyberpsychosis: anecdotal; cited only where flagged [sec].
- Baudrillard, *Simulacra and Simulation*: heavy and gives a generator little it can act on beyond Debord.
- Naomi Klein, *The Shock Doctrine*: peripheral; Night City's post-war rebuilding fits it, but I found nothing generator-specific.
- Heist-film scholarship: belongs to the mission-structure lens, and I did not verify any source.
- GDC or Digital Dragons talks on Night City design: searched, none found; the PC Gamer "Faces of Night City" and GamesRadar district pieces were truncated, so unused.
- Domus article on Night City's construction and the GameSpot sidequest article: returned 403; used only via search summaries.
- Walter Jon Williams, *Hardwired* (named as an inspiration for Pondsmith in Wikipedia): not pursued as its own field.
- Snow Crash and Altered Carbon as separate cyberpunk canon: they largely overlap fields 1 and 11, and I added nothing beyond Alphin's use of them.

## 1. Flash fiction, microfiction and the iceberg principle
- **Definition:** Complete stories of roughly 6 to 1,000 words that work by implication, leaving most of the story unwritten but felt.
- **Scales:** Glance, encounter.
- **Why it matters here:** An overheard line or a one-second sight (a lone child's shoe on a rooftop, a shuttered stall with fresh flowers) has to feel like the visible tip of a caused situation. This field is the craft of making a fragment imply a cause and a consequence without delivering either.
- **Key sources:**
  - Shapard and Thomas (eds.), *Sudden Fiction* (Gibbs Smith, 1986), and *Flash Fiction Forward* (Norton, 2006) [sec].
  - Jerome Stern, *Micro Fiction* (Norton, 1996), stories under about 250 words [sec].
  - Hemingway, *Death in the Afternoon* (Scribner, 1932), the iceberg passage.
  - Hemingway, *A Moveable Feast* (Scribner, 1964), on cutting the real ending of "Out of Season" [sec].
- **Instance:** The "baby shoes" six-word story. Its Hemingway attribution is unverified: the earliest link found was 1991, and the advertisement form appeared in newspapers as early as 1906 [sec]. Use it as an example of the form, never as a citation.
- **Principles:**
  1. A glance works only if the omitted material exists somewhere in the world model: when what is shown is backed by authored or simulated facts, players infer that more lies behind it (Hemingway's claim that the reader feels what the writer knows but omits). Test: show glances backed by real facts and glances with no backing, and measure follow-up rates.
  2. One glance carries one implied change: a glance that implies two unrelated situations reads as noise. Test: ask players to state what they think happened; one answer is a pass.
  3. Omission by ignorance leaves "hollow places" (Hemingway's other half). Test: generated glances whose backing fact is missing are spotted by players as filler at a higher rate.
- **Failure modes:** Cleverness over cause; every glance built as a twist; implication so thin that nobody notices it; six-word cuteness that has no follow-up path.
- **Relevance:** Core for the glance scale. Confidence high on the principle, medium on exact thresholds.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 2. Invisible theatre, street theatre and public overhearing
- **Definition:** Performers stage a rehearsed scene in a public place so that bystanders take it as real and react, becoming "spect-actors" (Boal). Closely related is the sociology of how strangers manage glances and overheard talk in public.
- **Scales:** Glance, encounter.
- **Why it matters here:** The player passes strangers all day and the system never controls them. Invisible theatre is a worked-out craft for staging a scene that must stay legible while a stranger is free to walk past, join in or intervene, and for what a designer does about the three possible audience behaviours (ignore, watch, join).
- **Key sources:**
  - Boal, *Theatre of the Oppressed* (Urizen, 1979; Pluto, 2000) [bk].
  - Wikipedia, "Invisible theater," for the origin (Argentina, 1960s, a restaurant bill dispute) [sec]: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invisible_theater
  - Beautiful Trouble, "Invisible theatre": https://beautifultrouble.org/tool/invisible-theatre [sec]
  - Goffman, *Behavior in Public Places* (Free Press, 1963), on civil inattention [bk].
- **Instance:** Boal's restaurant piece: actors at different tables escalate a dispute over a bill until diners take sides. Translate: two street figures argue near a food stall, and the system watches who engages and for how long.
- **Principles:**
  1. A street scene needs a readable premise within its first few seconds and a second layer for those who stay: observers who stop for ten seconds get something the passers-by do not. Test: dwell times split into a glance group and a stay group, each with a distinct payoff.
  2. A scene built for observers must be complete if nobody joins; joining only adds. Test: no scene soft-locks or looks broken when ignored.
  3. The scene should degrade by dissolving, not by hanging unresolved. Test: abandoned scenes leave a plausible aftermath trace rather than a frozen tableau.
- **Failure modes:** Scenes that only work if the player joins; staged scenes so loud that they read as a trap; repeating the same argument at every street corner.
- **Relevance:** Core for glance and encounter. Confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes. It is a performing-arts tradition built around the audience not knowing it is an audience, which is the system's exact condition.

## 3. The short story and unity of effect
- **Definition:** A single narrative built to be taken in at one sitting, in which every element serves one effect (Poe's argument for the tale over the novel).
- **Scales:** Vignette, small gig.
- **Why it matters here:** A vignette or a one-hour gig that is not allowed to stretch across sessions must still end satisfyingly. The short story supplies the closing discipline that long serial forms often lack, in particular the rule that a short piece should fix on one effect.
- **Key sources:**
  - Poe, review of Hawthorne's *Twice-Told Tales*, *Graham's Magazine*, May 1842: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Review_of_Twice-Told_Tales
  - Boston Literary History, Poe review transcript: https://www.bostonliteraryhistory.com/node/32.html [sec]
  - Joyce Carol Oates and others on the short story as a form could extend this [not searched].
- **Instance:** A twenty-minute courier gig built to leave one feeling (unease at what was in the package) rather than a three-way moral dilemma.
- **Principles:**
  1. A piece meant to be finished in one sitting should name one dominant effect at authoring time, and every scene should be checkable against it. Test: reviewers can name the effect and flag scenes that do not serve it.
  2. The ending should arrive at the end of the player's play-time, not the plot's. Test: completion happens within a stated duration budget.
  3. Unity breaks when a second effect is added from outside (an unrelated callback); keep it as a hook, not an ingredient. Test: callbacks are placed after the effect has landed.
- **Failure modes:** Stretching a short piece into mush; ending on a lecture; effect chosen by default (always danger).
- **Relevance:** Supporting. Confidence medium. The Poe doctrine is old and partial (it ignores the serial), but it is the right counterweight.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 4. Screenplay structural hierarchy (beat, scene, sequence, act)
- **Definition:** A nested decomposition of a story into units, each defined by a change in value (McKee's beat as action-reaction exchange, scene as turn in a character's life, sequence of two to five scenes, act as extreme change).
- **Scales:** All, as a ladder.
- **Why it matters here:** It answers "what is a unit at each scale and what makes it complete?" in a way a procedural engine can check: a scene must turn a value, a sequence must build to a bigger turn, an act must produce an extreme change. This gives the system testable criteria for deciding that a hook has become a scene, a scene a sequence, and so on.
- **Key sources:**
  - McKee, *Story* (ReganBooks, 1997) [bk].
  - Summary of McKee's definitions: https://www.shortform.com/blog/robert-mckee-story-structure/ [sec]
  - Wikipedia, "Beat (filmmaking)," for the looser industry sense of "beat": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beat_(filmmaking) [sec]
- **Instance:** In McKee's terms a scene in which nothing changes on any value is a non-event, so a "street encounter" in which the player simply walks past and nothing shifts is a beat, not a scene.
- **Principles:**
  1. Each scale can be checked by whether a named value changed: a unit that changes no tracked value should not be offered as a scene. Test: log value deltas per unit; non-events are the filler.
  2. Units nest: a scene's turn should feed its sequence's build. Test: a sequence's final turn is larger than each scene's.
  3. "Beat" means different things in different crafts (McKee's exchange versus the industry's "moment"); the engine's vocabulary must pick one. Test: a glossary used consistently across the data model.
- **Failure modes:** Treating the hierarchy as a template instead of a check; five-scene sequences forced onto content that wants one; ambiguity about "beat."
- **Relevance:** Core as a scaffold. Confidence high.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 5. Episodic television: A/B/C plots, runners and the story circle
- **Definition:** A weekly unit built from several interleaved plots of different weight: A (main, most scenes), B (parallel, often lead characters' personal lives), C (a light runner), with each episode self-contained enough to be watched alone.
- **Scales:** Gig (episode), arc.
- **Why it matters here:** This is the practical form of multi-thread management at one-hour scale. It says how to braid a short job (A) with a relationship thread (B) and a cheap, funny or atmospheric runner (C), and that C plots are the usual place where the next B or A plot is seeded.
- **Key sources:**
  - Creative Screenwriting, "Creating A, B and C storylines for TV series": https://creativescreenwriting.com/creating-a-b-and-c-storylines-for-tv-series [sec]
  - The Conversation, on ABC's *The Heights* as a worked example of A/B/C: https://theconversation.com/inside-the-story-the-abc-of-screenwriting-as-demonstrated-by-abcs-the-heights-115854 [sec]
  - Dan Harmon's eight-step story circle (summarised at https://swipefile.com/dan-harmons-8-step-story-circle) as an episode skeleton [sec]
  - List of *Leverage* episodes as a heist-of-the-week example: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Leverage_episodes [sec]
- **Instance:** A fixer gig (A), a recurring contact's worry about a sibling (B), and a running joke about a vending machine that keeps charging V (C), where the sibling thread later becomes an A.
- **Principles:**
  1. An hour of play holds at most three active threads at three distinct weights; a fourth is read as noise. Test: threads shown per hour versus recall of each.
  2. C-weight material that gets a strong audience reaction is the cheapest promotion candidate. Test: promoted threads originate disproportionately from runners.
  3. Every episode is enterable alone. Test: a player new to the thread understands the A plot without prior context.
- **Failure modes:** B plot that never touches A; formulaic cold-open-and-twist that becomes predictable; stories that all follow the same circle and therefore feel the same.
- **Relevance:** Core. Confidence medium-high. Sources are craft guides, not scholarship.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 6. Sitcom status quo and the reset button
- **Definition:** A format that returns the world to a baseline at the end of each episode, so consequences are local and the cast stays stable.
- **Scales:** Gig, arc (as a contrast).
- **Why it matters here:** The system must be consequential. The reset format shows the cost of the opposite choice: it makes each gig enterable alone, but makes characters static. The practical question is which consequences persist, and the continuum from episodic to serialized gives language for setting that dial per thread.
- **Key sources:**
  - Pajiba, "Why sitcom finales are so hard to pull off," on the serialized/episodic continuum: https://www.pajiba.com/think_pieces/why-sitcom-finales-are-so-hard-to-pull-off.php [sec]
  - Reactor, "In praise of Star Trek: The Next Generation's infamous reset button": https://reactormag.com/in-praise-of-star-trek-the-next-generations-infamous-reset-button [sec]
  - The Believer, "Whose time is it, anyway": https://www.thebeliever.net/logger/is-that-all-there-is-whose-time-is-it-anyway/ [sec]
  - Mittell, *Complex TV* (below) for the serialized pole.
- **Instance:** *TNG*'s reset let viewers join at any point, at the price of characters who rarely carried the last episode's damage [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. Reset trades consequence for enterability, so set the persistence level per thread: lasting for relationship and reputation facts, resettable for the incidental (a damaged car). Test: players can say which things from last week still hold.
  2. A world that resets everything reads as hollow after a few repeats (the "amnesiac" criticism). Test: count callbacks to prior gigs per hour.
  3. Some persistence can be cosmetic: a mark on a wall counts as consequence. Test: players notice traces without a mechanical change.
- **Failure modes:** Resetting what the player cares about; persisting so much that new players cannot enter; inconsistent rules so nobody knows what carries over.
- **Relevance:** Supporting. Confidence medium. Sources are essays, not studies.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 7. Complex television and long-form serial narrative
- **Definition:** Series that combine episodic closure with cumulative, character-driven arcs across seasons, studied by Mittell as a "poetics" with distinctive "narrative special effects."
- **Scales:** Arc, season.
- **Why it matters here:** It is the best-theorised account of how an audience holds many threads across weeks, with the viewer's comprehension work (memory, forensic fandom) as part of the design. It also documents how serial shows pace revelation against episode-level satisfaction.
- **Key sources:**
  - Mittell, *Complex TV: The Poetics of Contemporary Television Storytelling* (NYU Press, 2015); catalogue page at https://muse.jhu.edu/book/76248 [sec]
  - Scalar edition with examples: https://scalar.usc.edu/works/complex-television/ [sec]
  - Review of the book: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/619525/pdf [sec]
- **Instance:** *The Wire*, *Lost*, *The Sopranos* and *Breaking Bad* as case studies [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. Season-scale satisfaction depends on audience memory, so the system must track what the player has noticed, not only what happened. Test: callbacks tied to noticed facts land better than callbacks tied to unnoticed ones.
  2. Episode-level closure and season-level accumulation can coexist. Test: each gig stands alone and also advances a named arc.
  3. The audience reads "narrative special effects" (a formal flourish that displays the writer's craft) as a reward when the story's mechanism is visible. Test: players mention such moments unprompted.
- **Failure modes:** Mystery boxes without payoff; arcs that outrun the audience's memory; late-season retcons.
- **Relevance:** Core for arc and season. Confidence medium; I did not read the book.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 8. Soap opera and serial narrative: many threads, no ending
- **Definition:** Open-ended serials in which storylines overlap, rarely resolve in sync, and are managed by a head writer who decides which characters are front-burner and which rest.
- **Scales:** Arc, season (and beyond).
- **Why it matters here:** This is the long-standing answer to the exact worry in the brief: how to keep many threads alive without overload, and how to retire them. Soaps solve it by ensemble rotation, long gestation, and overlapping beginnings and endings so that no single thread dominates.
- **Key sources:**
  - Allen (ed.), *To Be Continued... Soap Operas Around the World* (Routledge, 1995), including Roger Hagedorn, "Doubtless to be continued: a brief history of serial narrative": https://www.routledge.com/To-Be-Continued-Soap-Operas-Around-the-World/Allen/p/book/9780415110068 [sec]
  - Hobson, *Crossroads: The Drama of a Soap Opera* (Methuen, 1982) [bk].
  - Wikipedia, "Head writer," for who plans long-term story: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_writer [sec]
  - Fan-board evidence of front/back-burner practice (weak): https://boards.soapoperanetwork.com/topic/25001-lorraine-broderick/ [sec]
- **Instance:** Days of Our Lives' 2015 head-writer change drew fan complaints that newcomers had taken over from veterans, a visible illustration of the cast-balance failure [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. A rotation of front- and back-burner threads (a few at full weight, the rest simmering) keeps an ensemble alive without overload. Test: in a long play session, at most N threads are foregrounded, and each cast member returns within a bounded interval.
  2. Threads overlap at their joins: a new one starts before an old one ends. Test: there is no hour with zero live arcs and none with a dozen.
  3. Retirement is gradual (fade to back-burner, then a closing beat), not abrupt. Test: retired threads receive a closing trace.
- **Failure modes:** A new writer abandoning the inherited cast; favouritism toward a few characters; perpetual suspension with no payoff.
- **Relevance:** Core for thread management. Confidence medium. The practitioner sources are fan-level, so the practice is attested but not documented in a rigorous form.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 9. Comics: panel time, closure and the long run
- **Definition:** Two linked bodies of practice: McCloud's account of how readers fill the gutter and how panel size sets duration, and the long-run serial craft of superhero writers such as Claremont.
- **Scales:** Glance (panel), arc, season (run).
- **Why it matters here:** McCloud explains how a few frames can imply a long interval, which matches how the system can show a before and an after and let the player infer the middle. Claremont's run shows seeding subplots for years, accepting that many are never resolved, and using them as insurance when the main story slows.
- **Key sources:**
  - McCloud, *Understanding Comics* (Tundra, 1993), chapters 3 and 4 [bk].
  - Hooded Utilitarian, tag on closure (critiques of McCloud): https://hoodedutilitarian.com/tag/closure [sec]
  - The Conversation, on Claremont's influence on long-form TV: https://theconversation.com/how-an-x-men-writer-inspired-binge-worthy-character-driven-tv-from-buffy-to-game-of-thrones-110764 [sec]
  - Tom Ewing's review (via Goodreads) on subplots as insurance: https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/30733105 [sec]
- **Instance:** A fan wiki claims Claremont never followed through on three quarters of his subplots, partly because editors vetoed them or he moved books [sec]. Weak source, but the pattern is consistent across reviews.
- **Principles:**
  1. The gap between two sightings carries meaning; a longer or more visible gap implies more elapsed time and change. Test: players estimate elapsed in-world time from the gap and the estimate matches the intended one.
  2. Unresolved subplots are an asset if enough threads are alive that something is always ready, but they become debt if they pile up without payoff. Test: track the ratio of opened to closed threads and player complaints about dangling ones.
  3. Many subplots are never paid off, and a reader tolerates this if the main thread pays. Test: ask players which dangling threads they mind.
- **Failure modes:** Dropped threads that the player cared about; retcons; long run that stalls on its own continuity.
- **Relevance:** Supporting. Confidence medium-low on the Claremont factual claims.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 10. Radio and audio drama
- **Definition:** Drama carried entirely by voice, sound and silence, with dialogue-heavy exposition and sound as set and action.
- **Scales:** Glance, encounter, scene.
- **Why it matters here:** Much of an open-world city is heard before it is seen: radio chatter, a neighbour's arguing, a scanner call. Radio drama is the craft of getting a whole situation across with only the ear, with economical cast, distinct voices and soundscape cues for place and time.
- **Key sources:**
  - Beck, "Listening to radio drama" (eContact 5.3): https://econtact.ca/5_3/beck_listening.html [sec]
  - TCK Publishing, "Radio drama": https://tckpublishing.com/radio-drama/ (weak) [sec]
  - Specific sources on *The Archers* and Whitehead's sound-only work were not found [sec].
- **Instance:** A fixer's phone call overheard through a door: two voices, one distance cue, a clipped sentence, and the player infers the job.
- **Principles:**
  1. Audio-only scenes should carry the premise in the first line and place cues; a listener who joins mid-line should still get the situation. Test: players who hear only half a scene can state the premise.
  2. Few, distinct voices work better than many similar ones. Test: voice recognition in an overheard exchange.
  3. A voice heard before it is seen can be the start of a thread. Test: players later recognise the same voice.
- **Failure modes:** Exposition by announcement; indistinct voices; audio that cannot be located in space.
- **Relevance:** Supporting. Confidence medium-low; sources are introductory.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 11. Promise, progress and payoff (and setup and payoff craft)
- **Definition:** Sanderson's account of plot: early promises set expectations, progress shows movement toward them, payoff gives a surprising but fulfilling answer. Chekhov's gun is the same idea at object level.
- **Scales:** All, nested (scene promises inside book promises).
- **Why it matters here:** A throwaway line that pays off later is a promise the system makes. This gives a precise vocabulary for tracking them: every hook is a promise with a type (tone, character, plot, structure), progress must be visible, and payoff must be surprising yet fulfilling. It also supports a "no orphaned promises" check.
- **Key sources:**
  - Sanderson, 2025 plot lecture write-up: https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/brandon-sandersons-2025-guide-to-plot-lecture-2 (read this session)
  - Odyssey Workshop, "Brandon Sanderson on story promise, progress and payoff": https://www.odysseyworkshop.org/?p=19170 [sec]
  - Bearblog, "Storytelling lessons: Brandon Sanderson's promises": https://shanahan.bearblog.dev/storytelling-lessons-brandon-sandersons-promises/ [sec]
- **Instance:** Sanderson's own Wheel of Time example in the lecture page: a prologue promising a confrontation that the series pays off across many books.
- **Principles:**
  1. Progress is the part that sustains attention and must be visible between promise and payoff. Test: threads with visible intermediate steps keep players engaged longer than those with a gap.
  2. Small problems add up to the large one ("little p" feeding "big P"), so nested promises can be built from small ones. Test: arcs assembled from gig-sized pieces are read as one story.
  3. A payoff is judged on being both surprising and fulfilling. Test: playtest reactions separate "saw it coming" from "came from nowhere."
- **Failure modes:** Unpaid promises; payoffs that need knowledge the player lacks; chekhov overuse making every object a trap.
- **Relevance:** Core. Confidence high on the framework as a design tool, medium as a theory (lecture-based).
- **Surprise:** No.

## 12. Escalation and breakout practice
- **Definition:** How serials promote a minor element into a major one: the breakout character who outgrows a guest role, the runner that becomes a B plot, the background figure who is brought forward by audience response.
- **Scales:** Arc, season.
- **Why it matters here:** This is the question the brief asks most directly. The practice is mostly reactive, driven by audience response rather than a plan, which maps onto a system that can observe what the player engages with and promote accordingly, with the safeguards that serials learned the hard way.
- **Key sources:**
  - Wikipedia, "List of breakout characters": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_breakout_characters [sec]
  - TV Tropes, "Breakout Character" (live-action): https://www.tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/BreakoutCharacter/LiveActionTV [sec]
  - Fanlore, "Breakout Character": https://www.fanlore.org/wiki/Breakout_Character [sec]
  - Creative Screenwriting, noting that C stories sometimes grow into B or A plots [sec]
- **Instance:** Fonzie in *Happy Days* is the classic case; Dylan McKay in *Beverly Hills, 90210* is cited as a character who was absent from the pilot and became a favourite [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. Promote on engagement, not on intent: threads the player returns to unprompted are the candidates. Test: promotion decisions correlate with measured returns, not authoring order.
  2. Promotion needs a ladder with gates (seen, recurring, featured, central), each costing more authored or generated depth. Test: every promoted element has met the next rung's prerequisites.
  3. A promoted element should not displace the original focus: overpromotion hurts the show ("jump the shark"). Test: the player's original interests keep screen share.
- **Failure modes:** Overpromotion; promoting what a vocal minority liked; promotion without depth so the element is thin when central.
- **Relevance:** Core. Confidence medium. Evidence is largely trope-level, not systematic.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 13. Tabletop fronts and countdown clocks
- **Definition:** GM-side tools in Apocalypse World, Dungeon World and Blades in the Dark that track threats as dangers with portents and a doom, or as clocks that tick when triggered by events or time, so the world advances off-screen.
- **Scales:** Arc, campaign.
- **Why it matters here:** The system must make content feel caused and consequential even when the player did nothing. Fronts and clocks are the cheapest known mechanism: a threat advances by visible steps, the steps are warnings the player can read, and the doom is a stake the player can act against. A campaign front unifies the whole run, adventure fronts handle the local piece.
- **Key sources:**
  - Koebel and LaTorra, *Dungeon World* (Sage Kobold Productions, 2012) [bk]; Roll20 compendium: https://roll20.net/compendium/dw/Fronts [sec]
  - Baker, *Apocalypse World* (Lumpley Games, 2010) [bk]
  - Harper, *Blades in the Dark* (One Seven Design, 2017) [bk]; Roll20 compendium: https://roll20.net/compendium/BITD/Progress%20clocks [sec]
  - Sly Flourish, "Fronts in D&D": https://slyflourish.com/fronts_in_dnd.html [sec]
- **Instance:** A corporate faction's campaign front with four grim portents (a quiet buyout, a rival fixer found dead, a district blackout, an enforced curfew), advancing whether or not V attends.
- **Principles:**
  1. A threat that advances in discrete, visible steps is legible and fair. Test: players can name the next step and the trigger.
  2. Impending doom should not be world-ending, or the player is forced into it (one advisor's rule). Test: the doom is something the player can lose to without the game ending.
  3. Clocks of 4, 6 or 8 segments are enough for most threats; the count controls pacing. Test: playtests find 4 too abrupt, 8 too slow for specific thread types.
- **Failure modes:** Off-screen advance that the player never sees; a doom so large it overrides agency; too many fronts at once.
- **Relevance:** Core. Confidence high.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 14. Node-based scenario design and the Three Clue Rule
- **Definition:** A scenario structured around nodes of information that point to other nodes, with at least three clues for any intended conclusion, so progress survives missed or misread clues.
- **Scales:** Gig, arc.
- **Why it matters here:** An hour-scale investigation in a world where the player may ignore half the content needs redundancy. The rule also says how to avoid soft-locks: when the system proposes a gig and the player takes an unexpected route, a node structure still has somewhere to go.
- **Key sources:**
  - Alexander, "Node-based scenario design," *The Alexandrian*: https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/8122/roleplaying-games/node-based-scenario-design-collectors-edition [sec]
  - Alexander, "The Three Clue Rule": https://thealexandrian.net/?p=46236 [sec]
  - Short, "Beyond Branching," which notes QBN handles "find three clues in any order" better than branching trees (read this session): https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/
- **Instance:** A missing-person gig with a witness, a camera log and a pawn-shop receipt, any two of which locate the person.
- **Principles:**
  1. For every conclusion the story needs, offer at least three independent clues. Test: a player who misses any one still reaches the conclusion.
  2. With three clues and three conclusions the player almost always reaches at least one, which keeps the structure live. Test: simulated players with random clue loss still advance.
  3. Information flows between nodes, not along a line. Test: any node can be entered from at least two others.
- **Failure modes:** Clue chains that end if one link is missed; clues that are redundant but identical; players overwhelmed by too many nodes.
- **Relevance:** Core for gig-scale investigation. Confidence high.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 15. West Marches and sandbox campaign play
- **Definition:** A campaign style with no overarching plot, only an environment; a large pool of players self-organise into rotating groups who choose where to go, with a shared record of what was found.
- **Scales:** Campaign.
- **Why it matters here:** It is the clearest model for a system where the player is never controlled. Stories emerge from the player's choices of where to go, and the GM's job becomes keeping the environment rich, mapped and consequential. It sets the baseline: threads are offered, not pushed.
- **Key sources:**
  - Robbins, "West Marches: Mapping the Players," *Ars Ludi* (2007), at arsludi.lamemage.com [bk, not fetched]
  - World Anvil, "What is Westmarch?": https://www.worldanvil.com/w/verdraxis-chaosdraco/a/what-is-westmarch3F-article [sec]
  - Czepeku, "What is a West Marches campaign?": https://www.czepeku.com/blog/what-is-a-west-marches-campaign [sec]
  - Penny Arcade Automata, "D&D sandbox": https://automata.penny-arcade.com/news/post/2010/01/06/dnd-sandbox [sec]
- **Instance:** A wilderness map with a base town, key locations occupied by factions, and a shared record that later groups read before choosing a destination.
- **Principles:**
  1. Cross-session continuity can come from a shared environment record rather than a plot. Test: players treat past discoveries as present facts.
  2. Offering many places and leaving the choice to the player produces pace set by the player. Test: pacing complaints fall when choices are many.
  3. Reputation and rumours can transmit threads between groups. Test: players follow threads they heard of, not only lived.
- **Failure modes:** Nothing to find; no signal about danger; too few threads so the sandbox feels empty.
- **Relevance:** Core as an anti-railroad baseline. Confidence medium (secondary sources only).
- **Surprise:** No.

## 16. Storylets, quality-based and salience-based narrative
- **Definition:** Content as modular chunks with preconditions, selected by state (qualities) or by best fit (salience), so stories assemble from small parts.
- **Scales:** All, from line to arc.
- **Why it matters here:** This is the structural idea closest to the system: authored pieces, a selection rule, and state that carries between them. Short's account says QBN lets many short arcs slot together and lets later content call back to earlier content, while salience-based selection lets authors add specific content over broad defaults with few side effects.
- **Key sources:**
  - Short, "Beyond Branching: Quality-Based, Salience-Based, and Waypoint Narrative Structures," 12 April 2016: https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/ (read this session)
  - Kreminski and Wardrip-Fruin, "Sketching a Map of the Storylets Design Space," ICIDS 2018 [sec]; summary: https://emshort.blog/2019/01/06/kreminski-on-storylets/
  - Fallen London wiki, "Storylets": https://fallenlondon.wiki/wiki/Storylets [sec]
- **Instance:** Fallen London's storylets gated by qualities (read this session via Short), and Left 4 Dead's dialogue system as a salience example, per Short.
- **Principles:**
  1. Many short arcs can slot together if the state they read is shared and named. Test: any two arcs touching the same quality interact without special-casing.
  2. Salience selection needs defaults and specifics: broad content fills gaps, specific content wins when it applies. Test: removing specific content degrades to a plausible default.
  3. Salience can pick dramatically weak endings, so arc-shape variables are needed. Test: ending quality does not depend on chance matches alone.
- **Failure modes:** Authors tracking arc progress by hand; big libraries overwhelming players with options; scores found only by trial and error.
- **Relevance:** Core. Confidence high (primary source read).
- **Surprise:** No.

## 17. Drama management and interactive drama (Façade, Riedl and Bulitko)
- **Definition:** A drama manager steers an interactive story to fit a dramatic aesthetic; in Façade, many authored story beats sit in a pool and are sequenced so that tension follows an arc.
- **Scales:** Scene, vignette, short arc.
- **Why it matters here:** The system is, in part, a drama manager that must observe and offer, never control. The research record shows what "steering" costs in authored content and what it does to player agency, which is the central tension of the brief.
- **Key sources:**
  - Mateas and Stern, "Structuring Content in the Façade Interactive Drama Architecture," AIIDE 2005: https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/18722 [sec]
  - Riedl and Bulitko, "Interactive Narrative: An Intelligent Systems Approach," *AI Magazine* 34(1), 2013: https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/aimagazine/article/view/2449 [sec]
  - Mateas and Stern, GDC 2003 paper: https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~isbell/classes/reading/papers/MateasSternGDC03.pdf [sec]
- **Instance:** Façade sequences beats from a large pool to raise and lower tension against an Aristotelian arc [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. A beat pool plus a sequencer gives variable runs with arc shape. Test: different runs show different beat orders yet comparable tension curves.
  2. The manager can bias what is offered without compelling the player. Test: players can decline offered beats without breaking the arc.
  3. The authoring burden grows with the pool; budget it. Test: content per minute of play is tracked.
- **Failure modes:** Over-steering until the player feels managed; one fixed arc shape applied to everything; drama manager hidden so failures cannot be debugged.
- **Relevance:** Core for the observe-and-offer problem. Confidence medium (abstracts only).
- **Surprise:** No.

## 18. AI directors: pacing as a controlled variable (Left 4 Dead, RimWorld)
- **Definition:** A system that estimates the player's recent intensity and controls what arrives next, to produce rises and rests.
- **Scales:** Encounter, gig; slowly, arc.
- **Why it matters here:** It is the most mature evidence on pacing at the minute-to-hour scale, and it offers measurable parameters.
- **Key sources:**
  - Booth, "The AI Systems of Left 4 Dead," Valve (2009): https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf (summary read via https://www.readkong.com/page/the-ai-systems-of-left-4-dead-michael-booth-valve-9664541)
  - RimWorld wiki, "AI Storytellers": https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/AI_storyteller [sec]
  - Sylvester, *Designing Games* (O'Reilly, 2013), on the storyteller [bk, not checked].
- **Instance:** The Left 4 Dead director in its four phases: build up, sustain peak for 3 to 5 seconds, peak fade, and relax for 30 to 45 seconds or until the team moves toward the next safe room. It tracks an intensity per player, rising with damage and nearby kills, decaying with time except during active engagement. It changes how often peaks occur, not how hard each is. RimWorld's storytellers differ by cadence: one escalates then rests, one gives long peace, one is random [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. Pace is a function of a measured intensity with enforced rests. Test: intensity traces show rest phases of a minimum length.
  2. Entering a rest should wait for a natural break, not cut action short. Test: no relax phase begins mid-engagement.
  3. Different cadences give different personalities. Test: players can tell storyteller profiles apart.
- **Failure modes:** Intensity proxies that do not measure story engagement (damage is not drama); rests so long that play drags; the player learns the cadence and games it.
- **Relevance:** Supporting to core for pacing. Confidence high on L4D (read), medium on RimWorld (wiki).
- **Surprise:** No.

## 19. Simulation-driven emergent narrative and story sifting (Dwarf Fortress, CK3, James Ryan)
- **Definition:** Stories arise from simulated characters with names, histories and relationships, and are found afterward by recognising patterns in the event log ("story sifting").
- **Scales:** Glance to season, with the narrative formed retrospectively.
- **Why it matters here:** Content "caused" by the simulation is more credible than content invented for the player. Adams' approach also shows that characterisation for its own sake (details with no mechanical use) raises the story value of events, which is cheap to add to a city simulated at levels of detail.
- **Key sources:**
  - Adams, "Characterization and Emergent Narrative in Dwarf Fortress," in Short and Adams (eds.), *Procedural Storytelling in Game Design* (CRC Press, 2019): https://wileywiggins.com//documents/dorf/Adams - Characterization and Emergent Narrative in Dwarf F.pdf [sec]
  - Gamasutra Q&A with Adams: https://gamasutra.com/design/q-a-dissecting-the-development-of-i-dwarf-fortress-i-with-creator-tarn-adams [sec]
  - Ryan, *Curating Simulated Storyworlds* (PhD thesis, UC Santa Cruz, 2018), summarised at https://emshort.blog/2019/05/21/curating-simulated-storyworlds-james-ryan/ [sec]
  - Paradox, *Crusader Kings II* Dev Diary 6, on rare narrative events and chains: https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/crusader-kings-ii-dev-diary-6-events.525900/ [sec]
- **Instance:** In a 2026 interview Adams recounts a dog interrupting a meeting of dwarven barristers and biting someone [sec]; a small cause, a vivid story.
- **Principles:**
  1. Add a simulation layer if playing it yields "that was cool" stories (Adams' own test, paraphrased). Test: playtesters tell stories from the layer unprompted.
  2. Personal details that have no mechanical use still raise story yield. Test: events involving named characters are retold more than those involving anonymous ones.
  3. A simulation produces facts; a separate sifting step selects which become offers. Test: the offer rate is a small fraction of event volume.
- **Failure modes:** Stories only the player imagines; sifting that surfaces trivial events; sim detail that never reaches the player.
- **Relevance:** Core. Confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 20. Nemesis-style promotion of background agents
- **Definition:** A system that tracks minor NPCs who happen to interact with the player and promotes the notable ones into recurring named characters with memory of the player.
- **Scales:** Encounter to arc.
- **Why it matters here:** It is the only major shipped example of automatic escalation from background to central, matching the brief's request.
- **Key sources:**
  - Wikipedia, *Middle-earth: Shadow of Mordor*: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle-earth:_Shadow_of_Mordor [sec]
  - GamesBeat, "Shadow of Mordor's Nemesis system draws from Burnout, football, and an architecture book": https://gamesbeat.com/shadow-of-mordors-nemesis-system-draws-from-burnout-football-and-an-architecture-book/ [sec]
  - Game Informer, "Opening up Mordor" (de Plater): https://www.gameinformer.com/games/middle-earth_shadow_of_mordor/b/xboxone/archive/2014/05/22/opening-up-mordor.aspx [sec]
  - Patent Arcade on U.S. Patent 10,926,179: https://www.patentarcade.com/2021/02/warner-brothers-granted-patent-for-nemesis-system-from-middle-earth-video-games.html [sec]
- **Instance:** An orc who kills the player is tracked, promoted to captain, and remembers the encounter later [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. Promotion triggers on notable outcomes (killing the player, surviving the player). Test: promoted agents all have a recorded notable event.
  2. Remembered history makes later meetings feel caused. Test: promoted agents' dialogue references the earlier event.
  3. De Plater's "anchors of gameplay": the system cannot fill every story point in an open world, so it gives players a few anchors to latch onto [sec]. Test: promoted agents are a small share of all agents.
- **Failure modes:** Same-shaped rivalries everywhere; promotion of the trivial; **legal risk:** Warner Bros was granted a patent covering the hierarchy and vendetta mechanisms in February 2021 [sec]. The project's own rule against building on patented techniques applies. I did not read the claims, so the scope is unconfirmed; a design must be checked against the claims or deliberately differ.
- **Relevance:** Core as the escalation precedent. Confidence medium-high on the pattern, low on the legal scope.
- **Surprise:** Yes. The most-cited escalation mechanism in games is encumbered by a live patent that runs to about 2035 [sec].

## 21. Radiant procedural quests (Skyrim)
- **Definition:** Template quests that fill slots (target, location, giver) using the player's state and history, extended from the Radiant AI system.
- **Scales:** Gig.
- **Why it matters here:** It is the best-known case of a procedural engine generating gig-scale content in a big RPG, and its reception is evidence about what players dislike.
- **Key sources:**
  - Game Informer, "The technology behind The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim": https://gameinformer.com/games/the_elder_scrolls_v_skyrim/b/pc/archive/2011/01/17/the-technology-behind-the-elder-scrolls-v-skyrim.aspx [sec]
  - Wikipedia, "Radiant AI": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiant_AI [sec]
  - Instituto Superior Técnico thesis, "Procedural Quest Generation - Improving Skyrim's Radiant Story": https://labjogos.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/en/thesis/procedural-quest-generation-improving-skyrim-s-radiant-story [sec]
- **Instance:** A bounty or retrieval quest tailored to where the player is and what they have done; the thesis reports community dislike and proposes chaining generated quests for coherence [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. Slot-filled quests read as filler unless they carry a hook tied to the player's history. Test: players rate the same template higher with a history-linked hook.
  2. Chaining generated quests raises coherence (the thesis's proposal). Test: chained sets are rated as more connected than isolated ones.
  3. Variation that is only in names is invisible; players notice variation in structure and stakes. Test: players distinguish templates by structure.
- **Failure modes:** Endless repeats; rewards decoupled from effort; player recognises the template quickly.
- **Relevance:** Supporting, as a cautionary case. Confidence medium.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 22. Reactive repeatable writing (Hades)
- **Definition:** A large body of short authored lines that react to the player's history, so that a repeated loop still advances relationships and story.
- **Scales:** Glance, encounter, with continuity across arcs.
- **Why it matters here:** The player will meet the same NPCs many times. Hades shows that the same short loop can keep producing story if every encounter picks something new and remembers what has been said.
- **Key sources:**
  - Kasavin and Korb, "Breathing Life into Greek Myth: The Dialogue of Hades," GDC 2021: https://gdcvault.com/play/1026975/Breathing-Life-into-Greek-Myth [sec]
  - Gamasutra, "Roguelikes and narrative design with Hades creative director Greg Kasavin": https://GamaSutra.com/design/roguelikes-and-narrative-design-with-i-hades-i-creative-director-greg-kasavin [sec]
- **Instance:** Kasavin's stated goal was that a boss remembers earlier meetings, giving narrative continuity inside a loop [sec]. I did not find the priority mechanism itself.
- **Principles:**
  1. Remembered history in a short line makes a repeated encounter feel new. Test: players report characters as aware of them.
  2. Priority between candidate lines must favour unseen over seen. Test: line repetition rate over a fixed play length.
  3. Early access feedback improved the narrative [sec]. Test: content is shipped in small testable slices.
- **Failure modes:** Lines that react to facts the player does not know they caused; running out of lines; reactivity that reads as surveillance.
- **Relevance:** Supporting. Confidence medium-low (talk behind a paywall; mechanism not verified).
- **Surprise:** No.

## 23. Live-service and seasonal storytelling (Guild Wars 2 Living World)
- **Definition:** Story delivered in episodes grouped into seasons across months, with a persistent world that changes and a journal for replays.
- **Scales:** Arc, season.
- **Why it matters here:** It documents a season-scale cadence in a shared world, including lessons about building momentum and keeping new players able to join.
- **Key sources:**
  - ArenaNet, "Designing the Living World": https://guildwars2.com/en/news/designing-the-living-world [sec]
  - PCGamesN, on Season 1 as "fire and forget": https://www.pcgamesn.com/guild-wars-2/season-1 [sec]
  - ArenaNet, "Introducing the Story Journal": https://www.guildwars2.com/en/news/introducing-the-story-journal/ [sec]
  - Mein-MMO, "How Guild Wars 2 learns to tell stories": https://mein-mmo.de/en/how-guild-wars-2-learns-to-tell-stories,12871 [sec]
- **Instance:** Season 1 events lasted a few weeks, were overwritten when the next shipped, and later seasons added a replay journal and let NPCs assume the player had completed earlier seasons [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. A story spanning months needs time to build momentum, so single-beat events are not enough. Test: engagement rises across episodes within a season.
  2. A replay and recap mechanism lets late arrivals catch up. Test: new players can start mid-season.
  3. Assume continuity rather than check it when checking would block (NPCs treat the player as having done the earlier story). Test: no mid-season NPC contradicts the player's history.
- **Failure modes:** Ephemeral content lost; lore gaps for new players; cadence driven by the release calendar rather than the story.
- **Relevance:** Peripheral to supporting. Confidence medium (older developer posts).
- **Surprise:** No.

## 24. Open-world side-content ladders (Cyberpunk 2077, Red Dead Redemption 2)
- **Definition:** The way existing open-world games scale content by effort and duration: throwaway street activities, gigs, side jobs, and main story, plus random encounters in the world.
- **Scales:** Glance to arc.
- **Why it matters here:** These are the system's host and neighbours. The game's own tiers show what players already accept at each scale and where they complain, and set the baseline against which "varied in ways players notice" is judged.
- **Key sources:**
  - Common Sense Gamer, "Cyberpunk 2077 side missions guide," listing side jobs, gigs, NCPD scanner hustles and rides: https://commonsensegamer.com/cyberpunk-2077-side-missions-guide/ [sec]
  - TrueAchievements walkthrough, on hustle map visibility after patch 1.5: https://trueachievements.com/game/Cyberpunk-2077/walkthrough/5 [sec]
  - SegmentNext, "Red Dead Redemption 2: random encounters guide": https://segmentnext.com/red-dead-redemption-2-chance-encounters-guide/ [sec]
  - GameStar, on Rockstar testing and rejecting procedural companion missions: https://www.gamestar.de/artikel/red-dead-redemption-2-rockstar-experimentierte-mit-generierten-missionen-entschied-sich-aber-dagegen,3329337.html [sec]
- **Instance:** One guide counts 156 NCPD scanner hustles; gigs arrive in batches of one to three and are fixer-gated (player-reported) [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. Each tier has a different contract on duration and reward. Test: players correctly predict the length of a job from its presentation.
  2. Short, repeatable street content wears out quickly: RDR2 players complain that encounters repeat. Test: repetition complaints rise with exposure count [sec].
  3. Rockstar judged generated missions unable to reach its desired quality and chose control [sec]. Test: any generated gig is checked against an authored one in blind comparison.
- **Failure modes:** Reuse that players identify; tiers that blur; generated content that cannot match authored quality at the same tier.
- **Relevance:** Core as a baseline, with sources that are weak (player guides). Confidence medium.
- **Surprise:** No.

## 25. Open loops and the psychology of unfinished threads
- **Definition:** The claim that unfinished tasks are remembered better (Zeigarnik) and that people tend to resume interrupted tasks (Ovsiankina).
- **Scales:** Arc, season.
- **Why it matters here:** Serial design leans on the idea that an unresolved thread stays in the audience's mind. If that is wrong, a thread-retention strategy needs another basis, and the system should not assume dangling threads keep themselves alive.
- **Key sources:**
  - Ghibellini and Meier, 2025 meta-analysis, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 12: https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html [sec]
  - Wikipedia, "Zeigarnik effect": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect [sec]
- **Instance:** The meta-analysis, as summarised, found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks but a general tendency to resume interrupted ones [sec]. A secondary post gives d = 0.15; I could not verify that figure [sec].
- **Principles:**
  1. Resumption, not memory, is the reliable effect: players tend to return to something interrupted. Test: interrupted gigs are resumed at a higher rate than abandoned-by-choice ones.
  2. Do not rely on recall of dangling threads; add cues. Test: cued threads are resumed more than uncued ones.
  3. The effect depends on involvement and situation. Test: engaged players resume more.
- **Failure modes:** Using the Zeigarnik effect as a justification for cliffhangers; assuming forgotten threads are tolerated.
- **Relevance:** Supporting. Confidence medium; based on a search summary of one meta-analysis.
- **Surprise:** Yes. The popular "unfinished things stick in the mind" rule lacks universal support, while the less famous resumption tendency holds.

## Considered and rejected

- Dan Harmon's story circle as a standalone entry: folded into episodic TV (entry 5); it is a single-episode skeleton.
- Hero's Journey (Campbell, Vogler): too generic to say anything specific at any scale; the circle is its useful reduction.
- Freytag's pyramid: single-arc shape, covered by McKee's hierarchy and drama management.
- Kishotenketsu (four-act without conflict): interesting for low-conflict vignettes, but I found no sources this session, so I left it out rather than cite it unseen.
- Janet Murray, *Hamlet on the Holodeck* (procedural authorship): relevant to the field but not to scale; confirmed only in outline.
- Visual novels and branching CYOA: branching explosion makes them poor models at any scale beyond a vignette.
- Opera and ballet (leitmotif): leitmotif as recurring cue is a technique, but covered by setup and payoff.
- Oral epic and folk-tale (formulaic composition): interesting for repeated formulae but no accessible scale-specific sources found.
- Stage improv: strong for scenes, but not about scale transitions; covered by Boal for audience handling.
- Dramatica and Truby's 22 steps: heavy template systems; not testable at the scales the brief names.
- Tabletop "Alexandrian" prep beyond nodes (e.g. campaign "Don't prep plots" posts): the search returned nothing for that title, so I did not cite it.
- The Sims and Ultima-style NPC schedules: relevant to the simulation, not to story scale.
- Pixar's story rules, Save the Cat: popular but they target the feature film, a single-scale form.

## What the entries add up to for the scale question

1. **Each scale has a different completion criterion.** A glance completes by implication (1), an encounter by being legible without the player (2), a vignette or gig by one effect (3) or a reached conclusion despite missing clues (14), an arc by a payoff of a visible promise (11), a season by memory and cadence (7, 23). The McKee ladder (4) gives the unit definitions; the others give each rung's check.
2. **Moving up a scale is promotion, and serials do it reactively.** Practice (12, 5, 20) is: observe engagement, promote on repeat attention, deepen only after the gate. The real risks are overpromotion, thin depth when central, and (for the best-known game mechanism) a patent.
3. **Moving down is compression by implication.** Seasons break into gigs (14), gigs into glances (1, 10) through clues, traces and overheard lines, which only work if backed by real facts (19).
4. **Thread overload is solved by ensemble rotation and overlap** (8, 5), not by capping total threads; retirement is gradual and leaves a trace.
5. **Pacing is different at each scale.** Seconds-to-minutes pacing is controllable with measured intensity and enforced rests (18); hours-to-weeks pacing is set by cadence, clocks and the player's own choice (13, 15, 23).
6. **Consequence is a per-thread dial** between full reset and permanent change (6); fronts and clocks make off-screen advance legible (13).
7. **Evidence quality is uneven.** Strongest here: Short (16), Booth's L4D (18), Sanderson (11). Weakest: soap and comics practice (8, 9), which are attested by fans and reviews more than by documented method.

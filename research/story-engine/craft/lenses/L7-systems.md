## 1. Agent-based social simulation and its validation

- **Definition:** Bottom-up models in which individual agents following local rules produce aggregate patterns, together with the methods for documenting, calibrating and testing them.
- **Why it matters here:** The system needs a defensible meaning of "plausible and cheap". These methods say how to decide a subsystem is good enough: reproduce several independent patterns, at more than one scale, and document it well enough that someone else can rebuild it. They bear directly on the individual-to-field coupling, since a field value and the individuals beneath it must tell the same story.
- **Key sources:**
  - Grimm, V., et al. (2020). "The ODD Protocol for Describing Agent-Based and Other Simulation Models: A Second Update to Improve Clarity, Replication, and Structural Realism." *JASSS* 23(2), 7. https://jasss.soc.surrey.ac.uk/23/2/7.html
  - Grimm, V., Revilla, E., Berger, U., Jeltsch, F., Mooij, W. M., Railsback, S. F., Thulke, H.-H., Weiner, J., Wiegand, T., DeAngelis, D. L. (2005). "Pattern-Oriented Modeling of Agent-Based Complex Systems: Lessons from Ecology." *Science* 310(5750), 987-991. https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70161783
  - Windrum, P., Fagiolo, G., Moneta, A. (2007). "Empirical Validation of Agent-Based Models: Alternatives and Prospects." *JASSS* 10(2), 8. https://www.jasss.org/10/2/8.html
  - Moss, S. (2008). "Alternative Approaches to the Empirical Validation of Agent-Based Models." *JASSS* 11(1), 5. https://jasss.soc.surrey.ac.uk/11/1/5.html
- **Instance:** Pattern-oriented modelling in ecology. A model built to fit one observed pattern leaves structure and parameters too loosely constrained, so the strategy is to require several patterns at once. `[sec]` The ODD update adds a rationale section and a fitness-for-purpose section, and argues the protocol can describe any simulation model, not only agent-based ones. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. A subsystem is accepted only if it reproduces at least three independent qualitative patterns at more than one scale. Testable: write the pattern checks first (for example "half of incidents fall on a small share of street segments", "foot traffic peaks at shift change", "a rumour reaches a bounded share of a district"), run many seeds, and fail the build when any pattern disappears. (Pattern-oriented modelling; the check list is my extension.)
  2. Every subsystem ships with a short structured spec (purpose, entities and state, process schedule, design concepts, and a fitness-for-purpose statement), and a second implementer working from the spec alone must reproduce the same patterns. (ODD; the reimplementation test is my extension.)
  3. For a game, validation is to the standard of plausibility to stakeholders, not prediction of data. Moss argues that empirical-validation techniques borrowed from econometrics hold only under narrower conditions than Windrum et al. claim, and that participatory ("companion") modelling avoids some of their problems. `[sec]` The extension: designers and playtesters are the stakeholders, so scripted plausibility reviews are a legitimate validation step.
- **Failure modes:** Matching patterns does not identify the mechanism that produced them, so two different rule sets can pass the same checks (my extension). Pattern lists are written by the people who built the model and tend to confirm it. Empirical-validation literature is built around economic and ecological data that we do not have for a fictional city.
- **Relevance:** Core as method; confidence high that it is useful, medium on how far the validation machinery transfers to a fictional setting.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 2. Routine activity theory

- **Definition:** Most crimes require, at one place and time, a likely offender, a suitable target and the absence of a capable guardian, so crime rates follow the rhythms of everyday life as much as the supply of motivated offenders.
- **Why it matters here:** It is the cheapest causal model of "where and when does something happen" and it runs on the same schedule layer the city already needs. It turns routine changes (a shift change, a curfew, a festival, a closed market) into story causes without any change in anyone's motives. At fine detail the three ingredients are literally agents co-present in a cell; at coarse detail they are three density fields multiplied.
- **Key sources:**
  - Cohen, L. E., Felson, M. (1979). "Social Change and Crime Rate Trends: A Routine Activity Approach." *American Sociological Review* 44(4), 588-608. DOI 10.2307/2094589.
  - Felson, M., Clarke, R. V. (1998). "Opportunity Makes the Thief: Practical Theory for Crime Prevention." Police Research Series Paper 98, UK Home Office. (from memory; not searched)
  - Clarke, R. V., Felson, M. (eds.) (1993). *Routine Activity and Rational Choice*. Advances in Criminological Theory, vol. 5. Transaction. (from memory; not searched)
- **Instance:** The original 1947-1974 US analysis, which tied crime-rate trends to the movement of activities away from households and to labour-force and household-structure change. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. An incident can only occur in a cell and time slot where all three ingredients are present. Testable: log every simulated incident and assert that offender, target and absent guardian were co-located in that slot.
  2. Hazard in a cell and slot scales with the product of offender density, target density and (1 minus guardian density). A routine shift with unchanged motivation must change the rate. Testable: move a block's guardians (shopkeepers closing early) and the cell's rate rises while no agent's drives change. (My extension of the theory's logic into a rate rule.)
  3. Macro-level change in routines (activity moving away from the home) shifts opportunity supply across a whole population. `[sec]` For this system: city-wide events such as a transit strike should move the hazard field wholesale.
- **Failure modes:** Offender motivation is taken as given, so the theory is silent on why anyone wants to offend; that has to come from other fields (blocked goals, debt and feuds: fields 9, 23 and 22). "Capable guardian" is slippery and varies by offence. The 1979 test was a national time series, so applying it at street level is an extension.
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence high.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 3. Crime pattern theory (the geometry of crime)

- **Definition:** Offenders find opportunities within the awareness space created by their own routine nodes (home, work, leisure), paths between them and the edges of areas they know, so crime clusters at nodes, along paths and on boundaries.
- **Why it matters here:** It gives the fine-detail layer a rule for where a particular individual will act, which keeps individual offenders local and traceable rather than teleporting to targets. It also explains why the same street gets repeated trouble and why boundaries between districts are story-rich.
- **Key sources:**
  - Brantingham, P. L., Brantingham, P. J. (1993). "Environment, Routine, and Situation: Toward a Pattern Theory of Crime." In Clarke and Felson (eds.), *Routine Activity and Rational Choice*, 259-294. (chapter details from memory; search confirmed the work and its concepts `[sec]`)
  - Brantingham, P. J., Brantingham, P. L. (1981). *Environmental Criminology*. Sage. (from memory; not searched)
  - Lynch, K. (1960). *The Image of the City*. MIT Press. (named in a search summary as the origin of the node/path/edge vocabulary `[sec]`)
  - Popcenter overview: https://www.popcenter.org/node/3652
- **Instance:** The Brantinghams' early Tallahassee burglary study, which found clustering where affluent areas bordered poorer ones. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Offences are mostly committed in the course of the offender's daily routine, not on special journeys. `[sec]` Rule: an offender agent samples offence locations from targets inside its own activity space (nodes plus paths) and weights unknown territory near zero. Testable: simulated offences should lie near the offender's routine nodes and routes.
  2. Crime concentrates where offenders' paths meet target-rich nodes and along district edges. `[sec]` Testable: in a generated city, offence density should peak at nodes and edges, not uniformly across area.
  3. Awareness space grows with life changes (a new job, a new partner, a move). (My extension.) Rule: a drift in activity space is a story trigger: an offender appearing in a district where they have no history is notable.
- **Failure modes:** Awareness space is usually modelled as static. The theory describes where, not why. In a car-based or transit-based city, paths are not walkable lines; in a vertical city, nodes and paths are three-dimensional and the framework gives no guidance.
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 4. Crime concentration and near-repeat victimisation

- **Definition:** A small share of places produces a large share of incidents, and an incident raises the short-term risk to nearby places and to the same target (self-excitation).
- **Why it matters here:** This is the coarse-level generator of where and when something happens and it produces episodic arcs for free. Seed one event and the hazard around it rises and decays, so follow-up events and retaliation chains occur without simulating every individual. It also tells the system how skewed the placement of incidents should be.
- **Key sources:**
  - Weisburd, D. (2015). "The Law of Crime Concentration and the Criminology of Place." *Criminology* 53(2). (volume and issue confirmed in secondary sources; the article itself was not retrieved `[sec]`)
  - Bowers, K. J., Johnson, S. D., Pease, K. (2004). "Prospective Hot-Spotting: The Future of Crime Mapping?" *British Journal of Criminology* 44, 641-658. DOI 10.1093/bjc/azh036.
  - Mohler, G. O., Short, M. B., Brantingham, P. J., Schoenberg, F. P., Tita, G. E. (2011). "Self-Exciting Point Process Modeling of Crime." *Journal of the American Statistical Association* 106(493), 100-108.
- **Instance:** Mohler et al.'s model of Los Angeles residential burglary, which splits the event rate into a background part that can vary in space and a self-exciting part that accounts for near-repeats and, for gang violence, retaliations. `[sec]` The idea is borrowed from seismology. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Incidents concentrate. Across studies the median is about half of crime on about 4.5% of street segments and a quarter on about 1.25%. `[sec]` Testable: the Lorenz curve of generated incidents over street segments should reach 50% at well under 10% of segments; if it is flatter, placement is too uniform.
  2. Each incident adds a decaying boost to hazard near it. In the UK burglary data, homes within about 400 m of a burgled home had elevated risk for up to about two months. `[sec]` Rule: hazard(x, t) = background(x) + sum over past incidents of a kernel decaying in distance and time. Testable: after a seeded incident, the number of further incidents within radius r and window w should exceed the background expectation by the fitted factor.
  3. For gang violence the self-exciting term represents retaliation. `[sec]` Rule: a violent incident between groups A and B boosts the hazard of the next incident between A and B (not elsewhere), with its own decay constant.
- **Failure modes:** The concentration is stable over years, so a purely self-exciting model gives drifting maps; the model needs a stable background (my extension). The radius and decay figures come from one country's burglary data and do not transfer to a fictional city; they are shapes to tune, not constants. Real systems of this kind are fed by reported incidents, which are not the same as incidents; a game that feeds forecasts back into patrol placement can lock in its own bias (my extension).
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence high on the mechanism, medium on parameter values.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 5. Displacement and diffusion of benefits

- **Definition:** What happens elsewhere when crime is suppressed in one place: it can move (displacement), it can fall in neighbouring places too (diffusion of benefits), or nothing can change.
- **Why it matters here:** Consequence is the product. When a player or a faction clears a block, the world has to respond plausibly, and the lazy answer (crime pops up next door) is not what the evidence mostly shows. This field supplies defensible odds for the response and a limit on how much of the original problem comes back.
- **Key sources:**
  - Guerette, R. T., Bowers, K. J. (2009). "Assessing the Extent of Crime Displacement and Diffusion of Benefits: A Review of Situational Crime Prevention Evaluations." *Criminology* 47(4), 1331-1368. DOI 10.1111/j.1745-9125.2009.00177.x.
  - Johnson, S. D., Guerette, R. T., Bowers, K. J. (2014). "Crime Displacement: What We Know, What We Don't Know, and What It Means for Crime Reduction." *Journal of Experimental Criminology* (title from memory; DOI prefix 10.1007/s11292-014-9209-4; volume and pages not confirmed).
  - Popcenter displacement tool: https://live-popcenter-ws2.ws.asu.edu/tools/displacement/9
- **Instance:** The Guerette and Bowers review of 102 evaluations with 574 observations: displacement appeared in 26% of observations and diffusion of benefits in 27%, and where displacement occurred it was usually smaller than the gain. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Displacement is the exception, not the rule. `[sec]` Rule: after local suppression, draw one of three outcomes with roughly 26% displacement, 27% diffusion, remainder no spillover. Testable: over many simulated interventions the outcome frequencies match the targets.
  2. Net effect is positive even when displacement occurs: displaced crime is smaller than the reduction. `[sec]` Rule: cap displaced volume below the suppressed volume. Testable: summed citywide incidence after suppression is below its pre-suppression level in nearly all runs.
  3. Where a market has a persistent supply and demand, spillover should be stronger (my extension, not from the review). Treat as a hypothesis to test with the illicit-economy field (field 9).
- **Failure modes:** The review covers situational prevention projects, mostly property and street offences; it does not obviously cover violent turf conflict or organised markets, where the logic differs (my extension). Displacement has several forms (place, time, tactic, target, offender, offence type) and the summary I saw reports mainly spatial effects. `[sec]`
- **Relevance:** Supporting to core; confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 6. Crime scripts and the procedural analysis of offending

- **Definition:** Breaking an offence into an ordered sequence of stages, with the actors, tools, places and decisions needed at each, borrowed from cognitive science's idea of a script.
- **Why it matters here:** A multi-hour heist is a script. Treating it as one connects the simulation's resources (people, access, equipment, information) to scenes: each stage has preconditions the world must satisfy, and each is a place where the story can branch, fail or be intercepted. It also tells a designer where a player's intervention can break the chain.
- **Key sources:**
  - Cornish, D. B. (1994). "The Procedural Analysis of Offending and Its Relevance for Situational Prevention." In R. V. Clarke (ed.), *Crime Prevention Studies*, vol. 3. Criminal Justice Press. PDF: https://popcenter.asu.edu/sites/default/files/library/CrimePrevention/Volume_03/06_cornish.pdf
  - Schank, R. C., Abelson, R. P. (1977). *Scripts, Plans, Goals and Understanding*. Lawrence Erlbaum. (from memory; search referred only to "Abelson and colleagues")
  - Dehghanniri, H., Borrion, H. "Crime Scripting: A Systematic Review." *European Journal of Criminology* (listed as in press in the record found; final volume, pages and DOI not confirmed). It covers over one hundred scripts published 1994 to 2018. Accepted manuscript: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10072755/ `[sec]`
- **Instance:** The scripting literature codes what offenders do before, during and after a crime, and records the actors, props and locations involved. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. An offence is a sequence of stages from preparation to exit, and each stage has required actors, props and places. `[sec]` Rule: a generated heist is valid only if every stage's requirements can be met from world state. Testable: remove any required prop or role and the generator must reroute or mark the heist infeasible.
  2. Prevention (and therefore player or opponent interference) works by raising effort or risk at one stage. `[sec]` Rule: a countermeasure attaches to a stage, not to the whole heist. Testable: every interference option maps to exactly one stage.
  3. Failure at a stage should change the next stage's preconditions (injury, alarm, lost time) rather than end the heist. (My extension.)
- **Failure modes:** Scripts are built from offender interviews and case files, so they favour cases that were caught or told, and describe how crimes were done, not the improvisation around them (my extension). Critics argue the cognitive-script foundation is unclear and too narrow for current research. `[sec]` The rational-choice premise (offenders weigh effort and risk) is a simplification.
- **Relevance:** Core for the hours-long scale; confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 7. Social disorganisation and collective efficacy

- **Definition:** Neighbourhood violence depends less on poverty alone than on the community's capacity to act together: social cohesion combined with a willingness to intervene for the common good.
- **Why it matters here:** It supplies a coarse-level state variable that is neither wealth nor policing, and that individual behaviour can feed. A block where neighbours will step in behaves differently from an equally poor block where they will not. Player acts of help or intimidation can move it, and the consequence is visible in how the district behaves.
- **Key sources:**
  - Sampson, R. J., Raudenbush, S. W., Earls, F. (1997). "Neighborhoods and Violent Crime: A Multilevel Study of Collective Efficacy." *Science* 277(5328), 918-924. DOI 10.1126/science.277.5328.918.
  - Maxwell, C. D., Garner, J. H., Skogan, W. G. (2011). Replication and extension of the Chicago collective-efficacy findings to 2004, NIJ-published (summary found via https://nij.ojp.gov/library/publications/collective-efficacy-and-criminal-behavior-chicago-1995-2004 `[sec]`).
  - Shaw, C. R., McKay, H. D. (1942). *Juvenile Delinquency and Urban Areas*. University of Chicago Press. (from memory; not searched)
- **Instance:** A 1995 survey of 8,782 residents across 343 Chicago neighbourhoods. Collective efficacy was negatively associated with violence after controls and largely accounted for the link from concentrated disadvantage and residential instability to violence. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Per-district violence hazard falls as collective efficacy rises, holding disadvantage and instability fixed. `[sec]` Testable: two districts matched on poverty and turnover but differing in efficacy should differ in violence.
  2. Disadvantage and residential turnover lower efficacy, and efficacy carries most of their effect on violence. `[sec]` Rule: efficacy is a stock that erodes with turnover and disadvantage and recovers with stable ties; violence depends on efficacy, not directly on poverty. Testable: remove the direct poverty term in a run and the violence pattern across districts should barely change.
  3. Efficacy can be computed bottom-up: the share of residents with ties and a willingness to intervene. (My extension, and the bridge between the two levels of detail; fields 11 and 18 give the switching and tie-building rules.)
- **Failure modes:** The core result is cross-sectional (Chicago, 1995), with efficacy measured by survey; the causal arrow is argued, not proved, and collective efficacy could partly reflect lower crime. Treating it as a single dial hides different kinds of cohesion (kin, gang, church, co-workers) that point in different directions (my extension).
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 8. Ethnography of street life: codes, order and public space

- **Definition:** Close observational accounts of how people actually behave in streets and neighbourhoods: informal rules, who is present and why, who keeps order, and how people move between public codes and private lives.
- **Why it matters here:** It is the source of texture for the seconds-long scale: what a street encounter looks like, what the participants expect of each other, who is out at a given hour. It also supplies the realistic mix of people (mostly ordinary residents, a minority playing a harder game) that a sampler needs.
- **Key sources:**
  - Anderson, E. (1999). *Code of the Street: Decency, Violence, and the Moral Life of the Inner City*. W. W. Norton.
  - Duneier, M. (1999). *Sidewalk*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.
  - Jacobs, J. (1961). *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*. Random House. (from memory; the search summaries noted only a Jacobs connection to Duneier's setting `[sec]`)
- **Instance:** Anderson's study of the Germantown Avenue area of Philadelphia, which describes an informal rulebook in which authority rests on respect and is enforced by the threat of force, set against "decent" families who try to live peacefully. `[sec]` Duneier's participant observation of book vendors, panhandlers and scavengers on lower Sixth Avenue. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Public behaviour in contested spaces follows an informal code in which respect is the currency and credible threat enforces it. `[sec]` Rule: agents carry a stance (decent, street, or switching by setting), and a perceived slight in a contested setting demands a response. Testable: encounter outcomes should vary with setting and stance, not only with the individuals.
  2. Long-standing informal presences (vendors, regulars) contribute to street order. `[sec]` Rule: they count as guardians in the routine-activity calculation. Testable: removing such a presence raises the cell's incident rate (links to field 2).
  3. In the neighbourhoods studied the large majority of residents were decent. `[sec]` Rule: population samplers (field 12) should keep the code-following minority small and visible, not the default. (My extension: a minority that matters disproportionately, as in field 4's concentration.)
- **Failure modes:** Each account is a single site, era and observer, and the observer's position shapes what is seen. The decent/street split can harden into stereotype. The sources I saw were largely publisher and press descriptions, not independent critiques. `[sec]` In a dystopian setting the risk is caricature: the texture should be borrowed, the politics should not.
- **Relevance:** Supporting; confidence medium.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 9. Informal and illicit economies

- **Definition:** The unregulated, untaxed and often illegal side of economic life, from domestic work and street vending to drug-selling organisations, studied as ordinary economic systems with budgets, wages and risks.
- **Why it matters here:** It supplies drives and blocked goals that need no invention: money owed, wages too low, a promotion that will not come. It gives gangs a cash flow, gives ordinary residents side jobs that tie them to brokers and protectors, and gives the coarse "wealth" field a structure.
- **Key sources:**
  - Venkatesh, S. A. (2006). *Off the Books: The Underground Economy of the Urban Poor*. Harvard University Press.
  - Levitt, S. D., Venkatesh, S. A. (2000). "An Economic Analysis of a Drug-Selling Gang's Finances." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 115(3), 755-789. NBER Working Paper 6592. https://nber.org/papers/w6592
  - Reuter, P. (1983). *Disorganized Crime: The Economics of the Visible Hand*. MIT Press. (from memory; not searched)
- **Instance:** Levitt and Venkatesh analysed the gang's own records over four years: street-level dealing was less lucrative than commonly thought, average pay rose from about $6 to about $11 an hour over the period, the distribution of earnings was extremely skewed, and members implicitly valued their own lives very low. `[sec]` Venkatesh's ethnography of one Chicago South Side neighbourhood describes a whole world of unreported work, from domestic workers and mechanics to street vendors, storefront clergy and gangs. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Pay inside an illicit organisation is heavy-tailed: a few earn most, and the rank and file earn little. `[sec]` Rule: faction income splits by rank with a skewed distribution. Testable: the bottom tier's expected hourly wage should sit near legal alternatives, and recruitment should respond to changes in those alternatives. (The recruitment link is my extension.)
  2. Informal and formal work are interlinked in the same households. `[sec]` Rule: a resident agent can hold a formal job plus informal gigs, and the gigs run through named brokers. Testable: removing a broker removes a measurable share of a household's income.
  3. A faction is a firm with a budget and fixed overheads, so violence costs money. (My extension; the finding that gang wars were costly is from memory and was not in the search summary.) Testable: faction treasury falls during sustained conflict and the pace of conflict slows when it does.
- **Failure modes:** One gang and one neighbourhood in 1990s Chicago, with data supplied by the gang's own leader; the exact numbers do not carry over. A cyberpunk setting adds illicit goods (data, augmentations, organs) whose economics are not covered here. The ethnographer's embeddedness shapes what was visible. `[sec]`
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 10. Collective violence and rebellion models

- **Definition:** Agent-based models in which individuals decide whether to join unrest by weighing grievance against the perceived risk of punishment, producing outbreaks at the level of a district.
- **Why it matters here:** It is a compact local mechanism for riots, strikes and uprisings that links a coarse field (police presence, legitimacy, grievance) to individual thresholds. A spark can be a change in one input (policing withdrawn, an injustice witnessed) rather than a scripted event, and the model is cheap to run only where the player is looking.
- **Key sources:**
  - Epstein, J. M. (2002). "Modeling Civil Violence: An Agent-Based Computational Approach." *PNAS* 99(suppl. 3), 7243-7250. DOI 10.1073/pnas.092080199. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC128592
  - Epstein, J. M., Steinbruner, J. D., Parker, M. T. (2001). Brookings working paper on the same model. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/modeling-civil-violence-an-agent-based-computational-approach/
  - Granovetter, M. (1978). "Threshold Models of Collective Behavior." *American Journal of Sociology* 83(6), 1420-1443. (from memory; not searched)
- **Instance:** Epstein's model has two variants: a central authority suppressing decentralised rebellion, and a central authority trying to halt communal violence between two ethnic groups. `[sec]` It is widely reimplemented (the Mesa framework and NetLogo both ship versions). `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. An agent turns active when grievance outweighs risk aversion times the estimated chance of arrest, and the arrest estimate depends on the local ratio of police to active agents in view. `[sec]` Rule: p_active compares grievance (hardship scaled by perceived illegitimacy) with risk aversion times arrest probability, where arrest probability falls as actives outnumber police. Testable: with grievance held constant, a local drop in police presence should produce an outbreak.
  2. Outbreaks are punctuated, not smooth: quiet stretches broken by bursts. (From memory of the model's behaviour; not in the search summaries.) Testable: the time series of active agents should show bursts with heavy-tailed sizes, not a steady level.
  3. Authority legitimacy is an input that scales grievance; losing it makes every threshold easier to cross. (My extension.)
- **Failure modes:** Minimal rules: no ties, leaders, coordination or shared identity, though real unrest depends heavily on groups and not on isolated individuals (fields 18, 21 and 23 supply ties, group identity and a fairness trigger). The arrest probability is local and the model is not tied to data in the form I saw. It is a demonstration that simple rules can give the patterns, not evidence the rules are right.
- **Relevance:** Supporting; confidence medium.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 11. Multi-level aggregation: compartmental, system-dynamics and hybrid models

- **Definition:** Describing a population by a few stocks and the flows between them (compartments: mean-field, well mixed, no individual identity) instead of by individuals, together with hybrid or variable-resolution designs that move between the two descriptions.
- **Why it matters here:** This is the machinery behind the central requirement, that one city exists as fields at coarse detail and as individuals at fine detail. It says when the aggregate can be trusted, when it hides what a story needs, and how to switch between the two without the numbers changing. It also gives the coarse layer its dynamics: gang strength, policing and crime pressure as stocks with delayed feedback, not independent dials.
- **Key sources:**
  - Kermack, W. O., McKendrick, A. G. (1927). "A Contribution to the Mathematical Theory of Epidemics." *Proceedings of the Royal Society A* 115(772), 700-721. (citation and the threshold result confirmed in search summaries `[sec]`)
  - Rahmandad, H., Sterman, J. (2008). "Heterogeneity and Network Structure in the Dynamics of Diffusion: Comparing Agent-Based and Differential Equation Models." *Management Science* 54(5), 998-1014. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1070.0787. https://www.mit.edu/~jsterman/Heterogeneity.html
  - Hunter, E., Mac Namee, B., Kelleher, J. (2020). "A Hybrid Agent-Based and Equation Based Model for the Spread of Infectious Diseases." *JASSS* 23(4), 14. DOI 10.18564/jasss.4421. https://www.jasss.org/23/4/14.html
  - Bobashev, G. V., Goedecke, D. M., Yu, F., Epstein, J. M. (2007). "A Hybrid Epidemic Model: Combining the Advantages of Agent-Based and Equation-Based Approaches." *Proceedings of the 2007 Winter Simulation Conference*, 1532-1537. DOI 10.1109/WSC.2007.4419767. `[sec]`
  - Davis, P. K., Hillestad, R. (1993). "Families of Models that Cross Levels of Resolution: Issues for Design, Calibration and Management." *Proceedings of the 1993 Winter Simulation Conference*, 1003-1012. DOI 10.1109/WSC.1993.718351. `[sec]`
  - Forrester, J. W. (1971). "Counterintuitive Behavior of Social Systems." *Technology Review* 73(3), 52-68. `[sec]` Forrester, J. W. (1969). *Urban Dynamics*. MIT Press. (from memory, including the publisher)
  - Bonabeau, E. (2002). "Agent-Based Modeling: Methods and Techniques for Simulating Human Systems." *PNAS* 99(suppl. 3), 7280-7287 (pages from memory). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC128598
- **Instance:** Rahmandad and Sterman compared the standard SEIR compartmental model with an agent model of the same disease on fully connected, random, small-world, scale-free and lattice networks. On headline public-health metrics (speed of spread, peak load, total burden) the two often did not differ significantly, so they argue the choice should rest on the costs and benefits of disaggregation, and they suggest the result extends to other diffusion processes such as innovation adoption and financial panics. Hunter et al. built a hybrid in which each area runs agents until the share of agents exposed or infected reaches a threshold, then switches to difference equations and reverts when the share falls back; a town-level switch at 20% saved about 2.16 ms per step against 6.77 ms for the fully agent-based town model, with no significant difference in outbreak size at the 1% level, and they recommend a town-level switch at 5% to 20%. Bobashev et al. used the opposite order: agents while infections are few, equations once the count is large enough for averaging. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Run the aggregate by default and instantiate individuals only where it stops being valid: when counts are small, when a process starts from a single seed, when contact is structured, or when the player is looking. Rahmandad and Sterman found aggregates often match agents on headline metrics; Hunter et al. switch on the share infected and Bobashev et al. on the count `[sec]`. Rule: keep a compartment model per district and process; switch a district to agents when a watched compartment falls below a floor count or the player's attention arrives, and back to the aggregate when counts are large and nobody is watching (the floor and the hysteresis are my extension; Hunter et al.'s criterion is a share). Testable: run the hybrid and an all-agent reference over the same seeds, and accept a switch setting only if peak, timing and total are not significantly different and per-step cost falls (the test Hunter et al. apply).
  2. Switching representation conserves stocks and distributions. Rule: hydration draws individuals whose attribute totals equal the field value (field 12); dehydration writes totals and updated distributions back; for anything unobserved the field is the source of truth. Testable: hydrate, run zero steps, dehydrate, and every aggregate returns to its prior value within a tolerance; in a run with random switching, total people, money and influence match a never-switching control. (My extension. Bobashev et al. hand aggregate values from the agent phase to the equation phase as initial conditions `[sec]`, and Davis and Hillestad's central finding is that moving across resolutions has to be designed in from the start `[sec]`.)
  3. Coarse fields are stocks with flows, feedback and delay, not dials. Forrester's argument was that such systems respond to interventions in counterintuitive ways, with compensation, delay and reversal. `[sec]` Rule: each field (gang strength, policing, crime pressure, rumour load) has explicit inflows and outflows and at least one delayed feedback. Testable: apply a step increase in patrols and track the field; expect a lagged response with an overshoot or partial reversion rather than an instant new level, and a long-run effect smaller than the naive first-order estimate. (The expected response shape is my extension.)
- **Failure modes:** The aggregate assumes mixing within a compartment, and Rahmandad and Sterman frame the agent model as worth its cost only where heterogeneity and network structure matter. Hybrids are not free: Hunter et al. found significantly different outbreak size and length distributions at low thresholds, and in their county model outbreak length differed from the agent model at every threshold tested. Every benchmark here is a single epidemic with a clear reference model; a city's many interacting processes have no ground truth to check a switch against (my extension). An observer-driven switch can rewrite history, because what was aggregate while unobserved is now specific, so hydrated individuals must agree with anything already shown about the field (my extension). Forrester's urban model drew critiques in the same period `[sec]`, and its stock-and-flow structure is a modeller's judgement, not a measurement.
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence high on the principles, medium on thresholds, which are model-specific.
- **Surprise:** Yes (aggregates often match agents on headline metrics).

## 12. Population synthesis and life-course demography

- **Definition:** Generating individuals and households whose attributes jointly match known aggregate tables (population synthesis), and then ageing them through partnership, childbearing, work and death with age-specific rates (demographic microsimulation and life-course models).
- **Why it matters here:** This is the hydration step. When the player's attention reaches a district, its field values (wealth, age structure, faction influence) must become named people who are consistent with those values, belong to households, have kin, and sit at a life stage that explains their drives. The life-course half says who is at risk of what, and when, and what changes it.
- **Key sources:**
  - Deming, W. E., Stephan, F. F. (1940). "On a Least Squares Adjustment of a Sampled Frequency Table When the Expected Marginal Totals Are Known." *Annals of Mathematical Statistics* 11(4), 427-444. DOI 10.1214/aoms/1177731829. (the origin of iterative proportional fitting, with an earlier independent version attributed to Kruithof (1937) `[sec]`)
  - Beckman, R. J., Baggerly, K. A., McKay, M. D. (1996). "Creating Synthetic Baseline Populations." *Transportation Research Part A* 30(6), 415-429. DOI 10.1016/0965-8564(96)00004-3. `[sec]`
  - Farooq, B., Bierlaire, M., Hurtubia, R., Flotterod, G. (2013). "Simulation Based Population Synthesis." *Transportation Research Part B* 58, 243-263. DOI 10.1016/j.trb.2013.09.012. `[sec]`
  - Guo, J. Y., Bhat, C. R. (2007). "Population Synthesis for Microsimulating Travel Behavior." *Transportation Research Record* (volume and pages not confirmed). Named in a search summary as the source of the zero-cell and household-plus-person problems. `[sec]`
  - Hammel, E. A., Hutchinson, D., Wachter, K., Lundy, R., Deuel, R. (1976). *The SOCSIM Demographic-Sociological Microsimulation Program: Operating Manual*. University of California, Berkeley. `[sec]`
  - Sampson, R. J., Laub, J. H. (1993). *Crime in the Making: Pathways and Turning Points through Life*. Harvard University Press. (publisher from memory) `[sec]`
  - Moffitt, T. E. (1993). "Adolescence-Limited and Life-Course-Persistent Antisocial Behavior: A Developmental Taxonomy." *Psychological Review* 100(4), 674-701. DOI 10.1037/0033-295x.100.4.674. `[sec]`
  - Hirschi, T., Gottfredson, M. R. (1983). "Age and the Explanation of Crime." *American Journal of Sociology* 89(3), 552-584. `[sec]`
- **Instance:** Beckman et al. combined 1990 US census summary tables with a sample of anonymised household records and used iterative proportional fitting to estimate the share of households in a block group with each demographic combination, then drew households from the sample in those proportions; synthetic joint distributions of household size and vehicle count did not differ substantially from the true ones in their test. `[sec]` SOCSIM takes monthly age-specific rates of birth, death and marriage, applies them to each person as competing risks, and tracks kin in linked structures, which is how it produces populations with plausible kinship. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Hydrated individuals must reproduce the field's aggregates. Rule: for each district, draw individuals from a joint attribute distribution fitted to the district's marginals (age, role, income band, faction membership, shift), by iterative proportional fitting where the full table is known and by sampling from partial views of the joint distribution where it is not. Farooq et al. report that fitting methods rely on cloning and scale poorly as attributes grow, and that their sampler beat the best IPF run in a Swiss test (error 0.35 for their worst case against 0.64 for IPF's best, though one record gives 0.85). `[sec]` Testable: tabulate the hydrated people and check every marginal against the field within sampling error; the same seed gives the same people (deterministic hydration).
  2. Households and kin come from rates, so people arrive already connected. Rule: generate household and family links from age-specific partnering and fertility rates (SOCSIM-style) instead of attaching relatives afterwards. Testable: sampled kin counts (siblings, children, living parents) match those implied by the rates; no one has a parent younger than a plausible minimum age; households sum to the headcount. (The mechanism is SOCSIM's `[sec]`; the checks are my extension. The household-plus-person consistency check answers the problem Guo and Bhat name `[sec]`.)
  3. Trouble is age-graded and changeable: offending peaks in adolescence and early adulthood and falls, and adult turning points (a stable job, a good marriage) raise desistance. `[sec]` Moffitt separates a large adolescence-limited group from a small life-course-persistent group. `[sec]` Rule: an individual's risk drive is an age-dependent hazard with two profiles, lowered by a job or partnership event. Testable: tabulate generated offenders by age and check the hump; a simulated person who gains stable work and a partner shows a lower offence rate afterwards than a matched control. (The rule form is my extension.)
- **Failure modes:** Fitting methods assume the sample the households are drawn from represents the district, and a fictional city has no census to fit: the targets are whatever the field says, so field errors propagate to the people (my extension). IPF has a zero-cell problem when a combination is absent from the sample, and cannot control household and person attributes at once. `[sec]` The age-crime regularity is contested: Hirschi and Gottfredson argued that it holds across conditions, and later cross-national tests found different curves. `[sec]` Sampson and Laub's turning-point results are mixed across studies. `[sec]` All of these hazards come from real populations; a city with augmentation, long life or corporate employment may have different life stages (my extension).
- **Relevance:** Core for the hydration step; confidence high on the sampling and kin-from-rates principles, medium on life-course parameters.
- **Surprise:** Partly (standard in transport and demography, but hydration being a testable statistics problem is not how game worlds usually treat it).

## 13. Time geography and human mobility

- **Definition:** Time geography treats a person as a path through space and time bounded by what they can do in a day (capability), who and what they must be with (coupling) and where they may be (authority), so places reachable from a position form a space-time prism. Human-mobility models give the statistical regularities of where and how far people go and how often they return, and activity-based models turn these into daily schedules.
- **Why it matters here:** It is the rule layer under every "who is where, when" question. Routines become constraints that can be checked (nobody is in two places, nobody crosses the city in ten minutes), the coarse level gets flows between districts from population alone, and the fine level gets believable daily plans. It also supplies the anchors, paths and co-presence that fields 2 and 3 need.
- **Key sources:**
  - Hagerstrand, T. (1970). "What About People in Regional Science?" *Papers of the Regional Science Association* 24(1), 7-21 (one index gives 6-21). DOI 10.1007/BF01936872. `[sec]`
  - Miller, H. J. (1991). "Modelling Accessibility Using Space-Time Prism Concepts within Geographical Information Systems." *International Journal of Geographical Information Systems* 5(3), 287-301. `[sec]`
  - Gonzalez, M. C., Hidalgo, C. A., Barabasi, A.-L. (2008). "Understanding Individual Human Mobility Patterns." *Nature* 453, 779-782. https://arxiv.org/abs/0806.1256
  - Song, C., Qu, Z., Blumm, N., Barabasi, A.-L. (2010). "Limits of Predictability in Human Mobility." *Science* 327(5968), 1018-1021. `[sec]`
  - Schneider, C. M., Belik, V., Couronne, T., Smoreda, Z., Gonzalez, M. C. (2013). "Unravelling Daily Human Mobility Motifs." *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* 10(84), 20130246. DOI 10.1098/rsif.2013.0246. `[sec]`
  - Brockmann, D., Hufnagel, L., Geisel, T. (2006). "The Scaling Laws of Human Travel." *Nature* 439, 462-465. `[sec]`
  - Simini, F., Gonzalez, M. C., Maritan, A., Barabasi, A.-L. (2012). "A Universal Model for Mobility and Migration Patterns." *Nature* 484, 96-100. `[sec]`
  - Horni, A., Nagel, K., Axhausen, K. W. (eds.) (2016). *The Multi-Agent Transport Simulation MATSim*. Ubiquity Press. DOI 10.5334/baw. (open access; the search confirmed its scope, not its treatment of plan scoring and replanning `[sec]`)
- **Instance:** Gonzalez et al. tracked about 100,000 anonymised mobile-phone users for six months. Individuals returned repeatedly to a small set of places, each with a stable characteristic travel distance, and once rescaled for distance and anisotropy their patterns collapsed onto one spatial distribution, unlike Levy-flight and random-walk models. Schneider et al. found that only 17 network motifs cover up to about 90% of people in survey and phone data, with each person's motif stable over months. `[sec]` Song et al. estimated potential predictability of about 93% from trajectory entropy, with no significant variation by distance travelled, age, gender or urban setting (a press-release summary). `[sec]` Simini et al.'s radiation model predicts flows between places from population distribution alone, with no fitted parameters, and was tested on commuting, migration, phone calls and freight. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Placement must pass a prism test. An agent can be at place x at time t only if it can reach x from its last fixed point and still reach its next fixed point (a shift, a meeting) in time at its speed and mode, subject to coupling (who must be present) and authority (where it may go). `[sec]` (The search source for the three constraints was a low-quality summary page; the names also match my memory of the paper.) Rule: every scheduled event carries a time window, and both schedule generation and the story generator reject any placement outside the agent's prism. Testable: replay all trajectories and assert that implied speed never exceeds the mode's maximum and that no agent holds two overlapping coupled commitments; moving one fixed time (a shift change) changes who can reach a scene. (The assertions are my extension.)
  2. Most days are routine, with heavy-tailed exceptions. Rule: each agent has two to five anchor places, a characteristic radius, a daily motif drawn from a short list, and a small probability of an exploratory trip with heavy-tailed length. `[sec]` Testable: radius of gyration stays bounded and stable over weeks; the top two places hold most of the agent's time; a handful of motifs covers most agents; predicting an agent's hour-by-hour location from its own history succeeds for most hours of a routine agent. The percentages are tuning targets, not constants to copy.
  3. Coarse flows follow populations and intervening opportunities, not distance alone. Rule: the flow from district i to district j is proportional to m_i n_j / ((m_i + s_ij)(m_i + n_j + s_ij)), where m_i and n_j are the origin and destination populations and s_ij is the population nearer to i than j is, with each origin's total outflow fixed (the radiation model; formula from memory). `[sec]` Testable: placing a large new destination near an origin lowers that origin's flows to farther destinations of unchanged size while total outflow stays fixed.
- **Failure modes:** The mobility evidence is phone records and a banknote-tracking proxy from particular countries and years; people with phones in range are not everyone, and the banknote work is a proxy for people. `[sec]` The 93% figure is an upper bound on potential predictability, and the press-release definition (one hour ahead) is not the paper's formal one. `[sec]` Critics note that the radiation model's large-population assumption produces systematic error in finite systems, especially for large cities. `[sec]` Time geography says what is feasible, not what people choose. In a vertical, transit-dependent or curfewed city, distances, anchors and the prism's speed assumptions change (my extension).
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence high on the prism and anchor principles, medium on numbers.
- **Surprise:** Yes (individual trajectories are far more predictable than intuition suggests).

## 14. Space syntax

- **Definition:** A family of methods that describe a street or building network by its configuration (how many changes of direction separate each space from all the others) and relate those measures, above all "integration", to movement, co-presence and land use.
- **Why it matters here:** It gives a cheap, map-only prediction of where pedestrians and chance encounters concentrate, hence where shops, markets and street trade settle and which streets are quiet. It supplies the coarse foot-traffic field from the street graph without simulating any walker, and it explains why a new wall, gate or closed street changes a district's life.
- **Key sources:**
  - Hillier, B., Hanson, J. (1984). *The Social Logic of Space*. Cambridge University Press. (introduced axial maps and integration measures `[sec]`)
  - Hillier, B., Penn, A., Hanson, J., Grajewski, T., Xu, J. (1993). "Natural Movement: Or, Configuration and Attraction in Urban Pedestrian Movement." *Environment and Planning B* 20(1), 29-66. DOI 10.1068/b200029. https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/envirb/v20y1993i1p29-66.html
  - Hillier, B. (1996). *Space Is the Machine: A Configurational Theory of Architecture*. Cambridge University Press. (from memory; not searched)
  - Jacobs, J. (1961), as in field 8, for the older "eyes on the street" argument that space syntax tries to quantify (my reading).
- **Instance:** Hillier et al. argue against theories that explain urban movement by trips to and from attractor land uses. Their evidence is that the layout of the street grid itself determines which spaces get more through movement, that the integration measure correlates best with observed movement, and that retail then locates where passing movement is strongest and amplifies the pattern the grid already creates. (abstract page read)
- **Principles:**
  1. Movement follows configuration. Through-movement on a street rises with its integration, largely independent of what lies on it. Rule: compute integration over the street graph once; base pedestrian density per segment is a monotone function of integration, before any attractor is added. Testable: on a generated map, density and integration correlate strongly; deleting a link (a closed bridge, a gate) lowers integration and flow on the segments that depended on it.
  2. Land use follows movement and then multiplies it. Rule: place retail and street trade with probability increasing in integration, then add an attraction term to the segments they sit on. Testable: retail density correlates with integration in the generated city; removing a retail cluster lowers but does not remove the flow on its street (the grid's share remains).
  3. Co-presence is the raw material of encounter and informal surveillance: well-connected streets mix residents with strangers, and poorly connected pockets mix residents only with residents. (My reading of the theory, not a finding I checked.) Rule: derive a stranger-to-resident ratio per segment from integration and feed it to field 2 (guardians and targets) and field 7 (ties and efficacy). Testable: low-integration cells log fewer stranger encounters and a higher resident share.
- **Failure modes:** The strong claim is that configuration alone generates movement, and the paper is explicitly arguing against the attractor view, so the dispute is live (my reading of the abstract). Axial maps and integration depend on how lines and graphs are drawn (from memory of the method's critiques; not searched). The framework is flat: a vertical city with stacked walkways, lifts and transit changes the graph and the meaning of "turns" (my extension). It describes pedestrian movement; vehicle and transit flow behave differently (field 24). I read one abstract, not the 1993 data.
- **Relevance:** Supporting to core; confidence medium.
- **Surprise:** Yes (the street layout alone predicts much of the foot traffic).

## 15. Urban scaling

- **Definition:** Quantitative regularities in how a city's totals change with its population N: socioeconomic outputs (wages, patents, crime) tend to grow faster than N, infrastructure grows slower than N, and a city's deviation from the trend is a persistent local signature.
- **Why it matters here:** It gives the coarse level a calibration ruler. For a district's population it says how much wealth creation, crime and infrastructure to expect, and the deviation marks which places are notable over- or under-performers and so carry stories. It supplies defensible magnitudes for the fields without a data table.
- **Key sources:**
  - Bettencourt, L. M. A., Lobo, J., Helbing, D., Kuhnert, C., West, G. B. (2007). "Growth, Innovation, Scaling, and the Pace of Life in Cities." *PNAS* 104(17), 7301-7306. DOI 10.1073/pnas.0610172104. `[sec]`
  - Bettencourt, L. M. A., Lobo, J., Strumsky, D., West, G. B. (2010). "Urban Scaling and Its Deviations: Revealing the Structure of Wealth, Innovation and Crime across Cities." *PLoS ONE* 5(11), e13541. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0013541. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2978092/
  - Arcaute, E., et al. (2015). "Constructing Cities, Deconstructing Scaling Laws." *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* 12, 20140745. https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.1674
  - Leitao, J. C., Miotto, J. M., Gerlach, M., Altmann, E. G. (2016). "Is This Scaling Nonlinear?" *Royal Society Open Science* 3, 150649. (only front matter seen `[sec]`)
- **Instance:** Across US metropolitan areas most socioeconomic indicators scale superlinearly with an exponent of about 1.15, roughly 15% more per person for each doubling of population; gross metropolitan product comes out near 1.13, and scaling accounts for about 86% of the variance in violent crime. A scale-adjusted metropolitan indicator (SAMI) is a city's residual from the log-log fit; SAMIs are dimensionless, largely uncorrelated with land area or density, and persist for decades, with autocorrelation decay times of about 19 years for patents and 35 for personal income. (2010 paper, read) The 2007 abstract puts wealth and innovation measures near 1.2 and infrastructure measures near 0.8. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Outputs scale superlinearly and infrastructure sublinearly. Rule: coarse targets for wealth-like and crime-like quantities scale as N^1.15 across districts, and roads, cabling and services as N^0.8, so a district twice as large has more than twice the incidents and less than twice the infrastructure. Testable: fit log-log slopes over generated districts and keep them inside tolerance bands. The exponents are shapes to tune, not constants.
  2. Judge a district by its residual, not by its per-capita rate, because per-capita rankings mix size effects with local conditions (the authors' argument). Rule: store a residual per district (observed minus expected, on the log scale) as slowly changing state, and weight story selection towards large residuals. Testable: residuals are uncorrelated with size by construction; districts with large positive crime residuals appear in the story-selection log more often than size alone predicts.
  3. Residuals persist for years. Rule: treat the residual as a long-memory process; a one-off event shifts it and the shift decays over simulated years. Testable: the residual series' autocorrelation decays on the configured timescale, and a district's rank by residual rarely changes within a year of simulated time. (The persistence is from the 2010 paper; the process form is my extension.)
- **Failure modes:** The data are US metropolitan areas around 2005-2006 and the authors state that similar persistence in fast-changing systems is untested; population is a proxy for mechanisms, not a cause. Arcaute et al. found, for England and Wales, that fitted exponents shift considerably with how the city is defined and that most indicators scale about linearly, and they conclude the proposed laws are not supported; Leitao et al. note that more recent results cast doubt on exponents different from 1 (front matter only). `[sec]` The fit is across cities at one time, so it does not say how one city's district would change if its population doubled, and districts inside a city may not follow between-city laws (my extension).
- **Relevance:** Supporting; confidence medium (strong as a calibration prior, weak as a law).
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 16. Residential sorting, segregation and gentrification

- **Definition:** Models of how individual location choices, each responding to neighbours and prices, add up to district composition: tipping and segregation (Schelling) and the economics of when capital returns to cheap, well-placed land (the rent gap).
- **Why it matters here:** It gives districts a population dynamic. Who lives where, who is displaced and which blocks flip all have causes here, and mild preferences can produce sharp outcomes. The rent gap supplies an economic trigger for redevelopment, evictions and turf pressure that needs no villain.
- **Key sources:**
  - Schelling, T. C. (1971). "Dynamic Models of Segregation." *Journal of Mathematical Sociology* 1(2), 143-186. `[sec]`
  - Card, D., Mas, A., Rothstein, J. (2008). "Tipping and the Dynamics of Segregation." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 123(1), 177-218. `[sec]`
  - Smith, N. (1979). "Toward a Theory of Gentrification: A Back to the City Movement by Capital, Not People." *Journal of the American Planning Association* 45(4), 538-548 (volume, issue and pages from memory; the search confirmed the journal, year and the rent-gap idea). `[sec]`
  - Hamnett, C. (1991), a critique of rent-gap theory (named in a search summary; title not confirmed). `[sec]`
- **Instance:** Schelling's paper combines a simulation, an analytic model of compartmented space and an application to neighbourhood tipping, and is known for showing that mild preferences for own-group neighbours can produce strong segregation, with no simple correspondence between individual incentive and collective result. `[sec]` Card, Mas and Rothstein used census tracts from 1970 to 2000 and found tipping-like behaviour in white population flows in most cities, with tipping points ranging from 5% to 20% minority share, higher in cities with more tolerant whites, and no systematic nonlinearity in rents or house prices around those points. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Small individual thresholds produce sharp district-level change. Rule: each household has a tolerance for the share of dissimilar neighbours (on whatever fictional axis the setting uses) and moves when it is exceeded; at coarse level this is a flow equation on district shares with a tipping point. Testable: sweep the tolerance and the district's composition shows a sharp transition, and long-run segregation exceeds what the individual tolerances alone would suggest. `[sec]`
  2. Tipping points are district-specific and depend on attitudes. Rule: draw each district's threshold from a range (5% to 20% in the US data, used here only as a range `[sec]`) and let attitude shocks move it. Testable: matched districts with different tolerance fields tip at different shares, and an incident that shifts attitudes moves the threshold.
  3. Redevelopment is triggered by a rent gap: capital moves to land whose current rent capitalises far below what redevelopment would earn. `[sec]` Rule: each block tracks current against potential income; when the gap exceeds a threshold and a catalyst is present (a corporate project, new transit), conversion starts and displaces residents, which lowers collective efficacy (field 7) and raises tension. Testable: remove the gap and conversions stop; conversions concentrate in the blocks with the largest gap. (The threshold rule is my extension.)
- **Failure modes:** Schelling's model is stylised, and the evidence is mixed: another analysis of 1970-2000 data found little evidence of tipping to all-black and all-Hispanic communities, and web explainers quote different tipping figures informally. `[sec]` Rent-gap theory is criticised for setting individual agents aside and reducing demographic change to consumer preference. `[sec]` Both literatures are about US and UK cities and about race and class; in a dystopian city displacement may be run by corporations, not by markets (my extension). The segregation axis invites caricature, so it should be fictional and not a real ethnic one (my extension, in line with the warning in field 8).
- **Relevance:** Supporting; confidence medium.
- **Surprise:** Yes (mild preferences, sharp outcomes).

## 17. Underworld governance and protection

- **Definition:** The study of criminal organisations as suppliers of governance (protection, dispute settlement, contract enforcement) where the state's version is absent, weak or hostile, and of the conditions under which that order is stable.
- **Why it matters here:** It explains why gangs exist as institutions and not as collections of offenders, what they sell to ordinary residents, why they fight over territory and customers, and how state and criminal order interact. It links low trust or weak policing to the rise of a protector, which is the causal chain a faction-level simulation needs.
- **Key sources:**
  - Gambetta, D. (1993). *The Sicilian Mafia: The Business of Private Protection*. Harvard University Press. `[sec]`
  - Skarbek, D. (2014). *The Social Order of the Underworld: How Prison Gangs Govern the American Penal System*. Oxford University Press. `[sec]`
  - Varese, F. (2001). *The Russian Mafia: Private Protection in a New Market Economy*. Oxford University Press. `[sec]`
  - Tilly, C. (1985). "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime." In P. Evans, D. Rueschemeyer, T. Skocpol (eds.), *Bringing the State Back In*. Cambridge University Press. (chapter details from memory; the argument was confirmed in a search summary `[sec]`)
  - Lessing, B. (2015). "Logics of Violence in Criminal War." *Journal of Conflict Resolution* 59(8), 1486-1516. `[sec]`
- **Instance:** Gambetta treats the Sicilian Mafia as an enterprise that produces and sells private protection in a low-trust setting, drawing on the confessions of Mafiosi and the trials they set off, and he separates protection from extortion, where the protector is the threat. Skarbek argues that prison gangs form to create order among outlaws and so produce alternative governance institutions to support illegal activity. Lessing separates cartels, which want to pressure the state into changing its behaviour, from rebels, which want territory, and predicts that turf war among cartels makes "violent lobbying" of the state rarer than "violent corruption", because the broad benefits of lobbying invite free-riding. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Demand for protection rises where the state's enforcement is weak and trust is low, and protection is a service with customers. Rule: each district has a protection gap, demand (transactions needing enforcement, distrust) minus state supply (policing, courts); a faction supplies the gap when it can credibly threaten, and its income is a share of the transactions it protects. Testable: raise police or court coverage in a district and a rival protector's income there falls; lower trust and the demand rises. (Gambetta's thesis `[sec]`; the gap formulation is my extension.)
  2. Sought protection and imposed tribute are different relationships with different consequences. Rule: tag each payment relationship as sought or imposed; the tag sets residents' attitude to the faction and the chance they report it. Testable: districts with mostly sought protection show less reporting to police and less hostility toward the faction than districts with imposed payments at the same payment size. (The distinction is Gambetta's `[sec]`; the consequences are my extension.)
  3. Violence between protectors is a cost of dividing a territory, and violence aimed at the state is chosen strategically. Rule: factions in contested territory spend their violence on each other and press the state less; where territory is uncontested they invest in influence over the state. Testable: when a second faction enters a district, inter-faction violence rises and corruption-type activity falls, and the converse when one faction becomes dominant. (A reading of Lessing's prediction. `[sec]`)
- **Failure modes:** The sources are single-region studies (Sicily, US prisons, Russia, Latin America) built from confessions, trials and interviews, which over-represent people who were caught or willing to talk (my extension). Reviewers dispute parts: one review of Gambetta objects to a libertarian reading of the protection argument, and one review of Skarbek argues the book underplays race as the central organising feature of US prison gangs. `[sec]` Tilly's analogy puts state and racket on a continuum, a provocation more than a measurement. `[sec]` In a setting where corporations are protectors with legal status, whether the mechanism carries over is an extension.
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 18. Social network structure and brokerage

- **Definition:** How the pattern of ties among people (who is connected to whom, how strongly, through whom) shapes access to information, opportunity and risk: small-world structure, clustering, weak ties as bridges, brokers who span gaps between groups, and the layered limit on how many close ties a person keeps.
- **Why it matters here:** It supplies the fine-level graph along which beliefs, loyalties and violence travel. It says who knows whom, who the connectors are (the people stories hang from), how cohesive a group is, and it gives a principled, cheap way to build social structure from a few parameters instead of authoring it.
- **Key sources:**
  - Granovetter, M. S. (1973). "The Strength of Weak Ties." *American Journal of Sociology* 78(6), 1360-1380. (citation and argument confirmed in search summaries `[sec]`)
  - Burt, R. S. (1992). *Structural Holes: The Social Structure of Competition*. Harvard University Press. `[sec]`
  - Watts, D. J., Strogatz, S. H. (1998). "Collective Dynamics of 'Small-World' Networks." *Nature* 393, 440-442. DOI 10.1038/30918. `[sec]`
  - Onnela, J.-P., Saramaki, J., Hyvonen, J., Szabo, G., Lazer, D., Kaski, K., Kertesz, J., Barabasi, A.-L. (2007). "Structure and Tie Strengths in Mobile Communication Networks." *PNAS* 104(18), 7332-7336. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1863470 `[sec]`
  - Zhou, W.-X., Sornette, D., Hill, R. A., Dunbar, R. I. M. (2005). "Discrete Hierarchical Organization of Social Group Sizes." *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 272(1561), 439-444. DOI 10.1098/rspb.2004.2970. `[sec]`
  - Padgett, J. F., Ansell, C. K. (1993). "Robust Action and the Rise of the Medici, 1400-1434." *American Journal of Sociology* 98(6), 1259-1319. `[sec]`
  - Papachristos, A. V. (2009). "Murder by Structure: Dominance Relations and the Social Structure of Gang Homicide." *American Journal of Sociology* 115(1), 74-128. DOI 10.1086/597791. `[sec]`
  - Papachristos, A. V., Wildeman, C., Roberto, E. (2015). "Tragic, but Not Random: The Social Contagion of Nonfatal Gunshot Injuries." *Social Science & Medicine* 125, 139-150. DOI 10.1016/j.socscimed.2014.01.056. `[sec]`
- **Instance:** Papachristos rebuilt the network of gang killings in Chicago from incident-level homicide records and found that murders between gangs form an institutionalised network of group conflict in which violence spreads by contagion as gangs watch rivals and respond to dominance concerns. In the follow-up, 70% of nonfatal gunshot victims over six years sat in co-offending networks making up under 6% of the city's population, each 1% increase in exposure to victims in one's network raised the odds of being shot by about 1.1%, and the effect reached ties two to three steps away. Onnela et al. built a national network from 18 weeks of call records covering about 20% of the population and found it robust to removal of strong ties but fragmenting when weak ties are removed, with the coupling of tie strength to structure trapping information inside communities. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Build social structure as clustered local ties plus a few long ties, in layers of closeness. Rule: for each individual generate ties in nested layers (a handful of intimates, a few tens of friends and associates, up to a couple of hundred acquaintances), dense inside households, workplaces and gangs, with a small rewiring probability that creates shortcuts. Zhou et al. found a hierarchy of group sizes with a preferred scaling ratio near three, `[sec]` and Watts and Strogatz showed that a little rewiring of a regular graph keeps clustering high while shortening paths sharply. `[sec]` Testable: generated graphs show high clustering with short average path length; layer sizes follow a ratio near three (a tunable).
  2. Weak ties bridge groups, so information and opportunity cross group boundaries through them, and brokers who span otherwise separate clusters hold a distinctive position. Rule: tag ties strong or weak by contact frequency; weak ties supply the links between clusters; an individual with weak ties into many clusters is a broker. Testable: removing weak ties raises the number of components and slows cross-cluster spread more than removing the same number of strong ties does (Onnela et al. `[sec]`). Burt's structural-hole argument is that the person spanning a gap gains access, timing and control `[sec]`, and Padgett and Ansell describe the Medici as the family whose networks spanned otherwise separate structures `[sec]`.
  3. Violence runs along ties, so risk depends on network position, not only on individual traits. Rule: when a violent event occurs, raise the hazard for ties within two to three steps of the victim, most for direct ties, on top of the field-level hazard; retaliation is likelier against the group just above or below in the dominance network. Testable: after an event the incident probability for ties at one, two and three steps decreases with distance, and victimisation concentrates in a small share of the population (under 6% accounting for about 70% in the cited data, as a tuning anchor). (Papachristos `[sec]`; the propagation rule is my extension and overlaps with field 4's self-excitation.)
- **Failure modes:** Granovetter's study was a retrospective survey of 282 people with mostly correlational support since, and one critique finds a logical slip between bridges and weak ties. `[sec]` Phone-call networks capture one channel; in a surveilled, augmented city the real channels differ (my extension). The Chicago results define ties through arrests and shared offending, which over-represents people already known to police (my reading of the data description). `[sec]` The figure of 150 belongs to the broader social-brain argument and is extrapolated, not a fitted tier of the Zhou et al. hierarchy. `[sec]` The brokerage literature rests on case studies and cross-sectional data; its causal arrows are argued.
- **Relevance:** Core for the fine level; confidence high on the structure principles, medium on parameter values.
- **Surprise:** Partly (weak ties are well known; the layered sizes and the concentration of violence along ties are less so).

## 19. Rumour and information diffusion

- **Definition:** Models of how information, rumour and behaviour spread over contact networks: epidemic-like (simple) contagion, threshold-based (complex) contagion, the cascades each produces, and what makes a rumour travel.
- **Why it matters here:** Rumour is one of the coarse fields the system keeps, and information is the substance of most stories (who knows what, and when). These models say how a fact moves from one person to a district, what it takes to turn a rumour into action, how it dies out, and how a false story can outrun a true one.
- **Key sources:**
  - Daley, D. J., Kendall, D. G. (1964). "Epidemics and Rumours." *Nature* 204, 1118. DOI 10.1038/2041118A0. `[sec]`
  - Maki, D. P., Thompson, M. (1973). *Mathematical Models and Applications*. Prentice-Hall. (from memory; the "Maki-Thompson variant" is named in a search summary `[sec]`)
  - Centola, D., Macy, M. (2007). "Complex Contagions and the Weakness of Long Ties." *American Journal of Sociology* 113(3), 702-734 (volume, issue and pages from memory; the search confirmed the journal and November 2007). `[sec]`
  - Kempe, D., Kleinberg, J., Tardos, E. (2003). "Maximizing the Spread of Influence through a Social Network." *Proceedings of the 9th ACM SIGKDD Conference (KDD 2003)*. `[sec]`
  - Vosoughi, S., Roy, D., Aral, S. (2018). "The Spread of True and False News Online." *Science* 359(6380), 1146-1151. DOI 10.1126/science.aap9559. `[sec]`
  - Allport, G. W., Postman, L. (1947). *The Psychology of Rumor*. Henry Holt. (publisher from memory; the "basic law" named in search summaries `[sec]`)
  - Granovetter, M. (1978), threshold models, as cited in field 10.
- **Instance:** The Daley-Kendall model splits a population into ignorants, spreaders and stiflers, and a spreader becomes a stifler on meeting someone who already knows the rumour, which is where it departs from an epidemic model; the Maki-Thompson variant converts only one of two meeting spreaders. `[sec]` Vosoughi et al. followed about 126,000 verified true and false stories on Twitter from 2006 to 2017, shared by about 3 million people, and found falsehood travelled farther, faster and deeper than truth in every category, strongest for political news, with bots not responsible; false stories were more novel, though the authors did not establish novelty as the cause. `[sec]` Kempe et al. study the independent-cascade and linear-threshold models and show that greedy seeding gets within a factor of 1 - 1/e of the optimal seed set, which is itself NP-hard to find. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. A rumour spreads like an epidemic that stops itself: spreaders lose interest when they meet people who already know. Rule: three states (unaware, spreading, informed and silent); a spreader meeting an informed person turns silent with some probability, so the final reach is a bounded share of the district and not everyone. `[sec]` Testable: across many seeds the final unaware share settles near the model's value in a well-mixed group (about one fifth in the classic Daley-Kendall result, my recollection, not searched) and is larger when contact is clustered.
  2. Simple items cross weak ties; behaviour that needs reinforcement does not. Rule: tag each propagating item as simple (a fact, a sighting) or complex (joining a protest, switching allegiance, which needs several independent sources); simple items cross clusters through single bridges, complex items need several adopters inside a cluster and several parallel ties between clusters. `[sec]` Testable: a call to protest spreads inside a tight community and stalls at its boundary while a sighting crosses it, and widening the bridge (more ties between two clusters) lets the complex item through.
  3. Importance times ambiguity drives rumour intensity, and novelty drives passing it on. Rule: an item's pass-on rate is proportional to the product of its importance to the listener and the ambiguity of the available evidence, with a boost for novelty, and authoritative clarity lowers it. Testable: an item with zero importance or zero ambiguity never becomes a rumour, and releasing clear evidence cuts spread. (Allport and Postman's "basic law" is a heuristic that has not been tested as such, `[sec]` and Vosoughi et al. link false news to novelty without proving cause; `[sec]` the rate rule is my extension.)
- **Failure modes:** Daley-Kendall assumes homogeneous mixing, so network structure (field 18) matters. The Twitter results describe a platform and an era, not a street-level whisper network. The "basic law" was adapted from earlier work on judgement and importance is hard to measure. `[sec]` Influence maximisation guarantees hold for the model, not the world. `[sec]` These models give reach and timing, not content: how a story is distorted as it is retold is not modelled (my extension).
- **Relevance:** Core (rumour is a declared coarse field); confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 20. Norms, reputation and common knowledge

- **Definition:** How shared expectations hold behaviour in place (a social norm as a conditional preference keyed to what others do and to what others think one should do), how reputation and graduated sanctions sustain cooperation among people who cannot all watch each other, and how beliefs about others' beliefs (pluralistic ignorance, preference falsification, common knowledge) can keep a norm alive that few privately hold, or let it collapse all at once.
- **Why it matters here:** Much of a dystopia lives here: a district that complies with something nobody likes, an informer culture, an unspoken truce, a sudden break when something becomes public. The field supplies mechanisms for false beliefs about the group's beliefs, for gossip as a reputation system, and for the public acts that change what is commonly known.
- **Key sources:**
  - Bicchieri, C. (2006). *The Grammar of Society: The Nature and Dynamics of Social Norms*. Cambridge University Press. (publisher from memory) `[sec]`
  - Kuran, T. (1995). *Private Truths, Public Lies: The Social Consequences of Preference Falsification*. Harvard University Press. `[sec]`
  - Prentice, D. A., Miller, D. T. (1993). "Pluralistic Ignorance and Alcohol Use on Campus: Some Consequences of Misperceiving the Social Norm." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 64(2), 243-256. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.64.2.243. `[sec]`
  - Centola, D., Willer, R., Macy, M. (2005). "The Emperor's Dilemma: A Computational Model of Self-Enforcing Norms." *American Journal of Sociology* 110(4), 1009-1040. `[sec]`
  - Willer, R., Kuwabara, K., Macy, M. W. (2009). "The False Enforcement of Unpopular Norms." *American Journal of Sociology* 115(2), 451-490. `[sec]`
  - Chwe, M. S.-Y. (2001). *Rational Ritual: Culture, Coordination, and Common Knowledge*. Princeton University Press. `[sec]`
  - Nowak, M. A., Sigmund, K. (1998). "Evolution of Indirect Reciprocity by Image Scoring." *Nature* 393, 573-577. DOI 10.1038/31225. `[sec]`
  - Ostrom, E. (1990). *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action*. Cambridge University Press. (publisher from memory; the design principles confirmed in search summaries `[sec]`)
- **Instance:** In Prentice and Miller's four studies, students rated themselves as more uncomfortable with campus drinking than the average student, and over a semester male students' attitudes drifted toward the norm they wrongly perceived. In Centola et al.'s agent model, a norm backed by a few zealots and opposed by most can become self-enforcing when agents see only their neighbours, but not in a fully connected network, and against intuition more zealots or more bridging ties made such cascades less likely. In Willer et al.'s experiments, people who had conformed under pressure then criticised a lone dissenter, partly to signal that their compliance was sincere. Chwe argues that public ceremonies work not only by sending a message but by letting each member know what the others know. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Compliance is conditional on two beliefs: what others actually do (empirical expectation) and what others think one should do (normative expectation). `[sec]` Rule: each agent has a norm-specific conditional preference and complies when both expectations, estimated from its neighbourhood and from public signals, exceed its threshold; changing either belief changes behaviour without changing preferences. Testable: hold preferences fixed, shift the visible rate of compliance, and behaviour moves in the predicted direction.
  2. Beliefs about others can be wrong in a stable way, and public acts correct them at a stroke. Rule: track for each norm and district both true private support and perceived support (estimated from local samples), allow enforcement that the enforcer does not privately endorse, and tag events as public (witnessed by many who know others witnessed it) or private; public events update everyone present's beliefs about everyone's beliefs, private events only the witness's. (Prentice and Miller, Centola et al., Willer et al. and Chwe `[sec]`; the public and private event tags are my extension.) Testable: seed a norm with a few zealots on a graph where agents see only neighbours and it persists although most oppose it; on a fully visible graph it collapses; the same act done privately leaves the perception gap and done publicly closes it and can flip the norm within a few steps.
  3. Cooperation without repeated meetings needs a knowable reputation and graduated sanctions. Rule: helping is sustained in a district only while the chance that a helper can learn a recipient's reputation exceeds the cost-to-benefit ratio of helping (Nowak and Sigmund's condition `[sec]`), so gossip reach (field 19) sets how much cooperation a district can hold; sanctions escalate from mild to severe and are imposed by members or accountable monitors (Ostrom's design principles `[sec]`). Testable: cut gossip reach and cooperation falls past a threshold; replace graduated sanctions with immediate severe ones and compliance becomes more brittle (the second effect is my extension).
- **Failure modes:** The empirical anchors are campus surveys and student experiments (a wine tasting, a text evaluation), and the computational results depend on network structure and carry no content. `[sec]` Image scoring is a simplification, and I did not search the critiques of it. Ostrom's principles come from small, stable commons; a review found strong support with modifications, `[sec]` but a large anonymous city is untested (my extension). The key quantity in Kuran's theory, private preference, is unobservable in real cases, so it is inferred after the fact (my extension).
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes (a norm can be held by a majority who privately reject it, and one public act can end it).

## 21. Groups and organisations: identity, exit and voice, frontline discretion

- **Definition:** How group membership by itself shifts behaviour (in-group favouritism, intergroup conflict and what reduces it), and how organisations (gangs, firms, agencies) hold their members and turn policy into action: the choice between leaving, protesting and staying loyal, and the discretion of frontline staff.
- **Why it matters here:** The system's individuals have group memberships, and these decide whom they help, whom they fight and what they do when the group declines. This field explains why a gang is not a hierarchy that does what the boss says, why police and corporate staff make de facto policy through their routines, and why a coalition can be fragile.
- **Key sources:**
  - Tajfel, H., Billig, M. G., Bundy, R. P., Flament, C. (1971). "Social Categorization and Intergroup Behaviour." *European Journal of Social Psychology* 1(2), 149-178. `[sec]`
  - Sherif, M., Harvey, O. J., White, B. J., Hood, W. R., Sherif, C. W. (1961). *Intergroup Conflict and Cooperation: The Robbers Cave Experiment*. University Book Exchange, Norman, Oklahoma (publisher from memory). Chapter 7 is hosted at https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Sherif/chap7.htm `[sec]`
  - Hirschman, A. O. (1970). *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty: Responses to Decline in Firms, Organizations, and States*. Harvard University Press. `[sec]`
  - Lipsky, M. (1980). *Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Services*. Russell Sage Foundation. `[sec]`
  - Decker, S. H., Van Winkle, B. (1996). *Life in the Gang: Family, Friends, and Violence*. Cambridge University Press. `[sec]`
- **Instance:** Tajfel et al. split participants on trivial criteria and let them divide money between the groups; in-group favouritism appeared, even when it cost the in-group in absolute terms. At Robbers Cave, contact alone (shared films and meals) did little to defuse hostility between two groups of campers, and goals that needed both groups (restoring the camp water supply, freeing a stalled truck) reduced it gradually. Decker and Van Winkle drew on three years of fieldwork and interviews with 99 active gang members and 24 family members in St. Louis, and describe a loose, chaotic organisation and the process of joining. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Membership alone biases behaviour: an agent allocates help, trust and hostility by group label before any history. `[sec]` Rule: add an in-group bonus to an agent's valuation of resources and actions that benefit members; the bonus applies even to arbitrary groups, so any new label creates friction. Testable: an agent given a split between own and other labels favours its own at some cost to itself, and relabelling mid-run moves the favouritism.
  2. Contact does not reconcile; shared goals do, gradually. `[sec]` Rule: intergroup hostility falls only through joint tasks that need both groups, each task lowering it a little and the first often followed by relapse. Testable: two hostile groups in repeated contact without joint goals stay hostile or worsen, and adding joint goals lowers hostility stepwise.
  3. Members of a declining organisation exit or use voice, loyalty delays exit, and frontline staff turn heavy caseloads and unclear goals into de facto policy. `[sec]` Rule: each member facing worsening conditions chooses exit, voice (protest, inform upward) or silence, with loyalty raising the exit threshold and available voice channels diverting exit into voice; frontline roles (patrol officers, clerks, enforcers) get discretion that grows with load and goal ambiguity. Testable: tighten a gang's rewards and defection rises, more slowly where loyalty is high; remove its voice channels and the exit share rises; an overloaded patrol officer shows higher variance in enforcement than the stated policy implies. (The rule forms are my extension.)
- **Failure modes:** The minimal-group results come from laboratory experiments, largely with Western student samples, and one later test with three groups found no significant in-group bias. `[sec]` Robbers Cave was a single summer-camp field experiment with boys (from memory), and its tidy sequence of conflict and reconciliation is unusually clean. Lipsky's account has a tension: it treats policy as vague while faulting workers for not complying with it. `[sec]` Decker and Van Winkle is one city and era, and its loosely organised gangs are not every gang. `[sec]` Exit-voice-loyalty is a general framework; the rule forms above are my own operationalisation.
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Partly (exit and voice are well known; the arbitrary-label and contact-fails results are the useful surprises).

## 22. Feuds, honour and escalation

- **Definition:** Models of how disputes between individuals and groups start, escalate and end when no authority settles them: self-help and honour, contests between near-equals over rank, the situational barrier to violence, the security dilemma, and settlement by a third party; with Lanchester's attrition equations as a coarse model of the fighting itself.
- **Why it matters here:** It is the mechanism behind feuds and gang wars. It says why violence is rarer and clumsier than a script assumes, which pairs are dangerous (near-equals), how a defensive move reads as a threat, and how conflicts end. Lanchester gives the coarse level a way to resolve a battle between groups without simulating each fighter.
- **Key sources:**
  - Black, D. (1983). "Crime as Social Control." *American Sociological Review* 48(1), 34-45. `[sec]`
  - Gould, R. V. (2003). *Collision of Wills: How Ambiguity about Social Rank Breeds Conflict*. University of Chicago Press. `[sec]`
  - Collins, R. (2008). *Violence: A Micro-Sociological Theory*. Princeton University Press. `[sec]`
  - Jervis, R. (1978). "Cooperation under the Security Dilemma." *World Politics* 30(2), 167-214. `[sec]`
  - Boehm, C. (1984). *Blood Revenge: The Anthropology of Feuding in Montenegro and Other Tribal Societies*. University Press of Kansas (reprinted 1987 by the University of Pennsylvania Press under the subtitle "The Enactment and Management of Conflict in Montenegro and Other Tribal Societies"). `[sec]`
  - Deitchman, S. J. (1962). "A Lanchester Model of Guerrilla Warfare." *Operations Research* 10(6), 818-827. `[sec]`
  - Lanchester, F. W. (1916). *Aircraft in Warfare: The Dawn of the Fourth Arm*. Constable. (from memory; the 1916 date and the equations confirmed in a search summary `[sec]`)
- **Instance:** Black argues that much conduct classed as crime is self-help, airing a grievance by unilateral aggression, and that it persists where legal protection fails. Gould argues that conflict is likelier between social equals than between people whose rank is settled; one account reports homicide four to eight times likelier against an equal-status person, drawing on homicide data from US cities and Indian villages. Collins argues that violence is the exception because confrontational tension and fear must be overcome, that real violence is clumsy, and that when one side suddenly shows weakness the other can overshoot into "forward panic" (overkill). In Boehm's Montenegrin material a tribunal of elders adjudicates feuds to preserve tribal solidarity, and feuds follow trajectories that can end or escalate. In Lanchester's direct-fire model the loser is the side whose strength, which grows with the square of numbers, is exhausted first; area fire gives a linear law; Deitchman's variant mixes the two to show a smaller, hidden guerrilla force beating a larger regular one. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Grievances without a legitimate remedy become self-help, and ambiguity about rank among near-equals generates the most disputes. Rule: give each dispute a rank gap between the parties (individuals or groups); the hazard that a grievance turns violent is highest at small gaps and where no accepted authority can settle it. Testable: sort generated conflicts by rank gap and check they concentrate at small gaps; add a trusted arbiter and the conversion rate falls. (Black, Gould and Boehm `[sec]`; the hazard form is my extension.)
  2. Violence needs a situation, not only a motive, and at coarse detail a fight is an attrition process. Rule (fine): each confrontation passes a tension check; violence starts only if an audience or support condition is met or one side is visibly weak; outcomes have high variance (misses, wrong targets), and sudden weakness can trigger overkill. Rule (coarse): resolve an unobserved fight between groups by Lanchester attrition, with the square law for aimed fire between concentrated forces and a guerrilla variant where one side is hidden. Testable: the share of confrontations that turn violent is low and rises with audience and visible weakness; coarse outcomes are not proportional to headcount (under the square law, doubling numbers quadruples effective strength). (Collins and Lanchester `[sec]`; numbers are tuning.)
  3. Defensive moves look like threats unless they can be told apart, and escalation falls when offence and defence are distinguishable. Rule: when faction A arms or fortifies, B's threat estimate rises unless the move is recognisable as defensive; the hazard of a spiral falls when defensive postures are legible and when an accepted mediator exists. Testable: in runs where moves are ambiguous, arms-race spirals occur more often, and adding an elders' tribunal shortens feuds. (Jervis argues the dilemma eases when defence has the advantage and defensive postures can be told apart `[sec]`; the faction-level reading is my extension.)
- **Failure modes:** Black's is a broad sociological claim and I saw only an abstract. The "four to eight times" figure for equal-status homicide comes from a secondary write-up. `[sec]` Collins's claim that violence is the exception is about confrontations; his book also treats clandestine violence as one way round the tension barrier `[sec]`, and how well the theory covers planned, organised violence such as ambushes is something I did not check. Lanchester's equations assume homogeneous forces, aimed fire and no morale; Deitchman's model is specific to guerrilla against regulars. `[sec]` Jervis wrote about states; transfer to gangs is an extension. Boehm's settlement depends on a tribe with elders' authority and a shared identity, which gangs may lack.
- **Relevance:** Core; confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes.

## 23. Scarcity, debt and moral economy

- **Definition:** How people and households manage having too little (food, cash, time), what determines who goes without (entitlements, not only aggregate supply), how the poor use informal finance and debt, and how shared notions of fair prices and obligations drive crowd action when violated.
- **Why it matters here:** It supplies the economic side of drives and blocked goals: rent due, debt owed, a price rise that breaks a customary expectation. It explains why shortages hit some groups and not others even when supply is adequate, why debts create obligations to brokers and protectors, and why price shocks produce riots and not only grumbling.
- **Key sources:**
  - Sen, A. (1981). *Poverty and Famines: An Essay on Entitlement and Deprivation*. Clarendon Press. `[sec]`
  - Thompson, E. P. (1971). "The Moral Economy of the English Crowd in the Eighteenth Century." *Past & Present* 50, 76-136. `[sec]`
  - Collins, D., Morduch, J., Rutherford, S., Ruthven, O. (2009). *Portfolios of the Poor: How the World's Poor Live on $2 a Day*. Princeton University Press. `[sec]`
  - Shah, A. K., Mullainathan, S., Shafir, E. (2012). "Some Consequences of Having Too Little." *Science* 338, 682-685. `[sec]`
  - Shah, A. K., Mullainathan, S., Shafir, E. (2019). "An Exercise in Self-Replication: Replicating Shah, Mullainathan, and Shafir (2012)." *Journal of Economic Psychology* 75 (author list and volume taken from an IDEAS record `[sec]`).
- **Instance:** Sen argued that famine comes from entitlement failure and not only from falling food supply: in Bengal in 1943 aggregate per-capita foodgrain availability was only about 10% below the 1938-42 average, while groups without assured access to food were hit by soaring rice prices. Thompson read eighteenth-century food riots as action grounded in notions of the common weal, and set a traditional "moral" economy against the classical market economy. Collins et al. report yearlong financial diaries from more than 250 households in Bangladesh, India and South Africa, finding that poor households generally do not live hand to mouth but use savings, informal networks, savings clubs and microfinance. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Shortage is distributional: who goes without depends on each group's entitlements (what it can command by wages, trade, assets and support), so a district can go hungry under adequate supply when a price or income shock hits a group. Rule: give each resident group an entitlement (income and assets mapped to a basket); shortage events change prices and entitlements, and deprivation is computed from entitlement against basket, not from supply alone. Testable: raise a staple's price by 30% with supply unchanged and deprivation concentrates in the lowest-entitlement groups; restore supply without restoring entitlements and deprivation persists. (Sen `[sec]`; the mapping is my extension.)
  2. Households run portfolios of informal finance, and debts are relationships. Rule: each household holds several instruments (savings club, loan from a neighbour or broker, trade credit) with different costs and obligations; a shortfall draws on them in a set order, and unpaid debts convert into obligations to the creditor (labour, favours, information). Testable: remove a savings club and the same income shock produces more borrowing from brokers; the share of households owing favours to a broker rises after a shock. (The instruments are from Collins et al. `[sec]`; the conversion into obligations is my extension.)
  3. Crowd action follows violation of a customary standard of fairness, not shortage alone. Rule: each district carries a fairness norm (a customary price or provision); the hazard of riot, boycott or looting rises when prices or conduct breach it and falls when authorities visibly act to restore it, and the same shortage within the norm produces grumbling. Testable: the same price rise produces unrest when it breaches the norm and none when it does not, and a visible authority response lowers the hazard. (Thompson `[sec]`; the hazard form is my extension, and it feeds the grievance input of field 10.)
- **Failure modes:** Sen's reading of Bengal has been disputed by at least one economist who argues the data and the entitlement analysis are wrong. `[sec]` The cognitive-scarcity results did not all replicate: a multi-lab effort recorded a failed replication, and the original authors' own high-powered replications found some effects robust and others weak, with the cognitive-fatigue result running opposite to the original. `[sec]` Do not build tunnelling or reduced bandwidth into drives as established. Portfolios of the Poor is a small sample from three countries, rural and slum households. The moral-economy concept was used inconsistently even by its originator, `[sec]` and its application to a corporate-priced city is an extension.
- **Relevance:** Supporting to core; confidence medium.
- **Surprise:** Yes (shortage without scarcity of supply; unrest tied to a breached norm).

## 24. Crowd and traffic flow

- **Definition:** The mechanics of many bodies or vehicles moving through constrained space: pedestrian and traffic flow, jams and phantom jams, group walking, and macroscopic flow relations that summarise a whole network.
- **Why it matters here:** It is the cheap, plausible way to make streets feel populated and to produce congestion, queues and blocked routes. It is also a clean example of the level-of-detail problem (field 11): individual rules for the visible street, a flow-density relation for the rest of the city.
- **Key sources:**
  - Helbing, D., Molnar, P. (1995). "Social Force Model for Pedestrian Dynamics." *Physical Review E* 51, 4282-4286. https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/9805244
  - Moussaid, M., Perozo, N., Garnier, S., Helbing, D., Theraulaz, G. (2010). "The Walking Behaviour of Pedestrian Social Groups and Its Impact on Crowd Dynamics." *PLoS ONE* 5(4), e10047. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0010047.
  - Nagel, K., Schreckenberg, M. (1992). "A Cellular Automaton Model for Freeway Traffic." *Journal de Physique I* 2(12), 2221-2229. DOI 10.1051/jp1:1992277. `[sec]`
  - Geroliminis, N., Daganzo, C. F. (2008). "Existence of Urban-Scale Macroscopic Fundamental Diagrams: Some Experimental Findings." *Transportation Research Part B* 42(9), 759-770. DOI 10.1016/j.trb.2008.02.002. `[sec]`
- **Instance:** Moussaid et al. analysed 260 groups at low density and 1,093 at moderate density (55% and 70% of pedestrians walked in groups, all of two to four people). Groups walk side by side at low density and bend into a V (three people) or U (four) at higher density, walking speed falls linearly with group size, and in simulation these social interactions cut overall crowd speed by about 17%. Geroliminis and Daganzo combined detector, probe and GPS-taxi data from Yokohama and found network-wide flow, density and speed close to a single curve for a large urban area. `[sec]` In the Nagel-Schreckenberg cellular automaton, each car has an integer speed, and a randomised slowdown rule produces spontaneous "phantom" jams. `[sec]`
- **Principles:**
  1. Simple local rules give realistic crowd and traffic patterns, and a random slowdown term is what makes jams appear without a cause. Rule: pedestrians move towards a goal with repulsion from others and from walls (social-force style); vehicles follow a cellular-automaton rule with a random slowdown probability. Testable: above a critical density a jam forms from uniform flow with no obstacle, and setting the slowdown probability to zero removes it at the same density (my reading of the mechanism `[sec]`). The social-force abstract says the model reproduces several observed collective patterns of motion but does not name them (abstract read).
  2. Most pedestrians walk in groups, and groups cost speed. Rule: generate pedestrians as groups of two to four at a share of 55% to 70%, with a formation rule that depends on density (a line abreast at low density, a V or U at higher), and let group size slow the walk. Testable: group share in the generated crowd matches the target, formation changes with density, and flow is lower with groups than without. (Moussaid et al., read.)
  3. At coarse detail, use the flow-density relation, not vehicles. Rule: keep a density per district and read speed from a fitted flow-density curve, so congestion above a critical density lowers throughput; instantiate individual vehicles and pedestrians only near the camera. Testable: aggregating the fine simulation over a district reproduces a single flow-density curve with little scatter, and the coarse and fine levels agree on district speed within a tolerance (field 11, principle 2). `[sec]`
- **Failure modes:** The social-force model is hand-tuned and expensive for large crowds, so it belongs at the camera only. The group findings are from specific observation sites, at low to moderate density; behaviour at extreme density is not covered. Nagel-Schreckenberg is a single-lane freeway model. The macroscopic fundamental diagram was shown for Yokohama, with deviations attributed to experimental error, `[sec]` and a vertical city of drones, transit and stacked roads may not have one (my extension).
- **Relevance:** Supporting; confidence medium-high.
- **Surprise:** Yes (jams without a cause; groups slow whole crowds).

## 25. Cascading infrastructure failure and disaster sociology

- **Definition:** How failures spread through interdependent systems (power, communications, transport, water), and how people and institutions behave in the disasters that follow: whether they panic, help each other or die alone, and which neighbourhoods suffer.
- **Why it matters here:** In a dystopian city infrastructure is a rich source of causes that need no faction: a blackout, a datanet outage or a transit stoppage cascades into other systems and produces plausible, consequential events. The sociology half says how residents respond and who is hurt.
- **Key sources:**
  - Perrow, C. (1984). *Normal Accidents: Living with High-Risk Technologies*. Basic Books. (publisher from memory) `[sec]`
  - Rinaldi, S. M., Peerenboom, J. P., Kelly, T. K. (2001). "Identifying, Understanding, and Analyzing Critical Infrastructure Interdependencies." *IEEE Control Systems Magazine* 21(6), 11-25. DOI 10.1109/37.969131. `[sec]`
  - Buldyrev, S. V., Parshani, R., Paul, G., Stanley, H. E., Havlin, S. (2010). "Catastrophic Cascade of Failures in Interdependent Networks." *Nature* 464, 1025-1028. DOI 10.1038/nature08932. `[sec]`
  - Motter, A. E., Lai, Y.-C. (2002). "Cascade-Based Attacks on Complex Networks." *Physical Review E* 66, 065102. `[sec]`
  - Klinenberg, E. (2002). *Heat Wave: A Social Autopsy of Disaster in Chicago*. University of Chicago Press. `[sec]`
  - Drury, J., Cocking, C., Reicher, S. (2009). "Everyone for Themselves? A Comparative Study of Crowd Solidarity among Emergency Survivors." *British Journal of Social Psychology* 48(3), 487-506. DOI 10.1348/014466608X357893. `[sec]`
  - Helbing, D., Farkas, I., Vicsek, T. (2000). "Simulating Dynamical Features of Escape Panic." *Nature* 407, 487-490 (pages from memory). https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0009448
- **Instance:** Buldyrev et al. modelled the 28 September 2003 Italian blackout as two-way dependence between power stations and the communication nodes that control them, and showed that failing a very small fraction of nodes in one network can fragment several interdependent networks. Klinenberg's social autopsy of the July 1995 Chicago heat wave (over 700 deaths, mostly of old and poor people) found that death rates varied with isolation and neighbourhood abandonment; one review reports substantially higher death rates in communities with high crime and little social activity than in others with the same age and income profiles. Drury et al. interviewed 21 survivors of 11 emergencies and found that shared identity went with solidarity and helping and not with panic. `[sec]` Helbing et al.'s escape-panic simulations, in contrast, show panic and jamming arising from a lack of coordination (abstract read).
- **Principles:**
  1. Failure spreads through load redistribution and mutual dependence. Rule: model each system as a graph with load and capacity, capacity being (1 + tolerance) times the initial load, and add cross-system dependencies (power to control and communications and back); a failing node sheds its load and an overloaded node fails in turn. `[sec]` Testable: at zero tolerance a single removal can bring down a whole network, at high tolerance it cannot, and with two-way dependence the cascade exceeds what either network shows alone. `[sec]`
  2. Tight coupling plus interactive complexity make accidents likely and hard to contain, and slack and redundancy are the buffer, so efficiency drives that remove them cause later failures. `[sec]` Rule: give each subsystem a coupling (time to recover, slack) and a complexity (number of interactions), let cascade probability rise with both, and treat a cut to redundancy (a corporation optimising a district's grid) as a cause of future failure. Testable: halving a district's reserve capacity raises cascade frequency, and adding slack lowers it. (The numbers are my extension.)
  3. Disasters strike through social structure and bring solidarity more often than mass panic. `[sec]` Rule: harm equals hazard times vulnerability, with vulnerability rising with isolation (few ties, field 18) and institutional abandonment, and emergency crowds defaulting to helping when members share an identity and to self-interest otherwise, panic being a rare special case. Testable: in a heat or outage event, deaths concentrate in low-tie, low-service districts at equal age and income, helping events outnumber crush events, and a shared-identity cue raises helping within the same crowd. (Klinenberg and Drury et al. `[sec]`; the panic case is a design choice, since Helbing et al. model it.)
- **Failure modes:** The Italian blackout work is a stylised network model with full interdependence assumed. Perrow's theory is criticised as too pessimistic about containment. `[sec]` Klinenberg is a single case, and Drury et al. is 21 interviews, some about events two decades old, so memory is a limit. `[sec]` Emergency crowds in cramped or burning spaces can crush, as the escape-panic model shows (abstract read), and the combat, gas or fire disasters of a cyberpunk city may look more like those than like the cases studied (my extension).
- **Relevance:** Core for event generation; confidence medium.
- **Surprise:** Yes (a small failure can fell coupled systems; the poor die from isolation more than from the hazard itself).

## Considered and rejected

The first five were set aside in the first pass and were not researched in either pass; they stand as judgements. The rest were considered in the second pass, with the evidence noted where a search supplied it.

- **Strain theory (Merton):** descriptive typology with little mechanism; its content is better carried by blocked-goal drives.
- **Broken windows as a mechanism:** evidence is contested, and collective efficacy (field 7) covers the useful part.
- **Predictive-policing algorithms as a field in their own right:** the mechanism is near-repeat modelling (field 4).
- **Full economic general-equilibrium models of the city:** far heavier than the plausibility the system needs; field values are enough.
- **Biological and genetic criminology:** no use as a simulation mechanism.
- **Maslow's hierarchy of needs as the ordering of drives:** a review of the research found only partial support for the strict ordering, and longitudinal studies did not support the proposition that satisfied needs stop motivating, although one aspect (self-actualisation) fared better (Wahba, M. A., Bridwell, L. G. (1976). "Maslow Reconsidered: A Review of Research on the Need Hierarchy Theory." *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance*; volume and pages not confirmed). `[sec]` Drives should come from blocked goals and the economic and social fields (7, 20, 23) instead.
- **A hard Dunbar number (a cap of 150 contacts) as a mechanism:** Zhou et al. (field 18) fit a hierarchy of group sizes with a ratio near three, and 150 appears there as an extrapolation from the social-brain argument, not a fitted tier. `[sec]` Used as a soft guide for layer sizes only.
- **Continuous opinion-dynamics models (consensus, Friedkin-Johnsen, bounded confidence):** Hegselmann, R., Krause, U. (2002). "Opinion Dynamics and Bounded Confidence: Models, Analysis and Simulation." *JASSS* 5(3), 2, ask when a group reaches consensus, polarisation or fragmentation. `[sec]` They track numeric opinions, while the story needs discrete beliefs and who knows what, which fields 19 and 20 cover. They may be worth revisiting for district-level attitude fields.
- **Social-force or other individual-based crowd simulation across the whole city:** too costly. It belongs at the camera, with the flow-density relation elsewhere (field 24).
- **A pure gravity model as the coarse mobility rule:** Simini et al. (field 13) present the radiation model as an alternative to the gravity law that needs only population data and no adjustable parameters. `[sec]` Gravity remains a fallback if the radiation form misbehaves.
- **Culture-of-poverty explanations of behaviour in poor districts (Oscar Lewis's 1960s argument; from memory, not searched):** contested and open to stereotype. The situational accounts in fields 8, 9 and 23 do the work without it.
- **Full game-theoretic equilibrium models of faction interaction:** heavy, and they presuppose common knowledge that the setting's residents lack. Fields 20 and 22 give cheaper decision rules built on expectations, thresholds and legible threats (my judgement; no source).
- **Treating Forrester-style whole-city system dynamics as a predictive model:** retained only as a method for coarse fields (field 11), since the 1969 urban model drew critiques at the time and its structure is a modeller's judgement. `[sec]`

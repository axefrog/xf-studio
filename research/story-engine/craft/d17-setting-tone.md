## In brief

- **"Reads as Night City" is mostly a matter of relations, not props.** Genre theory (Altman) separates a genre's vocabulary from the relations among its elements, and a genre's critics (Shiner, Charity) fault work that has the first and not the second. The engine's test for fidelity is therefore two-part: the right words and objects are present, and the right relations hold between them (a corporate technology has a street use, a body part has an owner, a faction's trade explains its behaviour). A word-list check alone passes the costume (GNR-01 to GNR-04).
- **Most of the typed setting data the engine needs does not exist in the game's records.** The records say gang, corporation or civilian; they hold no territory, trade, size, wealth, danger, influence or ownership for a place, and the district-level gang list on the region records is read only by the map's gang panel ([places §3.3, §8](../../../knowledge/places.md), [population §5](../../../knowledge/population-and-affiliations.md)). Territory is a property of the leaf area, and V is almost never "in Watson" as the engine counts. So the typed setting data is pack material, keyed to leaf places, and the simulation derives what it can from indirect traces and marks them `derived`, never `mirrored` (NCD-01 to NCD-05, S17-S4-01, S17-S1-01, S17-S1-02).
- **The studio's own stated practice is a benchmark and a warning.** Developers say they start from the situation, use no fetch or escort typology, give main and side quests the same standard, treat failure as consequence, and alternate heavy and light quests (Weber). They also say the filter is volume: designers' ideas are accepted at roughly 5 to 10 per cent (Sasko). And they list their own misses: too many shards, choices too subtle to notice, some areas too noisy to read. The shipped data shows a small marker typology under the "no typology" claim (five street-story variants), so the usable reading is "no typology of the seed, a small typology of the marker" (STU-01 to STU-12).
- **Media are typed actors with positions and formats, not a text pool.** An outlet has an owner, a voice, a reach and a systematic divergence; a format (shard, scanner entry, text, bark) has a length and a voice. The foundation already has the right shape: an outlet is an actor and a headline is a belief that actor publishes. The knowledge base holds no inventory of the game's own news, radio or advertising, which is a research request (MED-01 to MED-06, MM-4).
- **A tone lint can check vocabulary, speaker, specifics, scale, polarity and curve. It cannot check whether anything lands.** Checkable: closed tone tags, a world lexicon with speaker classes, a stake scale against the entities named, expected polarity from the beat's effects against the declared tone, tone-naming words, an urgency claim with no clock, and the session's tone trace. Not checkable: wit, freshness, whether a stereotype is present in meaning, whether dread works. The lint must be calibrated by running it over the game's own authored text, where its false-positive rate is a number (TLN-01 to TLN-05).
- **Dread is anticipation under uncertainty, and the engine's open slots suit it.** A review of the anxiety literature finds that responses grow when a threat's nature, probability or timing is uncertain, and that anticipation, not reaction, carries the effect. The foundation lets a threat's nature stay open until contact, with every shown trace consistent with the ways it could resolve. That is a design fit, not a proof. Wrongness is a broken regularity the player had confirmed, and it always has a recorded answer. Horror in this setting should come from systems (debt, ownership, being sorted) and acts, not from augmented bodies as monsters (DRD-01 to DRD-09).
- **A procedure can place and time dread. It cannot write it.** Dread seeds should be authored fragments (traces, a threat's sketch, a handful of lines) with procedural placement, clocks and rest, and a player setting that can turn the register down or off (DRD-08, DRD-09).
- **Most of this is thin.** The tone and dread evidence is small studies, designers' accounts and theory. Several lens claims did not survive (§2.3). Seventeen measures give P9 a way to replace the guesses (§7).

## 1. Scope and why it matters here

Quality bar 6 says generated content reads as Night City: its factions, districts, economics, tone and themes. Bars 1 and 7 meet it at two points: a cause should be a *Night City* cause (rent, debt, ownership, a fee), and pacing is partly tonal. This dossier asks what makes content read as this setting and not as generic cyberpunk, what the studio that built it says it does, how in-world media carry events to the player, how a tone can be kept without a machine that understands tone, and how dread is built.

**What is already settled and used as given.**

### 2.3 Where the advice itself fails, and what did not survive

- **Almost every source is a critic's essay, a designer's interview or a theory.** Nothing here measures whether a generated item reads as Night City to players. Every principle is a [hypothesis] for the engine even where the underlying observation is [practice] or [analysis].

## 4. What the game and the sources show

### 4.1 The genre's own terms

- **Sterling** (page) presents the genre as a merger of high technology with underground pop culture. Technology is intimate and bodily (prosthetics, implants, a mind reached by interfaces) and is shown in places where ordinary people improvise uses its makers did not plan. He ties it to its decade and expects the label to fragment.
- **Shiner** (page) says the same impulse was a template within six years, and that what was lost was the interest in global culture and energetic prose. His closing ask is for work that treats technology as a tool and acknowledges the costs of solving problems.
- **Charity** (page) asks what the corporation does to the ordinary person. The question is the test, whatever one thinks of the verdict.
- **Altman** (excerpt) gives the working vocabulary: semantic elements (the content a genre is recognised by) and syntactic relations (how those elements are arranged to mean something). Vocabulary alone gives wide coverage and little explanation. Relations alone explain and fit fewer works. A genre's stable form is a negotiation between those who make it and those who watch it.

### 4.2 The setting's author

The *NME* interview (page) gives the stated intent. The genre is a warning, not a future to aim for. The look is gritty and grounded, with bulky, dangerous-looking weapons one step beyond today's. The politics are about fairness: people should be able to make a decent living without being exploited by the more powerful, and the heroes fight for family, friends and neighbourhood. The setting grows from small stories to crossovers and then an overarching narrative, which he likens to a comic book's growth. He hopes players find the city unpleasant and still worth visiting.

### 4.4 The studio's stated practice

From the Weber interview (page, 2019): hundreds of short pitches of about half a page; the strongest become design documents of at least ten pages, written like a screenplay without finished dialogue and divided into labelled sections; each quest is paired with a writer; a basic playable version with rough dialogue comes before final lines. No rigid templates or checklists, and no categories such as fetch or escort. Main and side quests get the same standard. Heavier story quests and energetic quests alternate, and leads and directors who play the whole game give the pacing feedback. Story outranks pacing. Designers adapt systems to quests. Quests cannot be failed in the classic sense; poor choices are lived with. Street Stories came from a dedicated Open World Team and are fixer jobs comparable to monster contracts, usually with several ways to complete.

From Sasko (pages, 2023 and 2024): the filter is volume, with designers' ideas accepted at about 5 to 10 per cent; memorable quests carry emotional weight drawn from real struggle and primary sources; shards may have been too many; choices were too subtle; urgency and freedom clashed.

From the art directors (page): four visual time layers used as a vocabulary (a bleached 1980s base, a bright plasticky 1960s-flavoured kitsch, a corporate neomilitarist layer, and a rare pseudo-natural layer for the ultra-rich); a rule of contrast almost everywhere; each district with its own theme and colour coding under city-wide shared rules; social layers read from the mix of eras; procedural tools tested and rejected, apart from a road tool whose output artists adjusted by hand.

### 6.1 The setting registries and where each field comes from

A registry entry's fields carry one of three source marks. `authored` is pack text. `derived` has a rule and its inputs. `mirrored` names a game record. A derived field must be derivable for any district, including a mod's (NCD-13).

### 6.2 A tone-lint pass

1. **Static (compile time).** Read the tags, the lexicon, the `stakeScale`, the declared effects and the text units. Run L-77 to L-92. All findings point at what the author wrote (S2 §11.1's source maps).
2. **Sampled (lab).** Run sample states; render stand-in lines; check the lexicon and specifics density on the rendered lines; check dread beats against `reveals` at each stage.
3. **Session (ledger).** Compute the tone trace from the beats actually released, and report the alternation, the floor and the rationing events. This is a measure, not a gate.
4. **Calibration.** Run 1 to 3 over the vanilla calibration set (TLN-04) and report the false-positive rate per rule. A rule above its budget is tuned or removed.

### 6.3 Dread, read through the foundation

A dread seed is a partly resolved quest. The threat's `nature` is an open slot. Traces are pins (observed) or constraints (shown, so committed). The engine's rule that displaying a trace fixes the past (S2 §2.15) means each shown trace narrows what the threat can be, and the authored kit lists the natures that are still consistent. Contact samples among them. This does the work of withholding with no new machinery. It adds one duty to the kit's author: every shown trace must be consistent with every nature the kit lists. That is a static check on the kit (S17-S2-05). [hypothesis].

## 7. Walk-through: one rent rise, two ways

**A generic version.** A megacorporation raises prices. A gang objects. V is hired to attack a warehouse. Nothing in it needs Night City.

**A typed version.**
1. S1 holds an administrative cost for a leaf (`wat_kab`): a landlord's tenancy fee reprices with the district's price level (`costToResidents`: `administrative`, `price`, borne by tenants).
2. An outlet with a corporate owner publishes a version that blames a gang and omits the beneficiary (MED-05). A second outlet, local and poor, publishes the opposite. Both are beliefs of their outlets.
3. A vendor's stall shows a handwritten price card in the district price band (NCD-09). A banner on a residents' board uses the district's idiom (NCD-08).
4. A fixer offers a gig whose `withheld` field says the client owns a share in the building (NOI-01). The terms carry a handling cut (SOC-01).
5. The stake is a block (PON-01). A success outcome carries a cost fact: the tenants keep the building and the vendor loses a patron (TON-03).
6. The lint checks the scale, the lexicon, a specific per scene, an `outlet` on each media fragment, and a `costToResidents` reference on the corporation named. It does not check whether the card is funny.

## 10. Lateral links

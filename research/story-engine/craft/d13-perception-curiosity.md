## In brief

- **The spec's word "displayed" covers four different things, and the engine only needs three of them.** A delivery can be *released* (handed to the game), *presented* (on V's screen or in earshot by a geometric test), *confirmed* (V's own input shows it was processed) or *noticed* (nobody can observe it). The ledger of [S2 §10.2](../spec/seeding.md#102-the-exposure-ledger) has one yes-or-no. I propose three grades, `ambient`, `presented` and `confirmed`, with a criterion for each channel (§5.2), and a rule that a callback may cite only what was confirmed (DSP-01 to DSP-11). Without this a callback to an unnoticed trace reads as the world knowing something V never saw.
- **Interruption cost and noticing rate are the same dial, read from two ends.** Dossier 7 ranks channels by how much they interrupt: class 0 traces cost nothing and class 4 approaches cost most. The same ranking is the order in which they get noticed. A class 0 trace passed on foot, with V's attention on a route, is a coin toss or worse (§4.A, NTC-01 to NTC-05). So the cheap channel that the engine leans on is also the least reliable, and its answer is redundancy across different kinds of sign (§5.3), never one trace, never one channel.
- **Load is two-sided, and dossier 7's load table misses the second side.** Dual-task research says conflict rises when two tasks share a modality and a code. Reading a banner while driving shares the eyes. A spoken line during a conversation shares the verbal channel. A phone conversation made drivers recognise fewer roadside objects they had looked at (NTC-03, NTC-04). §5.3 turns this into an activity-by-channel table of noticing tiers, with placeholder numbers inside the range the studies span.
- **The player's memory is cue-driven, source-blind and reconstructive, so every callback carries its own cue.** Recall works best when the retrieval cue matches the encoding context; people keep content better than where it came from; they fill gaps with what a schema expects; and unfinished tasks are not remembered better, only resumed. The engine should treat "V was shown X" as a claim about the screen, not about V's head. Callbacks re-cue the cited event with the features that were present (RMB-04 to RMB-08), and the count of threads that matters is the count V can retrieve with a visible cue (RMB-09, a correction to DIR-32's reading of Cowan).
- **Habituation is about features, and the engine can count them without any model of V.** Response falls for the repeated feature, recovers when it changes, recovers partly after a rest and falls faster on the next round. The engine already logs six skeleton features per display (S12-S2-07). Counting repeats per feature and per channel is a ledger query, not a profile (HAB-01 to HAB-07). The sharpest testable form of "it feels generated" is a prediction task after N encounters.
- **Curiosity needs an anchor that was already confirmed.** A hook with no known fact to attach to does nothing; a precise question beats a vague mystery; a payoff thinner than the gap costs the next hook; and telling the outcome first does not spoil the pull in short-text experiments. So an `introduce` delivery for a mystery-shaped seed should declare the facts it leans on and wait until they were confirmed (CUR-01 to CUR-07).
- **Several folk rules do not hold, and the dossier says so first.** The unfinished-task memory effect, the doorway effect and "peak-end beats the plain average" were all weaker than the popular versions (§2.3). Almost no study measures generated narrative or open-world play, so the principles are a testing agenda (§11), and §7 gives eighteen measures P9 can compute from the session ledger plus short probes.
- **Evaluation must not trust what players say about why.** Stated reasons are inferences, recognition probes over-report, and retrospective ratings tilt to the peak and the end. The measures use behaviour logs, foils and cued recall, and never reward a confabulated "why" (CNF-01 to CNF-04). The same findings (choice blindness, hindsight) are things the engine must never use on V: covert swapping of a result is class 4 (CNF-03).

## 1. Scope and why it matters here

Every scheduling choice in dossiers 7, 8 and 11 assumes something about the person at the screen: that a trace will be seen, that a thread is remembered, that a repeat is boring, that a gap pulls. This dossier checks those assumptions against what is known about perception, memory and curiosity, says how information reaches the player, and writes the answers as requirements. It is about the real player, who is driving, fighting or talking in a dense open world. It is not about NPC memory (dossier 11) or the engine's model of V (dossier 12).

| Layer or concern | What this dossier gives it |
|---|---|
| 1. Simulation (S0, S1) | Nothing new in the kernel. The kernel's relevance and perception pins are the other side of the ledger grades |
| 2. Seeding (S2) | Exposure grades and a per-channel `displayed` contract (S5's side), a re-cue slot for callbacks, source tags, redundancy for class-0 signs, an anchor for curiosity hooks, a counter-cue rule for unreliable sources, lint |
| 3. Procedural engine (S3, P5) | A noticing prior per channel and load, a redundancy planner, a re-cue planner, a spacing rule for plants, per-feature and per-channel repeat counters, a predictability proxy, a twist rule, closing-delivery rules |

| Expression (X) | Word budgets, a rule for naming the channel, and the self-reference lexicon |
| Player configuration (C) | Nothing is inferred about V's attention. One possible setting (how long threads keep a visible cue) is in the open questions |

**What it does not cover.** The arbiter's gates, rest and rate (dossier 7). Scale, threads and tempo (dossier 8). Save and reload rules, NPC forgetting and the return view's structure (dossier 11). The player model and manipulation (dossier 12). The wording and craft of lines (dossier 21). Perceptual variety as a template defence is mostly dossier 5's; this dossier supplies the perceptual facts it needs (HAB-02, HAB-07).

**What is already settled and used as given.**
- **V is observed, never owned** (D-09). Nothing here infers what V attends to. Noticing rates are population priors, set in P9 and applied equally to every V.
- **The exposure ledger answers "was V shown this?"** ([S2 §10.2](../spec/seeding.md#102-the-exposure-ledger)) and a callback may cite only exposed events (L-24). This dossier splits that "exposed" into grades. It adds no per-V data (S12-S2-13: layer 2 persists no gaze, camera, menu, idle or input data).
- **Release is not exposure** ([S2 §8.5](../spec/seeding.md#85-display-and-exposure)), **the arbiter never creates content** and **class 0 deliveries are cheap but capped** (dossier 7).
- **No retention levers** (DIR-07, DRK-05, DRK-06). Perception facts are used to be seen and understood, never to hold V in the game. The one place this bites hardest is peak-end (RMB-01, RMB-03).

### 2.3 Where the advice itself fails

- **Almost no study uses generated narrative or open-world play.** The attention work is lab search and simulated driving. The memory work is word lists, faces and short stories. Curiosity work is infants and adults in puzzles. Habituation work is physiology. I found no study of how players remember the events of a game after a delay, and the nearest result (a 2024 study of 160 shooter players) used word lists. Every principle is therefore [hypothesis] for our setting even where the underlying effect is [empirical].
- **Inattentional-blindness figures are aggregates over very different conditions.** The popular "half miss it" depends on how hard the primary task is and how like the attended items the intruder looks. Fast-moving unexpected objects are noticed more than slow ones in at least one report. I use the figures to show the range (about a fifth to nine tenths missed) and not as rates.
- **Load theory is contested.** Perceptual load and working-memory load pull in opposite directions in Lavie's account (high perceptual load reduces distractor processing and awareness of unexpected items; memory load weakens filtering). A critique argues that some of this is dilution in small displays. So "busy" is not one number, and the table in §5.3 is a set of hypotheses.
- **Own-name capture is a minority effect.** Roughly a third of listeners report hearing their name in an unattended message, and it is more common in low working-memory people (§3.1). A design that relies on overheard hooks relies on a minority.
- **Spacing and encoding-specificity results come from lists and cue words.** The gap that works best depends on how long V must remember, and the best-gap fractions I found come from a 2008 follow-up read only as a summary. They are starting points.
- **The information-gap account is one of several.** Learning progress and compression progress also predict what people explore. The advice "open a gap" says when a hook fails (no anchor), not that gaps are the whole of curiosity.
- **Responsiveness research is about close relationships.** It may transfer weakly to NPCs, and the self-reference advantage is partly plain elaboration.
- **Transportation scales were built on text.** Absorption is also not unambiguously good: it lowers scrutiny.

## 4. Principles

IDs: NTC (noticing), DSP (what counts as displayed), RMB (remembering), HAB (habituation and expectation), CUR (curiosity), SLF (being noticed), SRC (sources and range of knowledge), TRP (transportation), CNF (confabulation). Grades are P1 §4's. Scale codes follow dossier 8 (gl glance, en encounter, vg vignette, gg gig, ar arc, se season). Layer codes: 1 to 4 are the four layers; C player configuration; E evaluation; X expression. "Produce" says what a procedure can do; "Check" says what it can test. No row asks the engine to learn anything about V's attention: the noticing priors are population values, set in P9.

## 5. Models the engine could run

Everything in this section is small enough to run in the arbiter's one-second loop or at display time, and every number is a starting value for P9 to replace. Values marked [J] are judgement. None of them is learned about V.

### 5.1 Four steps from the engine to V's head

| Step | Meaning | Observable? | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Released | The arbiter handed the delivery to the game port | Yes | S2 `released` |
| Presented | The port's geometric and state tests say V could have perceived it (§5.2) | Yes, by computation | New: `ExposureEntry.grade = presented` |
| Confirmed | V's own input shows the delivery was processed | Yes | S2 `displayed` for modal channels; new grade for the rest |
| Noticed | V became aware of it | **No** | Estimated only, as a population prior (§5.3) |
| Remembered | V can retrieve it later | **No** | Probed in P9 (cued recall with foils) |

The ledger entry, reduced to what layer 2 may store (S12-S2-13 forbids gaze, camera, menu, idle or input data):

```ts
type ExposureGrade = 'ambient' | 'presented' | 'confirmed';

interface ExposureEntry {
  delivery: Id;                  // or the event or fragment it carried
  grade: ExposureGrade;          // the highest grade reached
  channel: ChannelId;
  class: 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4;
  cue: { place?: Id; speaker?: Id; object?: Id };   // the features present, for re-cueing (RMB-05)
  playTotal: number;             // cumulative play seconds, as in §10.1
  shownBeforeEffect?: boolean;   // for trace deliveries (CNF-02)
}
```

The tests that decide the grade, and the raw numbers they use (distance, angle, speed, dwell), exist in the game port and in the session ledger for P9 only. Layer 2 sees the grade.

### 5.3 Noticing priors, by activity and channel

A table of hypotheses, in four tiers. The tiers stand for placeholder priors [J]: H 0.8, M 0.5, L 0.2, N 0. They sit inside the range the studies span (about a fifth to nine tenths missed) and are not estimates of any game. Reading the table:
- **The ladder of interruption is the ladder of noticing.** The channels that cost V least (class 0) are the ones noticed least while V is doing something. A designer cannot have both a free channel and a sure one. The engine buys surety with redundancy (NTC-11), with persistent channels (DSP-10) and with the `mustReach` marker (DSP-11).
- **Driving and combat are close to a dead zone for anything but sound,** and sound during driving pays the Strayer cost on the road.
- **Dialogue blocks text.** A banner during a conversation shares the verbal code with the subtitles.
- **Game settings change columns.** If the chatter setting is off, or subtitles are off, or the HUD is hidden, the relevant columns go to N. The port reads these from the game's options, as stated settings (not inferred), and the arbiter drops the rung (S13-P5-08).

### 5.5 Re-cueing and the channel tag

A callback fragment gets one extra slot and one extra field.

```ts
interface Callback {
  cites: Id;                       // an exposed event or fragment
  atLeast: ExposureGrade;          // default 'confirmed'; 'presented' only for the soft form
  recue?: { place?: Id; speaker?: Id; object?: Id; channel?: ChannelId };
}
```

- **When to re-cue.** Always, unless the cited event was confirmed in this session within 10 play-minutes [J] (RMB-05). The slot is filled from the display record's `cue`.
- **The soft form.** For an event only `presented`, the template must carry the hedge ("you may have seen…"). The lexicon of dossier 12 (S12-S2-09) still bars any statement of what the game "noticed" about V.
- **Channel naming.** Only for `confirmed` entries ("you read…", "you heard…"), and never for a rumour V did not act on (RMB-06).
- **The cost of re-cueing.** A clause. The rule against restating a log entry (SLF-02) is met by also naming the consequence.

### 5.6 Repeat counters and the predictability proxy

Both are queries over the ledger, with no state about V.

- **Run length per feature.** For each of the six skeleton features, the number of the last displays (confirmed or presented) sharing the same value as the candidate. The candidate's score is penalised by its longest run, as VAR-19's separation rules do. A change to a feature V is tracking restores the response; which features that is comes from sorting tasks (M13-10).
- **Channel run.** The same, per channel. A retry after `ambient` picks the channel with the shortest run that has a rung in the request's ladder.
- **Predictability proxy.** The hit rate of an order-2 predictor over the last 30 displayed skeletons [J]. The band 0.3 to 0.7 is a hypothesis (HAB-05).
- **Twist rule.** A beat marked `breaks: pattern` is released only if that pattern has at least 3 displays at `presented` or better (HAB-06).
- **Demotion.** The effective class of a recurring pattern drops one step per repeat inside a window [J: 3 game days], to a floor of 0 (HAB-04).

### 5.7 Curiosity records and the disclosure label

```ts
interface Hook {
  anchor: ExposurePattern[];       // facts V must already have, at 'presented' or better
  question: { form: 'who' | 'what' | 'why' | 'where' | 'how-many'; terms: Id[] };   // over exposed terms
  payoff: { strength: number };    // at least the hook's declared gap strength
}

function disclosure(beat: Beat): 'curiosity' | 'suspense' | 'surprise' {
  const e = beat.event;
  if (inRecord(e) && !exposed(e, 'confirmed')) return 'curiosity';        // a gap about the past
  if (pending(e) && exposed(cueFor(e), 'confirmed')) return 'suspense';   // a gap about the future
  return 'surprise';                                                       // a gap not felt until it closes
}
```

The label is computed at release from the ledger, shown in the fit report, and capped for surprise (CUR-05). The anchor makes a hook wait for its own ground (CUR-01); a hook whose question cannot be written over exposed terms is a texture item (CUR-02).

## 6. Walk-through: a shuttered stall, twice

A protection drive is at clock segment 2. Its sign for that segment is a shuttered stall with fresh flowers (the glance fragment of UNT-06), with a tag on a wall nearby and a vendor's bark two stalls on as the other two signs (NTC-11). The collector's call is due later in the gig, and its callback cites the stall.

**Run A: V on foot, following a tracked objective down the street.**
- The stall is in view for 3 s, 8 m away, 0.6 m wide: presented (tier M, and it matches the Claws job V is on, so NTC-02 favours it).
- V looks at the minimap as the tag passes: the tag is `ambient`.
- The bark plays at 6 m while V's screen is clear: presented, not confirmed.
- Ledger: stall presented, tag ambient, bark presented. The probability that at least one sign was presented, with all three at tier M, is 1 minus 0.5 cubed, or 88 per cent; here two of three were.
- The call arrives. Its callback cites the stall at `presented`, so it uses the soft form with a re-cue: "You've probably seen the shutters down on Fifth, the flower stall." It does not say "as you saw".
- If V then walks to the stall and stops for 2 s, the entry is `confirmed`, and later lines may say "you stopped at the flower stall".

**Run B: V drives through at speed with a call open.**
- The stall is 3 degrees across for under a second from a vehicle: `ambient`. The tag and the bark: `ambient` (driving, a call in progress; NTC-03).
- The engine retries the sign once, on a different route and kind, when V is next on foot (DSP-10), within the plant cap. The call's callback does not cite the stall (DSP-01): it names the collector's demand and the district, which are in the confirmed record.
- Had the stall been the only route to learn that the market was being squeezed, the seed would have failed its `mustReach` lint (DSP-11) and been authored with a message V can open.

## 9. Lateral links

- **Fair-play clues in detective fiction:** the reader must have been shown the clue. The grade `presented` is the engine's version of "shown", and `confirmed` is the reader marking it.
- **Signal detection:** a hit is an exposure that was noticed, a miss is one that was not, a false alarm is a credited exposure that never happened. Foils and d-prime put the same idea in the probes.
- **Alarm management** (dossier 7 PAC-06): a signal's value depends on whether it can be told from the background. Class 0 furniture and banner blindness are the same problem.
- **Magicians' misdirection** (lens L1): what the audience attends decides what exists for them. The engine wants the opposite, a pointer, and uses the same levers (gaze, onset, relevance).
- **Cache hit rates:** a presented delivery is a hit, an ambient one a miss, and the retry is a fill on another path. The redundancy arithmetic is a hit-rate calculation.
- **Spaced repetition:** the gap between signs plays the part of the interval between reviews.
- **Level of detail** (foundation): spend detail where attention is guided (NTC-07) is the perceptual twin of "materialise near V". Cross-check only (D-21).

## 10. Open questions

1. **Calibrating the priors.** What are the real noticing rates in Night City, by channel and activity? The first prepared session (§11.3) starts the answer.
2. **Visibility feed for arbitrary objects.** Can the port compute frustum, occlusion, angular size and dwell for any world object, as the bridge's `scene_report` does for subjects? If not, which traces can be graded?
3. **Audibility.** Can the port say a bark was audible, given the mix and the chatter setting, or only that it was within range?
4. **Map views and skips.** Can the port see that the map was open with a pin in view, and that a dialogue line was skipped?
5. **Game settings as aspects.** The bridge's `game_options_read` lists settings. Which of chatter, subtitles and HUD visibility does it expose, and can the arbiter use them as stated, non-inferred aspects?
6. **Accessibility.** Players with low vision, hearing loss or little time for text break the modality assumptions. The answer is stated settings, never inference, and not a separate content path; it needs dossier 18's owner.
7. **Whose reading speed.** The 4 words a second is for English-speaking adults reading non-fiction. Non-native readers, subtitle languages and fast readers differ. Is a reading-speed setting worth the clutter?
8. **A setting for threads' cues.** Should V be able to choose how long a thread keeps a visible cue (dossier 11's settings page)?
9. **Does the cue clause read as nagging?** Re-cueing every callback may feel like over-explanation to attentive players. Is the 10-minute rule the right release?
10. **Does a presented sign teach?** Evidence that a presented-only (not confirmed) trace changes later behaviour is thin. Is "presented" worth anything beyond softening a callback?

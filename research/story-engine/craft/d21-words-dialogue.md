## In brief

- **Text reads as written when its specifics are bound to the record and its voice is a small set of strong, constant markers.** Three things separate a written line from an assembled one. Its unexplained specifics each cite a fact (a registry item, a role attribute, a past event). Its speaker keeps a few strong habits of word choice and of what they pick to say. It adds only what the hearer does not already hold. The evidence is thin and mostly craft testimony (Sasko; the Caves of Qud authors; Delany as Gunn reports him), but one controlled study of generated personality (Mairesse and Walker) says what is recognisable: strong settings of a few markers, with content choice mattering as much as phrasing, and weak differences inside a single side of a scale. So a speaker's voice is a *voice card*: at most four strong markers, plus a stance on what they choose to say (SPC-01 to SPC-06, VOC-01 to VOC-06).
- **Variety is counted in frames, not in combinations.** A grammar with five slots of six fillers makes 7,776 strings and one sentence skeleton. Compton's "10,000 bowls of oatmeal" is the name for this, and the habituation work in dossier 13 says why: readers learn what follows what within a few exposures (HAB-07). The units to count are skeleton ("frame") ids, and the distinct values of the slots a reader tracks (who, where, what, how much). The selection rule stays "most specific wins, then least recently used" (Ruskin's talk as reported; S2 §7.2), but it needs a recency memory per speaker and hearer, a check that slot fillers fit one another, and a fit-report view that shows authors the extremes of a text's product space, because nobody can read all of it (GEN-04 to GEN-09, RUL-01 to RUL-05).
- **Templates are not the weak option; flat strings are.** van Deemter, Krahmer and Theune argue that syntactic templates with conditions, topics and recursive slots can do every generation task a "real" pipeline does, given the same information. The weak design is a flat string with a name pasted in, which breaks reference ("he" with no antecedent), agreement and translation (GEN-01 to GEN-03, LOC-01 to LOC-03). Whole-sentence messages with plural and select forms (ICU; Fluent) are the localisation answer, and they need entity metadata, not pre-inflected words.
- **V's options show V's actual words, and NPCs speak of V's acts, never V's heart.** The paraphrase wheel of Fallout 4 is the shipped failure: short labels that did not match the spoken line, and the studio's own lead said the dialogue "didn't work as well". The engine has a structural reason to avoid it. V's voiced lines are reused recorded lines, so the option can and should show the line's own words. An option label from a closed list of *acts* (ask, refuse, bargain, press) can sit beside it; a label naming an emotion cannot (OFR-01 to OFR-05). An NPC's belief about V's motive (D-22) gets a fixed frame: a hedge, the observed act, the belief as the NPC's view, and a reply that corrects it.
- **A told story is built from the teller's record, and its point is the teller's.** Labov's analysis of a single recorded narrative gives a form that a procedure can fill. A story is told about the most *reportable* event: rare, and with large effect on the participants' welfare. The teller must supply a causal chain back to an ordinary orientation, because rarity costs credibility. The teller reports only what they saw or were told. The *evaluation* assigns praise and blame, and is shaped by the teller's stake. That is the engine's own perspective model (D-10) applied to speech (TLD-01 to TLD-07).
- **Humour is a register with a target.** A comic beat names its script opposition and its target (Attardo and Raskin, via secondary accounts), and the target must not be the person the scene is hurting unless it is the speaker's own gallows humour. The benign-violation work supports the "wrong but harmless" condition and is itself contested. Generated humour works only where the mechanism is formalised (JAPE's puns, judged comparable to children's published riddles in an informal test); for the rest, authors write comic frames and the engine places them (HUM-01 to HUM-04).
- **The game's own text channels have measurable rhythm, and we have not measured their words.** The corpus gives line counts: a holocall scene averages about 7.5 lines and almost never has a choice; an ambient chat about 6.7 lines; a city scene about one; choice nodes offer about two options; phone choice groups about two. No source in the repository gives words per message, shard or scanner entry. Budgets in §5.4 are marked [J] until §11.1's measurement lands.
- **Almost all of this is craft testimony and small lab work.** I found no study of whether readers can tell assembled game text from written text, none of how many exposures reveal a frame in game text, and none of generated NPC speech and trust. The principles are a testing agenda (§11), and §7 gives fifteen measures P9 can compute.

## 1. Scope and why it matters here

The engine decides what happens and to whom. Most of what V actually perceives of it is words: a banner, a phone thread, a shard, a scanner line, a bark, a holocall, a line in a scene. Text is also the cheapest channel and the one that can be added late (Sasko). So the engine's quality bar 1 ("must not feel generated") will be judged mostly on sentences. This dossier asks what makes assembled text read as written, how a rule database and a grammar share a scene with authored lines, how V is addressed without being spoken for, what the game's own text channels expect, and how told stories and jokes are built.

**What is already settled and used as given.**
- **V is observed, never owned** (D-09). The engine never writes V's feelings, motives or reasons. Hooks are situations and offers. Where V speaks, V chooses the line from a span of stances. Nothing here infers what V prefers to read.
- **Attribution lines** (D-22). An NPC may voice its own belief about V's motive under four conditions (S2 §2.9). This dossier adds the wording frame (OFR-04).
- **Author-fill seams** (D-23). For classes whose source text must be human-written, the engine composes (sows, fits, casts, places, times) and people supply the words, with a non-explicit partner that leaves the same facts. This dossier adds the slot contract (MIX-03).
- **The voice plan** (S2 §2.20). Text is the default. New characters may be voiced by pack recordings or non-cloned synthetic voices. Existing V lines may be reused whole or cut and spliced. Cloning a real person without consent is barred. This dossier adds what it means for the *words* of V's options (OFR-02) and for writing lines meant to be spoken (CHN-05).
- **Everyone has their own model** (D-10). A told story is the teller's version and may be wrong.
- **Text is a rendering of the record.** Tone follows facts first, words second (TON-07). A line that states a count needs a witnessed count atom (L-58).
- **Text budgets exist per channel** (S2 §2.12: banner 12 words, toast preview 15; a pulled thread's first line carries the premise, L-69), and reading time is words divided by four per second plus half a second (D13 DSP-07).

### 2.2 What the failures have in common

1. **Combinations are not perception** (F-01, F-04, F-05). Counting strings measures the generator, not the reader. Readers meet a frame, a slot and a name; the first repeated frame ends the effect. The inflected-language case (F-04) is the same failure from the translator's side: the joined parts were never sentences.
2. **The surface promises what the system does not hold** (F-02, F-03, F-06). A label says one thing, a line another; a gospel names a cause the history lacks. Either the surface must show exactly what will happen or the claim must be marked as someone's account.
3. **The cost is authoring, and it is hidden** (F-05, F-07, F-13). A rule or grammar turns writing into condition design. Nobody can proofread a product space, and the failures appear as bugs.
4. **Volume without function reads as filler** (F-01, F-08, F-09). Shards, repeated exposition and decorative generation share a fault: they add words and no information.
5. **The best evidence is narrow** (F-10 to F-12). Controlled work exists for generated personality, puns and violation humour, each in a small domain. None addresses game dialogue.

### 2.3 Where the advice itself fails

- **"Be as informative as required, and no more"** (Grice's quantity maxim) assumes a cooperative speaker and a hearer who can recover what was left out. The engine's hearer is V, whose knowledge it can only observe (D-09). The practical check is recoverability from facts V has been shown (SUB-01), and `mustReach` information must be said, not hinted (S2 §2.8).
- **"Unexplained specifics build the world"** (Delany, as Gunn reports him) is a claim about genre-fluent readers decoding sentences. It says nothing about the *rate* of unexplained detail a street scene can bear. The art directors' own "too noisy" finding (D17 STU-11) says the rate has a ceiling. The density test is ours.
- **"Show, don't tell"** breaks for the cheapest channels, where telling is the channel (a message, a shard). The usable form is: tell what only words can carry (names, intent, voice), and never restate what the staging already shows (SUB-03).
- **Labov's six-part scheme** is drawn from interview narratives and can homogenise registers; the one narrative I read is a daughter retelling her mother's account of a death. It describes how people tell, not how well. The map's "interviews on near-death experiences" description I could not confirm from what I read (§3.3).
- **"The most specific rule wins"** is a heuristic. A rule with more conditions is not always the better fit, and vague, easily met conditions yield generic lines (Radiator, via the lens, not opened). Specificity needs a tie-break and a shadow check (RUL-03).
- **Template versus "real" NLG** is a received distinction that van Deemter and colleagues argue is false. Their paper is a position piece from 2005; the field has moved to data-driven methods since (Gatt and Krahmer's survey abstract says so), and I did not read how templates now compare.

## 4. Principles

IDs: SPC (specificity), VOC (voice), SUB (subtext and economy), RUL (rule databases and selection), GEN (templates, grammars and variety), OFR (offers and responses to V), CHN (the game's text channels), TLD (told stories), HUM (humour), MIX (generated and authored text in one scene), LOC (localisation). Grades are P1 §4's. Scale codes follow dossier 8 (gl glance, en encounter, vg vignette, gg gig, ar arc, se season). Layer codes: 1 to 4 are the four layers; C player configuration; E evaluation; X expression; S7 rendering; P8 realisation; P10 tooling. "Produce" says what a procedure can do; "Check" says what it can test. Requirement IDs (`S21-S2-nn`, `S21-S7-nn`, `S21-S4-nn`) are in §12; measure IDs (`M21-nn`) are in §7. No row asks the engine to learn anything about V: where a principle needs a number about readers, it is a population value set in P9 (D-09). Example lines are invented here and never kept as content.

### A. Specificity (EXP-02)

### B. Voice per speaker (EXP-02, EXP-09)

| ID | Principle | Grade | Source and instance | Scale · layers | Operationalise |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| VOC-01 | **A voice is what a speaker chooses to say as much as how they phrase it.** Content choice, such as whether to lead with the positive or negative point or to restate a point, moves the listener's impression of the speaker strongly | [empirical], narrow | Mairesse and Walker (text): their generator's parameters include content polarity, repetition and concessions; they report that "the polarity of the content selected" has a strong effect on perceived personality. | VOC-02 | **Few, strong markers read; fine gradations inside one side of a scale do not.** Judges recognised intended traits at the ends of a scale (extraversion best, conscientiousness not at all in these tests) and the within-side correlations were low | [empirical], narrow | Mairesse and Walker (text): correlations of judges' ratings with targets from .41 (openness) to .80 (extraversion), none useful for conscientiousness; the authors say the accuracy comes from modelling large differences between the ends. | VOC-04 | **A voice stays the same across channels and scenes.** The same person texting, barking and calling keeps the same card, and each channel has its own persona: a contact's thread is theirs, a shard is its author's, a banner is the system's | [hypothesis] | Follows from VOC-01 and VOC-02. | ID | Principle | Grade | Source and instance | Scale · layers | Operationalise |

### 5.1 How words realise the foundation

### 5.2 The text unit and its pipeline

S2 already has `Fragment.render { phrasings, reuse }`, `forms`, `textAttrs`, `ResponseTemplate` and `OfferSpec`. This dossier adds the shapes behind a `ContentRef` of kind `text`, in the terms the generation literature uses for the stages (van Deemter et al.: content determination, referring expressions, aggregation, lexicalisation, realisation). Names are provisional; S7 and S4 fix them.

```ts
interface TextUnit {
  id: TextUnitId;
  purpose: 'colour' | 'claim' | 'offer' | 'response' | 'told' | 'document' | 'scan' | 'banner';
  speaker: RoleKey | 'system';
  voice?: VoiceCardRef;                 // not allowed for 'v' (VOC-06)
  tier: 'authored-required' | 'authored-preferred' | 'assembled-ok';   // CHN-07
  origin: 'authored' | 'assisted' | 'generated';                       // S2 §2.20
  says: Phrasing[];                     // alternates; chosen by §7.2 of S2, then recency (RUL-01, RUL-04)
  means?: { aim: AimTag; recoverableFrom: FactRef[] };                 // SUB-01: at least two facts
  cites: CiteRef[];                     // facts, events, role attributes, registry items read (SPC-02)
  humour?: HumourSpec;                  // HUM-01
  spoken?: boolean;                     // written for the ear (CHN-05)
  forms: { short?: Phrasing[]; standard: Phrasing[]; full?: Phrasing[] };
}

interface Phrasing {
  id: PhrasingId;
  frame: FrameId;                       // skeleton identity, for rotation (GEN-06)
  tree: TemplateTree;                   // syntactic template with typed slots (GEN-01); messages use plural/select forms (LOC-01)
  applies?: Pred;                       // its atom count is its specificity (RUL-01)
  topics: string[];                     // for grouping lines into a coherent run
  slots: Record<SlotKey, SlotSpec>;
  tracked: SlotKey[];                   // the slots a reader tracks: who, where, what, how much
  idiom?: { gloss: string; speakerClass: ClassId };                    // a fixed chunk, GEN-03
  tone?: ToneTag;
  hedge?: HedgeTag;                     // required on attribution, half-recall and inference clauses
}

interface SlotSpec {
  type: 'name' | 'np' | 'count' | 'time' | 'place' | 'item' | 'clause';
  source: 'fact' | 'attr' | 'registry' | 'grammar' | 'invented';       // SPC-02; 'invented' needs the L-90 mark
  path?: StatePath;                     // for grammar sources: where in the phrase table to look (GEN-04)
  fits?: Tag[];                         // pairwise compatibility with the cast (GEN-07)
  seam?: true;                          // a clause-edge fill (MIX-01)
}

interface VoiceCard {
  id: VoiceCardId;
  stance: { raiseFirst?: ContentKind; omit?: ContentKind[]; repeat?: ContentKind[] };   // VOC-01
  style: StyleMarker[];                 // at most four, each strongly set (VOC-02)
  register: { byTier: Record<RelationTier, RegisterBand>; told: RegisterBand };         // VOC-05
  idiom: GroupRef[];                    // idiom sets from memberships (VOC-03)
  taboo: LexemeRef[];
  hesitation?: DelayBand;               // SUB-04
}
```

**The stages, in order.**
1. **Content determination.** The fragment and the beat fix what is said: the facts it reads, the fact it leaves, the lines already in the scene. SPC-04 and SUB-03 drop what the hearer holds or the staging shows.
2. **Choice of phrasing.** Eligibility (`applies`, `repeat`, `minGap`), then most specific, then the recency-weighted layer-2 draw (§5.3).
3. **Referring expressions.** The scene context model picks full name, short name, pronoun or description for each entity (GEN-02).
4. **Lexical choice.** Words come from the lexicon by speaker class and register. Idioms are whole units (GEN-03).
5. **Realisation.** Plural, select and agreement through message forms (LOC-01, LOC-02). Seams only at clause edges (MIX-01).
6. **Lint and log.** The stage lints run on the rendered text, and the render logs its provenance (MIX-05).

### 5.3 Selection and the arithmetic of variety

**Selection.** For a beat with alternates:
1. Keep the eligible ones.
2. Keep the most specific (S2 §7.2).
3. Among those, give each a weight that falls with its recent use: uses in this (speaker, hearer) pair, uses in this scene, and a bonus for an unseen frame. Break remaining ties by the draw key `"alt:" + beat + ":" + n`.
4. Prefer a *different frame* from the last one shown in the channel before preferring a different filler (S21-S7-05).

**Variety.** Let a text unit be meant to be met up to *n* times by one player in a window (a district hour, a gig). Let *r* be the number of times a frame can recur before it is noticed. D13 HAB-07 says readers learn what follows what within a few exposures, so *r* is small: I use 2 **[J]**. The unit then needs at least ⌈n ÷ r⌉ frames. The tracked slots multiply the *felt* variety only for slots the reader tracks.

| Case | Strings the grammar can make | Frames | Felt variety over 12 meetings |
|---|---|---|---|
| One frame, five slots of six fillers | 7,776 | 1 | One bark said 12 times with changed nouns |
| Six frames, each with the same tracked slots (who, what) | thousands | 6 | Six distinct barks, each seen twice |
| Six frames, tracked slots bound to the district's current state | thousands | 6 | Six barks that also track the world |

*The rule of thumb is a hypothesis.* It is tested by panel (M21-03) and by the repeat rate in play (M21-06), and *r* is fitted from them.

**A fit-report view.** For each text unit the fit report prints: frames, tracked axes and their sizes, the shortest and longest rendering, the rarest slot pair, every axis at its extremes, and a pairwise-covering set of renderings (GEN-09). An author proofreads that set, about a dozen strings, and not the product space.

### 5.5 Wording frames for offers and responses

*All lines are invented for this dossier.*

### 5.6 The told-story form

*A worked derivation; the story is invented.* The record holds a power failure in the Kabuki market that cost the vendor two nights of stock, caused by a district grid fault, with the vendor a witness and one neighbour told.

| Part | Source | Rendered (standard form) |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract | Teller's `stance` and the event kind | "Lost the whole block's power once, a while back." |
| Orientation | A routine of the teller (the `causes` chain's end) | "It was a warm night, so I'd left the freezer stocked for the week." |
| Complicating action | The event, in the order the teller *saw* it | "Lights went at ten. Nobody came. By morning it was soup." |
| Evaluation | A table keyed by the teller's relation and `integrate` stance | "Grid maintenance doesn't come to Kabuki, that's all." |
| Resolution | The effect on the teller | "Two nights' stock gone, and the landlord still wanted his week." |
| Coda | Returns to the present of telling | "I keep ice in the back now." |

The short form is the abstract and the coda. A second teller, the landlord's agent, tells the same event with an evaluation that blames the vendor's wiring (`polarise`), and quotes the vendor once, marked as his reconstruction. Both tellings draw from events the tellers witnessed or were told, and the half-recalled version of the second hedges ("something like ten, I heard").

### 5.7 The humour spec

```ts
interface HumourSpec {
  opposition: [ScriptTag, ScriptTag];   // e.g. 'service', 'danger'
  target: RoleKey | 'institution' | 'situation' | 'speaker';
  mechanism?: MechanismTag;             // from a closed list, e.g. 'literalism', 'bathos', 'pun'
  speakerClass?: ClassId;               // who may tell it (HUM-04)
}
```

A fragment tagged `wry` or `absurd` with a `humour` block is scheduled as humour. Without the block it is only a tone tag. The rule `target ≠ the bearer of harm in the beat` (HUM-03) is checked against the beat's effects. Joke frames are authored. The engine fills their slots and places them.

### 5.8 The author-fill contract

For a slot in an authored-source class (D-23), a pack states what the engine will compose around it and what a person must write.

```ts
interface AuthorFill {
  slot: ContentRefId;
  carries: EffectDecl[];                // the facts the text must establish (the same as its partner's)
  mayName: RoleKey[];                   // roles and their guaranteed attributes (textAttrs)
  voice: VoiceCardRef;
  length: { min: number; max: number }; // words, by form
  ceiling: { class: ClassId; intensity: 1 | 2 | 3 };
  partner: ContentRef;                  // the implied or fade form; leaves the same facts (L-100)
  author?: { name: string };            // required for authored: 'human' (L-97)
}
```

The engine sows, fits, casts, places and times the scene whether the slot is filled or not. An unfilled slot plays its partner. The preview (MIX-04) renders the person's text against sample casts so they can see how it reads with real bindings.

### 5.9 The localisation form

A phrase is a message: `"{count, plural, one {# crate} other {# crates}} went missing at {place}"`, with entity metadata (gender, number, case) passed alongside and never pre-inflected. A language pack replaces tables, not strings. A message with no entry for the language falls back to English, and a neutral form is the default for lines that address V. V's address forms come from the game's own gender variant of the line.

## 6. Walk-through: Friday at stall 7, in words

The scene is the one dossier 20 stages: the vendor's protection payment is due, the stall is shut, a tag is on the shutter. *All lines are invented.*

1. **A bark (CHN-04).** A passer-by slows at the shutter: "Closed again." The line is *colour*, one frame, with a bound specific ("again" cites the missed payment). A second frame is ready for the next passer-by (GEN-06). Nothing in the line claims a cause the record lacks.
2. **A scan line (CHN-06).** The shutter's scanner entry reads as a last-owner note: the date of the tag, the stall's registered trade. No adjective, no mention of V.
3. **A message from the vendor's cousin (CHN-01).** Thirty words in three short messages: *ask* ("You near the market?"), *terms* ("He's short on Friday's count. I can pay for someone to ask the Claws what they want."), *time* ("Before they close the street.") The reply delay is long; the cousin hesitates (SUB-04). The second message is a `means`: the cousin is asking V for help without saying so, and the facts that make this recoverable are two that V has seen: the shutter and the tag (SUB-01). V's reply options are recorded lines with act labels: [Ask] "What do they want?", [Refuse] "Not my fight.", [Bargain] "What's it worth?" Each has a `foresight`: "he'll look for someone else", "he'll tell you the terms", "he'll say a figure".
4. **A response (OFR-04).** If V cleared the tag earlier, the neighbour says: "Word is you took the tag down. I figure that was for the coin. Was it?" The hedge, the act, the belief as the neighbour's view, and three replies (agree, correct, deflect) each writing an observed telling (L-60).
5. **A told story (TLD).** The neighbour, asked what happened to the last stall owner, tells a short story: abstract, orientation (a Friday like this one), the action as the neighbour saw it, an `integrate` evaluation ("nobody could have done more"), and a coda. A second neighbour tells it with a `polarise` evaluation.
6. **A joke (HUM-01, HUM-03).** The vendor's rival jokes about the Claws' invoice style: the opposition is *service* against *extortion*, and the target is the institution. It is not placed in the vendor's moment of distress, and it is not aimed at the vendor.
7. **Provenance (MIX-05).** The log for each line shows the text unit, the phrasing and frame, the slot sources and the draw key. Asking "why this line?" is answered from it.

## 9. Lateral links

- **A rule database is the engine's `applies` and alternate choice in miniature** (S2 §7.2). The two agree on "most specific wins" and differ in that the engine's draw is deterministic per layer-2 key.
- **Qud's "decide the event, then justify the cause" is the compromise ledger's C-01 in a text form** (a comparison only). The difference is that Qud's gospels are accounts, which is what makes them safe, and the engine marks them as accounts too.
- **Labov's causal chain is the kernel's `causes` chain** read by a teller, and his orientation is D20's routine rung.
- **The no-flashback rule is the kernel's observer knowledge** (S0 §11.4).
- **Peak and end for authored words** is D13's RMB-01; **reply delay** is D7's pacing in miniature.
- **Evaluation clauses** are the teller's `perspective` made audible (D-10).
- **A joke's target** is dossier 18's representation question in a different form.
- **Whole-sentence messages** are the localisation twin of S2's rule that text names only guaranteed role attributes (L-17).

## 10. Open questions

1. **What are the game's actual lengths and rhythms?** Words per phone message, shard, scanner entry, holocall line, bark and conversation line; sentence length; contraction and slang rates; how many specifics per hundred words. This is the missing input for every [J] budget (§11.1).
2. **Which specificity order is better: atom count or rarity-weighted?** No source answers it (RUL-03).
3. **How many exposures reveal a frame in game text?** *r* = 2 is a guess (GEN-06).
4. **Can readers tell assembled from written lines in this setting?** The only test of the headline bar (M21-01).
5. **Do recorded V lines cover the stances the engine needs?** A coverage report from the index (S21-S4-05) is needed before the option rule can be promised.
6. **Is an option shown as the full line too long on the screen?** It may need scrolling or a shortened subtitle; the label list is the fallback.
7. **Is a humour setting needed?** Tone settings exist (S2 §2.18); whether jokes need their own switch is unanswered.
8. **How much can a half-recall hedge be varied before it becomes a tic?** (TLD-07)
9. **Can the scanner's description area take a generated line on an ordinary civilian?** A booked test (player channels, session row 1).
10. **What does a subtitle-only call look like without a call in progress?** Also a booked test.
11. **Does translation of speaker-class idiom survive?** Slang is the hardest part to localise (D17 MM-1).
